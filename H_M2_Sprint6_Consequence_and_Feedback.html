🎮 DESIGN FOCUS
Does falling behind make things worse, or does the game pull you back?
The Designer’s Table · Q2 Systems & Balance · feeds your systems revision
Every clue Oedipus uncovers makes the next one harder to escape. His own investigation is a feedback loop: the more he learns, the faster the remaining distance to the truth closes, until there is no room left to stop. Some games work the same way, where an early setback snowballs into a worse one. Others rubber-band, quietly helping whoever is behind catch back up. Neither approach is automatically right. This sprint asks you to look honestly at which one your game actually does, and to decide whether that is the consequence you want your design question to carry.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
Trace how an early choice in your game compounds over the course of play: does falling behind accelerate toward failure, the way Oedipus’s own investigation closes in on him, or does the system rubber-band a losing player back into contention? Revise your systems to make that consequence deliberate rather than accidental. Log a second JStor source on game studies or how rules persuade, and put an AI argument that your mechanics do not carry your theme through a real rebuttal.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
When a player in your game falls behind, does the system make their situation worse, or does it hand them a way back, and which of those two answers actually belongs to the argument your game is making?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Trace one compounding sequence. Pick one early decision or setback in your game and trace, step by step, what it does to a player’s position two or three turns later. Name whether your current design accelerates that player’s trouble (a snowballing feedback loop, the way Oedipus’s investigation closes in on itself) or corrects it (a rubber-band mechanic that helps a losing player catch up).
2
Revise your systems on purpose. Decide whether the compounding behavior you found is the one your design question actually needs. Revise at least one mechanic so the consequence in your game is a deliberate choice, not an accident of how you first built it, and write a short note explaining the change.
3
Find and log JStor source #2. Find one real scholarly source on game studies or on how rules persuade (procedural rhetoric). Log the full citation, the specific claim you are borrowing, how it shapes your feedback-loop revision, and a note confirming you located and read the source on JStor itself, not only in an AI summary of it.
4
Make AI argue against you, then rebut it. Describe your game and its theme to an AI tool, and ask it to argue directly that your mechanics do not actually carry that theme. Read its full case.
5
Respond in writing, or revise. For the AI’s strongest point, either rebut it directly in writing, explaining specifically why the critique misreads your design, or revise the mechanic it targeted and explain what you changed and why. A rebuttal that simply restates your original design without engaging the critique does not meet this step.
6
Log your AI use. Add one complete row to your AI-Use Log for the rebuttal step: what you asked, the argument it gave you, and how you rebutted or revised in response.
⚠️ AI-USE REMINDER
An AI argument that your mechanics fail your theme is a prompt for your own thinking, not a verdict. Your rebuttal has to answer the specific point it raised with reasoning about your own rules, not a general defense of your idea. If the critique turns out to be right, revising is a stronger response than arguing past it.
