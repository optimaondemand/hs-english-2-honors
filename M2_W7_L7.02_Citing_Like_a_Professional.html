📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Your brief cites two very different kinds of source: a 2,400-year-old play in translation, and a modern scholarly article. Each has its own correct MLA form.
You already know how to integrate a quotation into your own sentence. Today you learn how to credit it correctly: the in-text citation that tells a reader exactly where a quote came from, and the Works Cited entry that gives full publication information. You will need both forms for your brief, one for the play, one for your Week 5 JStor source, and one honesty rule governs both.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to cite a translated play in MLA in-text form and build its Works Cited entry.
2
I will be able to cite a scholarly journal article from JStor in MLA in-text form and build its Works Cited entry.
3
I will be able to explain why you only ever cite a source you have actually read yourself.
📚 PART 1 · CITE WHAT YOU ACTUALLY READ
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever repeated a fact you heard secondhand, only to realize later you were not sure where it originally came from? What would have made you more confident sharing it? (No wrong answer.)

A citation is a promise to your reader: I read this myself, and here is exactly where to find it. That promise only holds if it is true. Never cite a source you have not personally opened and read, even if an AI tool, a summary, or a classmate describes it accurately. If you cannot point to the actual page, article, or translation in your own hands, it does not belong in your Works Cited. This rule also settles a question students often ask about a translated play: you cite the translation you actually read, this Storr translation, not some other edition you have never seen.

📚 PART 2 · CITING A VERSE PLAY IN PROSE

This edition of the play has no page numbers or line numbers marked for you (that is why you have referred to scenes by shorthand all module: “the curse,” “the Tiresias confrontation”). MLA’s rule for a source like this is simple: when a source has no stable page or line numbers, do not invent one. Cite the author’s name alone.

IN-TEXT CITATION
Oedipus extends his curse to include himself “if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth” (Sophocles).
Cite the playwright, Sophocles, not the translator. The translator is credited fully in the Works Cited entry below, not repeated in every in-text citation.
WORKS CITED ENTRY
Sophocles. “Oedipus the King.” The Oedipus Trilogy, translated by F. Storr, Project Gutenberg, 2000, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31.
Notice the pieces: the playwright, the specific play in quotation marks (since it is one work inside the larger collected ebook), the collection title italicized, the translator named, the publisher, the year, and the URL.
📚 PART 3 · CITING YOUR JSTOR SOURCE

A scholarly journal article works differently: it does have stable page numbers, so your in-text citation names both the author and the exact page a quote or idea came from. The example below is a model, built to show the correct form; it is not a real article to look up. Use it to check the shape of your own citation, built from the actual source you logged in Quick Write 13.

IN-TEXT CITATION (MODEL)
One scholar argues that ancient audiences judged guilt by the act itself, since “pollution asks what was done, not what was known” (Bennett 220).
Author’s last name, then the page number, no comma between them, no word “page” or abbreviation.
WORKS CITED ENTRY (MODEL)
Bennett, Laura. “Guilt Without Knowledge: Pollution and Intent in Sophoclean Tragedy.” Classical Quarterly Review, vol. 42, no. 3, 2019, pp. 215–234. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/00000000.
Notice the pieces: author (last name first), article title in quotation marks, journal name italicized, volume and issue number, year, page range, then the database name italicized and the stable URL.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND YOUR CITATIONS
An AI tool can format a citation correctly and still cite a source that does not exist, or misstate the page number of a real one. Formatting and honesty are two different problems, and an AI tool can only ever help with the first. Every citation in your Works Cited must point to something you personally opened and read, whether or not you used an AI tool to help you format it.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH IN-TEXT CITATION IS CORRECT?
You are citing Tiresias’s line, “the accursed polluter of this land,” from this Gutenberg edition of the play. Which in-text citation is actually correct?
Which in-text citation is correct for this Gutenberg edition?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
“(Storr 45)” makes two errors at once: it credits the translator as though he were the play’s author, and it invents a page number this e-text edition does not have.
“(‘Oedipus the King’)” leads with the title instead of the author. MLA cites the author by name whenever the author is known, and here Sophocles is known.
“(Sophocles 45)” gets the author right but still invents a page number. This Gutenberg edition has none, so no number belongs in the citation at all.
