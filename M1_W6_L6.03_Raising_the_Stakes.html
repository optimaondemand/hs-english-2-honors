Raising the Stakes
Lesson · Week 6 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Book 12 did not tell you the cattle mattered once. It told you three separate times, each time a little louder, until the warning finally broke.
A strong turning point is rarely a surprise dropped out of nowhere. It is built, one raised cost at a time, until the reader can feel that something has to give. This lesson teaches you how a writer raises the stakes on purpose, so that when the turning point finally arrives, in your own story or in the reader’s mind, it lands as inevitable rather than random. This is direct preparation for Quick Write 6.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the three moves that raise the stakes toward a turning point: a clear cost, mounting pressure, and a choice that cannot be undone.
2
I will be able to draft a short scene of my own that builds toward an irreversible turning point.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Ten quiet minutes to draft a short scene of your own.
📚 PART 1 · THREE MOVES THAT BUILD A TURNING POINT

Raising the stakes is not the same as adding more danger. It is a sequence, and it works in a specific order. First, the writer states a clear cost: something the reader can name exactly, not a vague sense of doom. Tiresias does this precisely in Book 11, naming exactly what will happen and to whom. Second, pressure mounts on the character, usually from something ordinary and human, like hunger, exhaustion, or fear, that makes the original warning harder and harder to keep honoring. Third, a choice arrives that cannot be undone: once it is made, the story cannot go back to where it was before. Notice that the third move is what makes something a true turning point rather than just an obstacle. An obstacle can be solved and the story continues as before. A turning point changes what is possible afterward.

Study the worked example below. It was written for this lesson, not lifted from any published book, so you can take it apart freely.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, ANNOTATED
The old keeper had warned them once, clearly: never open the lower gate after the tide turned, or the whole harbor would flood by morning. Three days into the storm, with the well run dry and the children crying from thirst, Ren’s hand found the gate’s cold iron latch anyway. The latch gave before she had decided anything at all, and the sea below the gate began, very quietly, to rise.
MOVE 1: A CLEAR COST
The warning is specific and named in advance: the lower gate, the tide, the whole harbor. Nothing vague about what is at risk.
MOVE 2: MOUNTING PRESSURE
An ordinary, human pressure (thirst, crying children, days of storm) builds until the original warning gets harder and harder to keep honoring.
MOVE 3: IRREVERSIBLE CHOICE
The latch gives, almost before a decision is made, and the sea starts to rise. There is no undoing this moment. The story after it is permanently different from the story before it.

Notice what the example does not do. It never explains that this is a big, important moment. It trusts the three moves, cost, pressure, and an irreversible choice, to make that clear on their own.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Now try the same three moves in a scene of your own, ideally one that could belong to your Bard’s Hall tale. Think of a warning your character already knows, a kind of ordinary pressure that could make them break it, and the exact instant the choice becomes impossible to take back.

✍️ In your notebook, draft three to five sentences that name the cost, build the pressure, and land on the irreversible choice. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

The captain had made the rule plain: no one opened the sealed hold until they reached port, whatever noise came from inside it. By the fourth night without sleep, half the crew swore they heard someone crying in there. Osei broke the seal before the others could stop him, and whatever had been waiting was already loose in the corridor before he understood what he had done.

Compare it to your own draft. Is the original warning specific enough to name in one sentence? Does something ordinary, not a monster, actually apply the pressure? Does your last sentence make the choice impossible to undo, the way the seal breaking or the gate latch giving cannot be reversed? If any of the three moves is missing, add it before you move on.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH MOVE IS MISSING?
Each passage below is missing exactly one of the three moves: a clear cost, mounting pressure, or an irreversible choice. Read carefully, since two of the three moves are usually present and only one is genuinely absent.
1. “They had been told never to cross the frozen lake before the first snow. After three days without food, they stood at its edge, arguing about what to do.”
2. “Something had always felt wrong about the west corridor after dark. Three nights running, unable to sleep, she finally reached for the door and pushed it open.”
3. “The guide had warned them plainly: step off the marked trail and the mountain would not let you back down alive. Without a word, on the very first morning, Tomas stepped off it.”
