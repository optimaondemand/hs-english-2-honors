📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A sentence that is complete, and words that are precise: two of the smallest tools a writer has, and two of the most powerful.
Since you already know how to find a subject and a verb, this lesson moves one step further: making sure every sentence you write actually is one, and building your working vocabulary from this week’s reading. You will use both in Quick Write 1, so treat this less as review and more as the toolkit you are about to reach for.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a complete sentence from a fragment by checking for a subject, a verb, and a finished thought.
2
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT MAKES A SENTENCE COMPLETE

You already know how to find a subject and a verb: ask who or what the sentence is about, then ask what is said about it. A complete sentence needs one more thing beyond those two parts: it has to finish a thought. A group of words can have a subject and a verb and still leave you hanging, waiting for the rest. That kind of leftover piece is called a fragment, and it reads as unfinished even when it sounds dramatic.

Compare the two lines below, both describing the same moment from Book 2. The subject is underlined and the verb is boxed in each complete sentence.

✎ ON THE BOARD · COMPLETE OR FRAGMENT?
Telemachus called an assembly of the men of Ithaca.
Complete: a subject, a verb, and a finished thought. Nothing is left hanging.
Because the suitors would not leave the household in peace.
Fragment: it has a subject (the suitors) and a verb (would leave), but the word “Because” sets up an idea that never gets finished. It waits for a main clause that never arrives.
Antinous answered him in front of the whole assembly.
Complete: fix the fragment above by attaching it to a finished thought, for example: “Because the suitors would not leave the household in peace, Telemachus finally spoke against them.”

The fastest test: read the group of words alone and ask, does this stand on its own, or is it waiting for something else? If it is waiting, it is a fragment. Attach it to a complete thought, or rewrite it so it can stand alone.

⚡ TRY IT · COMPLETE OR FRAGMENT?
These are trickier than a simple length test: a fragment can look complete because it is long and busy inside, and a fragment-looking short line can actually be finished. Analyze the structure of each one before you decide.
1. “Because Penelope, who had held off the suitors for years with a trick nobody had discovered until now, finally saw her plan unravel.”
2. “Answer him now.”
3. “In the hall where the suitors had gathered for years, eating without shame.”
4. “And still the suitors did not leave.”
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 1 and 2. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

ingenious (adjective) — clever, resourceful, full of inventive solutions
The very first word used to describe Odysseus. His cleverness, not his strength, is announced as his defining trait before we even meet him.
guile (noun) — clever deception; cunning used to trick someone
Odysseus is famous for guile, and so, in her own quiet way, is Penelope: her shroud trick in Book 2 is a small masterpiece of guile used to buy time.
prudent (adjective) — careful and wise in making decisions, especially about the future
Penelope is often praised in the poem for exactly this quality: her caution and patience through years of pressure are treated as a form of wisdom, not weakness.
insolent (adjective) — disrespectful, boldly rude, showing no regard for authority
The word Antinous throws at Telemachus in Book 2, even though the suitors themselves are the ones acting without respect. Notice who actually earns the label.
lavish (adjective) — given or used in great, often excessive, amounts
Describes both sides of hospitality in this poem: a good host gives lavishly and freely, while the suitors lavish Odysseus’s own wealth on themselves without earning any of it.
suitor (noun) — a man seeking to marry a particular woman
In this poem, the word names the entire crowd of men camped in Odysseus’s hall, competing for Penelope’s hand while they wait him out.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
This week’s Quick Write is where these tools get tested for real. As you draft Quick Write 1, check that every sentence you write is complete, not a stray fragment, and work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what shows up in your own writing.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A finished thought and a precise word are both small things, and both are the difference between writing that is merely readable and writing that is controlled. Carry the fragment-check and two Word Study words with you into Quick Write 1.
