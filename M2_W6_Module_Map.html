🎯 THE QUESTION THIS WEEK SHARPENS
Now that the whole truth is known, what standard should decide Oedipus’s guilt, and how does an advocate build a case that argues for it?
You have finished the play. You know everything Oedipus and Jocasta come to know, and more. Now the advocate work begins in earnest: not reading for the first time, but rereading with a purpose, gathering the scenes you already know into evidence for a case you are about to build, piece by piece, in the shape a real formal argument actually takes.
📖 THIS WEEK’S READING — A REREAD WEEK
No new scenes. Reread targeted passages as evidence sources for your brief.
You already have everything you need. This week you go back into the play as an advocate, not a first-time reader, and pull evidence from wherever it lives. Three scenes are worth a close second look: the curse scene (Oedipus’s proclamation and the sweeping vow he lays on Laius’s murderer, including himself), the crossroads story (Jocasta’s account and Oedipus’s own account of the killing at the place where three roads meet), and the herdsman’s testimony (the unwilling witness whose words finally confirm the truth). These are not the only evidence available to you; they are simply the richest starting points.
🔗 Reread Oedipus the King at Project Gutenberg (#31)
⚖️ YOUR MODULE PROJECT — THE TRIAL OF OEDIPUS
This week you stop gathering pieces and start building the structure that holds them together. You will learn the architecture every formal brief shares (issue, verdict, arguments, counterclaim, response, conclusion), practice it on a small question before trying it on the big one, and make the single most consequential decision in your whole case: which standard of judgment you are asking the Council of Thebes to apply. Quick Write 14 asks you to draft your precise verdict-thesis and a full outline of your brief, an architect’s drawing, not the finished building.
📋 WHAT’S IN THIS WEEK
Lesson
6.01 · The Shape of a Brief
The six moves of a formal brief, modeled end to end on a small question (did Creon conspire with Tiresias?) before you apply the same structure to the Trial itself.
Lesson
6.02 · Choosing Your Standard
The deciding move of the whole trial: should the Council judge by the deed itself, or by what Oedipus intended? Your verdict follows from this choice, which is why the brief has to argue it first.
Grammar & Word Study
6.GV · Transitions and Cohesion
Transitions that actually carry logic (therefore, however, moreover, by contrast) versus transitions that only sound like they do, plus this week’s Word Study words.
Composition
Quick Write 14 · Thesis and Outline
Your precise verdict-thesis, naming its standard, plus a full outline of the brief you are about to draft. Feeder 6 of 8 toward the Trial of Oedipus.
Recitation
Practice continues
Your recitation menu (the priest’s plague-plea or the closing chorus) launched in Week 4. Keep reading your chosen passage aloud this week. It is not due until Week 8.
VR
Solo VR Activity 6.01 · Walking the Argument
Lay out your brief’s structure as physical stations, and walk it while speaking each station’s job aloud.
VR
Solo VR Activity 6.02 · The Opposing Bench
Stand where the opposing advocate will stand and deliver their strongest case against you, recorded, so you know exactly what your Response has to answer.
💪 BEFORE YOU BEGIN

Open your Module 2 Organizer and have your Evidence Bank in front of you; this week you organize what you have already gathered into an actual structure. Start with Lesson 6.01.
