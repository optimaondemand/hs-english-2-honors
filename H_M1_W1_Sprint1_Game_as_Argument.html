🎮 TODAY’S FOCUS
This year, alongside the four works we read together, you will design, build, playtest, and defend one game of your own. It will be set in the real historical world of one of our four anchor texts, and it will run all year: The Designer’s Table. This is not a side project. It carries its own line of graded work, separate from your literary writing, and it asks the same question the course keeps asking in a different form: what does a community owe its members, and what do they owe it back? Today you learn the idea that makes the whole project make sense, and you meet the four worlds you might choose to build in.

2

🎮 PART 1 · WHAT A GAME ARGUES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a game you already play often. When you win, what is it usually because of? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

Rules can persuade

A story argues with words: a narrator, a character’s choice, an ending that lands one way and not another. A game can argue with something else entirely: its rules. What a game lets you do, what it rewards, what it punishes, and what it leaves to chance is not neutral. It is a claim about what matters, built right into the system a player has to move through. The technical name for this idea is procedural rhetoric: a game persuades through its procedures, not only through its story or art. You will use this idea all year to talk about your own game.

Two examples make it concrete. Picture a simple property-trading board game where the only way to win is to out-accumulate every other player until they go bankrupt, and where a single unlucky roll can undo an hour of careful planning. Even if its box calls it a game about business, its rules argue that success means accumulation and that chance can flatten anyone’s plan, no matter how it is dressed up as strategy. Now picture a game where players must talk to each other to figure out who among them is secretly working against the group, and where winning depends on trust, suspicion, and how well you can read a room. Its rules argue that a group’s safety depends on vigilance and honest communication. Neither game says any of this out loud. Their systems say it.

Try it yourself first: in your notebook, pick the game you had in mind and answer three questions about it: what does it reward, what does it punish, and what does it leave to chance? Write a few sentences for each. When you are done, open this to see how a designer might read a different game the same way.

Worked example: a simple foot race with an obstacle partway through:

Rewards: speed and a steady pace early, since the obstacle punishes anyone who arrives exhausted.

Punishes: going all-out from the start; there is no reward for early speed alone if it leaves nothing for the obstacle.

Leaves to chance: almost nothing, which is itself a design choice. This is a game arguing that fair effort, not luck, should decide the outcome.

Compare that to your own notebook answers. Did your game leave more to chance, or less? What did that choice seem to argue?

🌍 PART 2 · THE WORLD TOUR

Your game will be set in one real historical world. You will declare your world by the end of Sprint 2 (Week 4), so take this tour with an open mind. Each snapshot describes the world, not the plot of the text we will read there, so nothing below spoils anything. The seed questions are genuinely open; you begin answering them for real once you start researching on JStor in Sprint 2.

WORLD A · BRONZE AGE / HOMERIC GREECE
Anchored by The Odyssey

A world of islands and sea routes, held together less by written law than by reputation, oaths, and the sacred bond between a guest and a host (sometimes called guest-friendship). A stranger at your door could be a beggar, a king, or a god in disguise, and how you treat them is a test you can pass or fail. In the public-domain Butler translation, a host’s welcome to an unknown visitor is simple: “Welcome, to our house, and when you have partaken of food you shall tell us what you have come for.” The guest is fed and seated before anyone even asks his business.

Design tension: trust negotiated face to face; gifts create obligations; cleverness can slide into treachery.

Seed questions: How did a traveler know a household would welcome, not harm, them? What did accepting a gift obligate you to do in return? When does cunning become dishonor?
WORLD B · CLASSICAL ATHENS, 5TH CENTURY BC
Anchored by Oedipus the King

A self-governing city of citizens who argued in the assembly by day and gathered by the thousands to watch tragedies at a civic religious festival. Prophecy and the will of the gods sat beside democratic debate. A real plague once tore through the city, and the community sometimes healed itself by fixing blame on a single figure.

Design tension: public truth, blame, fate, and collective decision colliding in a city that ran itself.

Seed questions: Why would a democracy stage tragedies as an official civic event? What did a whole community believe ritual pollution could do to it? What happens to a city’s institutions when disaster strikes?
WORLD C · ELIZABETHAN ENGLAND
Anchored by A Midsummer Night’s Dream

A tightly ranked society of court and commoner, where what you could wear, marry, and become was bound by custom and law, but where festival and theater offered a temporary, licensed turning-upside-down of all of it before everyday order returned. A world thick with fairy folklore and a brand-new commercial playhouse.

Design tension: a rigid social order that festival and disguise invert, and then restore.

Seed questions: Who decided whom a person could marry, and on what authority? What rules loosened during a festival, and why was that allowed? How did a playhouse make its money and its meaning?
WORLD D · JAZZ-AGE AMERICA, 1920s
Anchored by The Great Gatsby

A decade promising that anyone could remake themselves, lit by jazz, the automobile, advertising, and a soaring stock market, and shadowed by Prohibition, organized crime, the Great Migration, and lines of class and race that the promise of mobility did not actually erase.

Design tension: a society that sells self-reinvention and abundance while quietly guarding who gets through the door.

Seed questions: Could anyone really reinvent themselves in the 1920s, or were the doors open only to some? What did Prohibition do to who held money and power? What was being built, and on whose labor?

You may build in a world whose text we read later in the year. That is allowed on purpose: research it now, and the text will deepen your game when the class reaches it. Notice which world’s tensions and seed questions pull at you most; that pull is real data.

⚡ TRY IT · DOES THE MECHANIC CARRY THE THEME, OR JUST DECORATE IT?
Each world below already has a tension baked into its history. Every option is a mechanic someone could plausibly pitch for that world. Only one actually forces players to live out the tension through play; the rest are theme painted onto an unrelated system. Choose the one that would really carry the argument.
1. World A's tension is xenia: a stranger might be a beggar, a king, or a god, and how a household treats them is a test. Which mechanic would actually carry that tension through play?
2. World B's tension is a self-governing city that argues openly, yet sometimes heals itself by fixing blame on a single figure. Which mechanic would actually carry that tension?
3. World C's tension is a rigid social order that a licensed festival temporarily turns upside down, before everyday order returns. Which mechanic would actually carry that tension?
4. World D's tension is a decade that sells self-reinvention while quietly guarding who actually gets through the door. Which mechanic would actually carry that tension?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A game argues the way its rules let you win, lose, and gamble, whether or not it ever says so out loud. You now have a name for that idea, procedural rhetoric, and a first look at the four worlds you might build in. Neither choice is due yet. What is due is noticing: what does the game you already love actually argue, and which of these four worlds will not stop tugging at your attention?
