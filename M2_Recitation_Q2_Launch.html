📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
“A blight is on our harvest in the ear…”
You have already heard Thebes speak in one voice, through its priest, laying the city’s suffering at Oedipus’s feet. Before this module ends, you will hear the city speak in one voice again, through its chorus, at the very close of the play. Both moments are civic speech: not one citizen’s private complaint, but a whole community putting its fear, its grief, or its judgment into words. An advocate’s voice is trained the same way a bard’s memory is trained, by carrying something worth saying until it can be delivered without notes. This week you choose one of those two civic voices to carry in memory, practice it through Week 7, and record it in Week 8. The habit you build here, holding language in memory and delivering it with real weight, is the same habit your closing argument in the Trial of Oedipus will call on.

📚 PART 1 · WHY A CIVIC VOICE, AND WHY MEMORIZED

A recitation is the memorization and spoken delivery of a short passage: no notes, no script, no page to glance at, just language you have learned well enough to say aloud in your own voice. Both passages on this week’s menu are spoken by a group standing in for the whole city of Thebes, not by a single character pleading a private case. When a text speaks for everyone, an advocate learns something from carrying it: how to make one voice sound like it holds the weight of a whole community’s fear, or its grief, or its judgment.

This connects directly to your module project. The Trial of Oedipus asks you to deliver a closing argument in Week 9, standing before the Council the way an advocate stands before a bench. The recitation is your rehearsal for that same delivery, using the play’s own civic speech first, so that the discipline of memorized, paced, meaningful delivery is already familiar when the words are finally your own.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
In the Athenian theater where Sophocles’s plays were performed, the chorus spoke and sang as one body, often standing in for the ordinary citizens watching from the seats around them. Their lines were composed in verse and delivered from memory, in unison, to an audience who understood civic speech, formal, shared, and spoken aloud, as a normal part of public life, not just a feature of the stage.
📚 PART 2 · YOUR PASSAGE MENU (CHOOSE ONE)

Read both passages below aloud once before you decide. Notice which one feels natural in your mouth and voice.

📜 PASSAGE MENU
OPTION A · THE PRIEST’S PLEA
“A blight is on our harvest in the ear,
A blight upon the grazing flocks and herds,
A blight on wives in travail; and withal
Armed with his blazing torch the God of Plague
Hath swooped upon our city…”
trans. F. Storr, public domain
Why choose this one: it is the play’s opening cry, the city itself speaking through its priest, naming disaster after disaster before it ever names a cause. Short lines, heavy weight.
OPTION B · THE CLOSING CHORUS
“Look ye, countrymen and Thebans, this is Oedipus the great,
He who knew the Sphinx’s riddle and was mightiest in our state.
Who of all our townsmen gazed not on his fame with envious eyes?
Now, in what a sea of troubles sunk and overwhelmed he lies!
Therefore wait to see life’s ending ere thou count one mortal blest;
Wait till free from pain and sorrow he has gained his final rest.”
trans. F. Storr, public domain
Why choose this one: it is the play’s final words, the whole city drawing the lesson of what it has just watched. A warning delivered to everyone still listening, including you.

You do not need to decide alone right now. Say each one aloud once. Whichever passage you can already say without stumbling is probably your passage.

📚 PART 3 · HOW TO PRACTICE VERSE

You have several weeks of ungraded practice before the recording is due. Both passages are verse this quarter, not the prose you recited in Module 1, so let the line breaks do some of the work for you. Here is a routine that fits:

1
Memorize in chunks. Break your passage where the lines themselves break, mastering two or three lines at a time before linking them to the next.
2
Always say it aloud. Reading it silently will not build the same memory that speaking it out loud will.
3
Let the line break teach you the breath. A verse line often ends where a speaker would naturally pause to take a breath. Take a small pause at the end of each line, even where there is no comma, and your pacing will start to sound like the verse itself, not like flattened prose.
4
Eyes up. Each time you practice, try to look up from the page a little longer than the time before. By Week 8, you should not need it at all.
5
Let your voice carry the meaning. The priest’s plea is a plea, urgent and mounting. The closing chorus is a warning, delivered after the worst has already happened. Decide what your passage is doing, then let your pace and emphasis show it, the same skill your closing argument will ask of you in Week 9.
