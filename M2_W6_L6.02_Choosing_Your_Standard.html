Choosing Your Standard
Lesson · Week 6 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Before the Council of Thebes can reach a verdict, it has to decide what a verdict is even supposed to measure.
This is the single most consequential decision in your whole brief, and most advocates never realize they are making it. Should Oedipus be judged by what he did, full stop, or by what he meant to do? Your verdict will very likely follow directly from whichever standard you pick, which is exactly why the brief has to argue for the standard first, not assume it and rush to the interesting part.

2
I will be able to explain the case for judging Oedipus by what he intended.

📚 PART 1 · THE ANCIENT CASE: THE DEED, REGARDLESS OF INTENT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever seen a rule enforced the same way for everyone, no matter why someone broke it? Did that feel fair, or did it feel like something important was being ignored? (No wrong answer.)

To an ancient audience, Thebes is not just inconvenienced; it is dying. Creon reports Apollo’s demand in blunt, physical terms:

“King Phoebus bids us straitly extirpate a fell pollution that infests the land, and no more harbor an inveterate sore.”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Asked what the god actually requires, Oedipus gets a plain, procedural answer, not a question about motive:

OEDIPUS: “What expiation means he? What’s amiss?”
CREON: “Banishment, or the shedding blood for blood. This stain of blood makes shipwreck of our state.”
trans. F. Storr, public domain

Read those two lines as an advocate would. Nowhere does the oracle ask whether the killer meant to do it, was provoked, or acted alone against a threatening stranger. The demand is banishment or blood, because the stain itself is what is destroying Thebes. A city dying of plague cannot afford to wait for a jury to untangle a man’s state of mind; the deed alone has already made “shipwreck” of the state, and only removing the deed’s source will stop it. This is the deed-standard: the act pollutes the city, and the city must be cleansed, whatever the actor privately intended.

📚 PART 2 · THE MODERN CASE: WHAT HE MEANT TO DO

Set the deed-standard beside a different kind of evidence: everything Oedipus actually does with his life once he hears the prophecy. He does not shrug it off. He builds his whole future around avoiding it:

“So privily without their leave I went / To Delphi… To wit I should defile my mother’s bed / And raise up seed too loathsome to behold, / And slay the father from whose loins I sprang.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

A man who wanted to commit these acts would not flee the one home where the prophecy said they would happen. Oedipus flees precisely because he takes his own guilt seriously in advance and tries to make it impossible. Compare that to his own curse, months later in Thebes, where he judges by the very standard the modern case depends on: knowledge.

“And for myself, if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth, I pray the curse I laid on others fall on me.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Notice the word “privity”: knowledge, awareness, intent. Oedipus does not curse himself for any accidental contact with the guilty man; he curses himself only if he knowingly shelters him. That is Oedipus, in his own words, applying an intent-standard to himself, long before he has any reason to suspect he needs one. A modern case for judging by intent says: justice that punishes a blameless mind is not justice at all, and Oedipus’s own actions, fleeing what he feared, cursing only knowing guilt, show a man who agrees.

🏛️ WHY THE VERDICT FOLLOWS THE STANDARD
Apply the deed-standard consistently, and Thebes has its answer already: the killer of Laius must be exiled, whatever he intended, because the plague does not care. Apply the intent-standard consistently, and the case gets harder, and more interesting: Oedipus fled Corinth specifically to avoid this outcome, killed a stranger in what looked, in the moment, like self-defense against a threatening old man’s party, and never once acted with the knowledge his curse says would actually condemn him. This is exactly why the brief has to argue for its standard first. Reach for a verdict before settling the standard, and you have secretly already decided the case.
📚 PART 3 · TEST THE STANDARD ON SOMEONE ELSE

A standard is only honest if you are willing to apply it consistently, not just to the person you already suspect. Consider Jocasta. She married and had children with a man she did not know was her own son, and near the end she begs Oedipus to stop his search with words that only make sense once you know what she has already understood:

“Ah mayst thou ne’er discover who thou art!”
Jocasta, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Whatever standard you choose for Oedipus, an honest brief tests it against Jocasta too. Does it hold up the same way for her, or does it quietly bend the moment it stops being convenient?

⚡ TRY IT · APPLIED CONSISTENTLY, WHAT FOLLOWS?
Suppose the Council commits to a pure deed-standard: the act itself pollutes the city, regardless of knowledge or intent, full stop. Applied consistently to every character, not only Oedipus, what follows for Jocasta?
Applying a strict deed-standard consistently, what follows for Jocasta?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
The play never restricts pollution to kings specifically; it treats miasma as a stain on the whole royal house and city, not a rule that exempts queens.
Whether Jocasta can still be tried is a real, separate practical question, but it dodges the actual logical question this widget asks: what the deed-standard implies about her, not whether a court could still convene.
The infant’s exposure is a different act entirely from the incest the deed-standard is being tested on here; folding the two together changes the question rather than answering it.

This is why so many advocates end up preferring the intent-standard instead: it lets Jocasta’s genuine ignorance actually matter, the same way it would want Oedipus’s ignorance to matter. Neither standard is simply “correct.” Each one commits you to real consequences you have to be willing to accept, for every character it touches, not only the one on trial.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

The plague does not ask what Oedipus meant. Oedipus, cursing only “with my privity,” insists that meaning is exactly what should matter. Neither answer is free; each one has to hold for every character it touches, Jocasta included. Choose before you argue, and argue the choice itself, before you ever reach a verdict.
