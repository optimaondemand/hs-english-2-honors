How to Do Real Research on JStor
Lesson · used again in Quarter 2
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Your game has to be grounded in a real historical world, not a guess and not whatever an AI tool told you. JStor is the library you will use all year to do that grounding, starting with the first research find due this sprint. This lesson walks through getting in, searching well, telling a strong source from a weak one, reading a scholarly source efficiently, and combining more than one source into your own understanding instead of copying anyone’s words. You will use this exact process again in Quarter 2 for your Oedipus research, so it is worth learning well now.

🔒 PART 1 · GETTING INTO JSTOR

JStor is a digital library of academic journal articles, primary sources, and book chapters, most of it written by scholars for other scholars. It is a different kind of source than a general search engine turns up: everything in it has gone through some form of academic vetting, which is exactly why it is the right place to ground a game in real history instead of impressions.

🔍 PART 2 · SEARCHING EFFECTIVELY

A vague search wastes your time. Start with the specific noun tied to your world, not the broad topic: searching “guest-friendship ancient Greece” will get you closer to a usable article than searching “Greek life,” which returns far too much. Scholars also often use a different word than everyday speech does for the same idea; the World Tour’s starter research threads gave you some of these words already, such as xenia for guest-friendship or miasma for ritual pollution. Try your search both ways: the plain-language phrase and the scholarly term, since each one turns up sources the other misses. JStor also lets you filter results by discipline (history, classics, anthropology, and so on); narrowing to the discipline closest to your question cuts out a lot of noise fast.

Try it yourself first: think of your chosen world and your design question. Write down two different search phrases you could try, one in plain language and one using a more specific or scholarly term. When you are ready, open this to see a worked example.

Worked example, a student building in Jazz-Age America who wants to know how Prohibition affected who held power:

Plain-language search: “Prohibition organized crime 1920s.” Broad, returns a huge number of results, many too general to use.

Narrowed search: “Prohibition bootlegging economy urban.” Fewer, more specific results, several of them usable articles about how illegal alcohol shifted money and power in specific cities.

Compare that to your own two phrases. Which one is more likely to land on an article you could actually use?

⚖️ PART 3 · EVALUATING SOURCE RELIABILITY

Not every result JStor returns is equally trustworthy, and telling the difference is itself a skill worth practicing. A peer-reviewed journal article has been read and checked by other scholars in the same field before it was published; that review does not make it infallible, but it means someone with real expertise has already tested its evidence and reasoning. A popular source such as a general-interest magazine article covers similar ground for a broad audience, often without that same review, and may simplify or get details wrong. Four questions help you sort a source fast:

Is it peer-reviewed or popular? JStor usually labels an item as a journal article (more likely peer-reviewed) versus a magazine or newspaper piece.

What are the author’s credentials? Is the author affiliated with a university, museum, or research institution in a field connected to your question?

How old is it, and does that matter here? Some scholarship is foundational and still cited decades later (an old article can still be the right one); other questions need something closer to current research. Ask which kind of question you are asking.

Is it actually relevant to your specific question? A well-credentialed, current, peer-reviewed article on the wrong topic still will not help you. Relevance is not optional just because the other three checks pass.

📖 PART 4 · READING A SCHOLARLY SOURCE FOR WHAT YOU NEED

You do not need to read a twenty-page article start to finish to use it well. Read the abstract or opening paragraph first; it usually states the article’s main claim in a few sentences. Read the conclusion next; it often restates that claim plainly, sometimes more plainly than the introduction did. With the main claim in hand, scan the headings and topic sentences of the body paragraphs for the one or two sections that actually address your specific question, and read those closely. This is not skipping the hard part; it is reading with a purpose instead of reading everything equally.

💡 PART 5 · SYNTHESIZING, NOT COPYING

Synthesizing means taking what more than one source tells you and writing, in your own words, a sentence that states what you now understand, not a sentence that repeats either source’s phrasing. If one source tells you that a Bronze Age household’s wealth was measured partly in livestock and stored goods, and another tells you that hosting a stranger well could build a household’s reputation across a whole region, your synthesis is your own new sentence connecting the two: hospitality was not just kindness, it was a visible way to spend real wealth in exchange for something a household could not buy outright, its standing among its neighbors. That sentence belongs to you because you built the connection; neither source stated it that way.

When you are taking notes from a source, what usually tempts you to copy its exact wording instead of putting it in your own words? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)
🤖 AI AND YOUR RESEARCH

An AI tool such as Microsoft Copilot can be useful for orienting yourself in a period fast, or for suggesting search terms and possible sources to look for. It is not a source itself, and it is frequently wrong or invented on exactly the kind of specific historical detail your game needs.

⚠️ HARD RULE · VERIFY EVERY AI-SURFACED SOURCE
Every source, claim, or citation an AI tool gives you must be independently checked on JStor before it can appear in your project: the source must actually exist under the title given, and it must actually say what the AI claimed it says. If either check fails, the source and the claim are cut, no matter how useful or convincing it sounded. An unverifiable AI-surfaced source is not a shortcut; it is not research at all.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Get in with the shared login, search with both a plain phrase and a scholarly term, sort what you find by peer review, credentials, date, and relevance, read for the claim you need rather than the whole article, and write your own synthesis rather than anyone’s exact words. Use this same process for your first JStor find this sprint, and again all year, including in Quarter 2.
