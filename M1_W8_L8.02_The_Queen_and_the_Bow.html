Lesson 8.02
The Queen and the Bow
Lesson · Week 8 · Books 19–20
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The disguise nearly fails twice in one night, and the household stands one decision away from the climax.
Odysseus sits down with his own wife, still in disguise, and survives a conversation that tests him more than any suitor has. Then his old nurse very nearly exposes him by accident. By the end of Book 20, Penelope has made the decision that will bring every thread of this story to its climax. This lesson is about how Homer paces that build, delaying the moment of recognition again and again while the pressure keeps rising.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe what happens between Odysseus and Penelope, and what nearly gives him away with Eurycleia.
2
I will be able to explain how Penelope’s decision about the bow and the gathering omens build suspense toward a climax.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your own copy of The Odyssey (Fagles translation), Books 19 and 20.
▶
Your Module 1 Organizer, to track the closest calls the disguise survives this week.
📚 PART 1 · A CONVERSATION WITH PENELOPE

Penelope invites the beggar to sit with her by the fire, still not knowing who he is, and speaks to him with a frankness she has shown no one else in the house. She tells him about the suitors pressing her and the weaving trick she used to stall them for years. The stranger, still disguised, claims to be a traveler from Crete who once hosted Odysseus himself, and offers a confident prediction:

“Odysseus will return ere ever they can string the bow, handle it how they will, and send their arrows through the iron.”
Odysseus in disguise, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Notice what Homer is doing here: Odysseus tells his own wife the literal truth, wrapped inside what sounds like a stranger’s guess. The reader hears a promise. Penelope hears only comfort from a kind traveler. That gap between what is said and what it actually means is the same tool from Lesson 8.01, now used quietly, in a private conversation instead of a public insult.

📚 PART 2 · THE SCAR

Penelope asks her old nurse Eurycleia to wash the stranger’s feet, a simple act of hospitality. Odysseus has forgotten one detail: a scar above his knee, left by a boar’s tusk during a hunt in his youth. The instant Eurycleia’s hands reach it, she knows:

“As soon as Eurycleia had got the scarred limb in her hands and had well hold of it, she recognised it and dropped the foot at once.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

For one instant, the truth is inches from Penelope, who is sitting right there. Odysseus grabs Eurycleia’s throat and forces her to stay silent. This is the pacing move worth studying closely: Homer builds toward the true recognition scene by staging a near miss first. The reader’s pulse jumps exactly where the plot does not yet turn. A story that wants to build suspense toward a climax often earns it this way, with a close call that fails to break, before the real break finally comes.

⌛ HOW THE TENSION BUILDS ACROSS BOOKS 19–20
Near miss
Eurycleia’s hand finds the scar; the truth almost surfaces and is silenced.
Decision
Penelope announces the contest of the bow, setting a clock ticking toward a public event.
Omen
Thunder from Zeus and a servant’s prayer confirm, to Odysseus alone, that the moment is close.
📚 PART 3 · PENELOPE SETS THE CONTEST

Worn down by years of pressure, Penelope decides she can delay no longer. She recalls her husband’s own trick shot, firing an arrow through the sockets of twelve axes lined in a row, and turns it into a contest with a stake she names outright:

“Whichever of them can string the bow most easily, and send his arrow through all the twelve axes, him will I follow, and quit this house of my lawful husband.”
Penelope, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

This single decision sets the climax of the whole poem in motion. Only Odysseus’s own bow can be strung by his own hand, and Penelope, without fully realizing it, has just designed a contest that only her husband can win. As Book 20 closes, Zeus sends a clap of thunder from a clear sky, and a servant woman reads it as a sign that something is about to happen to the suitors. Odysseus alone understands what the omen means.

⚡ TRY IT · READING THE BUILD-UP
Each question offers four readings of a moment in Books 19–20. More than one will sound reasonable; choose the one the text best supports.
1. Still disguised, Odysseus tells Penelope that her husband will return before the suitors can string his bow, a claim that sounds like a stranger's prediction but is the literal truth. What does this moment accomplish?
2. Homer stages Eurycleia's hand finding the scar as a near miss, silenced at once, rather than the true recognition scene. What does this near miss accomplish that a direct recognition could not?

💪 BEFORE YOU GOThe near miss with Eurycleia, Penelope’s decision, and the omen from Zeus all happen within a few pages of each other, and none of them resolves anything yet. That restraint is the craft. Every scene this week raises the stakes without releasing them, which is exactly what a story needs to do on its way to a climax.
