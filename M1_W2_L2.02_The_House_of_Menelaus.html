📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A palace built on a scale Telemachus has never seen, and news that changes the whole poem.
From Pylos, Telemachus and Nestor’s son Pisistratus travel on to Sparta, where Menelaus and Helen rule in a home even richer than Nestor’s. Hospitality here is generous almost to excess, but it is bound up with grief: Menelaus cannot speak of Troy without mourning the men who never came home. By the end of Book 4, that grief opens into the biggest piece of news in the poem so far: Odysseus is alive.

📚 PART 1 · A PALACE LIKE THE SUN

Nestor’s hall already impressed Telemachus. Menelaus’s palace at Sparta leaves him speechless. The poem describes his and Pisistratus’s reaction directly:

“Telemachus and Pisistratus were astonished when they saw it, for its splendour was as that of the sun and moon.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
🏛️ WHAT A GUEST WOULD SEE AT MENELAUS’S COURT
The hall itself
Bronze, gold, amber, ivory, and silver on every surface, bright enough to be compared to the sun and moon.
The welcome
Guests are bathed, dressed, fed, and seated among the household before a single question is asked of them.
The mood beneath it
All this wealth sits beside real sorrow. Menelaus and Helen are still mourning the war that won them this life.

Just as at Pylos, the guests are welcomed before they are questioned, and Menelaus says so outright: “Fall to, and welcome; when you have done supper I shall ask who you are, for the lineage of such men as you cannot have been lost.” Hospitality this generous, offered to strangers whose names are not even known yet, is the same rule Nestor kept, now shown at the wealthiest court in the poem.

📚 PART 2 · GRIEF THAT WEALTH CANNOT COVER

Once Menelaus learns that his young guest is Odysseus’s son, the mood in the hall changes. He speaks of Odysseus with open grief, admitting he still cannot bear to think of him:

“I cannot even think of him without loathing both food and sleep, so miserable does he make me, for no one of all the Achaeans worked so hard or risked so much as he did.”
Menelaus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

This is worth noticing carefully: the richest, most secure household in the poem is not a happy one. Menelaus has everything wealth can buy and still mourns daily for a friend he has not seen in twenty years. The poem refuses to let hospitality and comfort stand in for peace of mind. A great house can still be a grieving one.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
An audience listening to this poem would have known the Trojan War as a shared national memory, not distant history. Every household in this world lost someone at Troy or waited years for a homecoming. Menelaus’s grief in his own gleaming hall would have felt familiar rather than strange: victory did not undo what the war cost.
📚 PART 3 · WHAT PROTEUS TOLD MENELAUS

Menelaus then tells a story from his own long trip home after the war: stranded in Egypt, he was forced to wrestle and question Proteus, an old shape-shifting sea god who can see the fates of men everywhere. Proteus told him plainly what has become of Odysseus:

“The third man is Odysseus who dwells in Ithaca. I can see him in an island sorrowing bitterly in the house of the nymph Calypso, who is keeping him prisoner, and he cannot reach his home for he has no ships nor sailors to take him over the sea.”
Proteus, as reported by Menelaus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

This is the first hard confirmation in the whole poem that Odysseus is alive. Notice what it also confirms: he is not lost by choice or by weakness. He is held against his will, without the means to leave, which is its own kind of captivity even inside a comfortable place. Telemachus leaves Sparta with something Ithaca could not give him: real news, not just hope.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND HISTORICAL DETAIL
An AI tool can quickly summarize who Proteus is or list the wealth described at Menelaus’s court. It is less reliable at getting the smaller mythological details exactly right, since these figures appear differently across sources. If you ever use an AI tool to check a detail about Greek mythology, verify it against your own reading or a source your teacher trusts before repeating it as fact.
⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE NEWS MEAN?
Each moment below has more than one plausible reading. Choose the one Book 4 best supports, and be ready to say why the others overreach or miss the mark.
1. Menelaus says he cannot “even think of him [Odysseus] without loathing both food and sleep,” in the middle of the wealthiest household Telemachus has visited. What does this most suggest about how the poem treats wealth and comfort?
2. Proteus reports that Odysseus is alive but held on Calypso’s island, without ship or crew to leave. What does this detail most change about how we should picture his absence?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Menelaus’s court shows hospitality at its most generous: wealth offered freely, guests honored before questioned, grief spoken openly rather than hidden. And from that grief comes the turn the whole poem has been waiting for. Odysseus is alive. He is simply not yet free to come home.
