📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Cutting words is not the same as cutting meaning. Today you learn to tell them apart, on purpose.
A wordy sentence is not automatically a strong one, and a short sentence is not automatically weak. Conciseness is its own revision skill: finding the padding that adds nothing and removing it, while protecting every actual argument underneath. You will use this exact skill on your own Quick Write 16 draft this week, trimming it by ten percent without losing a single argument.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to cut redundant pairs, empty intensifiers, and nominalizations from formal prose.
2
I will be able to tell the difference between a cut that trims fat and a cut that removes part of the actual argument.
3
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words, all drawn from a single ode in the play.
📚 PART 1 · THREE KINDS OF PADDING

Formal writing tends to accumulate padding in three predictable places. Learn to spot all three, and cutting becomes a search-and-remove task instead of a guessing game.

Redundant pairs say the same thing twice: “each and every,” “full and complete,” “first and foremost,” “due to the fact that” (which just means “because”). Empty intensifiers claim strength without earning it: “very,” “really,” “quite,” “honestly,” “clearly,” “basically.” Cut them and reread the sentence; if nothing is lost, they were never doing real work. Nominalizations turn a strong verb into a weak noun propped up by a filler verb: “make an argument” instead of “argue,” “conduct an analysis” instead of “analyze,” “reach a conclusion” instead of “conclude.” The verb form is almost always shorter and stronger.

✎ ON THE BOARD · ONE SENTENCE, CUT TO STRENGTH IN STAGES
STAGE 0 · ORIGINAL (54 words)
“In my own personal opinion, I think that it is fairly obvious and quite clear that Oedipus, due to the fact that he made the decision to kill a man who happened to be his own father at a crossroads, should therefore ultimately be considered guilty of committing a serious and very grave crime against the city of Thebes and its innocent people.”
STAGE 1 · CUT REDUNDANT PAIRS & WORDY PHRASES (38 words)
“In my own personal opinion, I think that it is fairly obvious and quite clear that Oedipus, because he killed a man who happened to be his own father at a crossroads, should be considered guilty of committing a grave crime against the city of Thebes and its innocent people.”
STAGE 2 · CUT EMPTY INTENSIFIERS & HEDGES (26 words)
“Oedipus, because he killed a man who happened to be his father at a crossroads, should be considered guilty of committing a grave crime against Thebes.”
STAGE 3 · CUT THE NOMINALIZATION, FINAL (14 words)
“Oedipus is guilty of a grave crime against Thebes: he killed his own father at a crossroads.”
Fifty-four words down to fourteen, and the claim, Oedipus killed his father and is guilty of a grave crime against Thebes, has not changed at all. Notice the colon doing the same job it learned to do back in Week 1: setting up the specific evidence.

A quick test for any cut: read the sentence before and after side by side. If a reader who saw only the “after” version could still state your exact claim, your evidence, and your reasoning, you cut fat. If any of those three is now missing or vaguer, you cut into the argument itself, and that is a different problem than wordiness.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH CUT LOSES MEANING?
Here is a bloated sentence from a draft brief: “In my personal opinion, I honestly think that Creon clearly had no real motive to conspire with Tiresias, because he says himself that being king would actually take away his freedom rather than give him more power, which is a very important point that basically proves he is innocent.” Below are four possible cuts. Three trim only fat. One actually removes part of the argument. Which one?
Which cut actually loses part of the argument, rather than only trimming fat?
WHY THE OTHER THREE ARE ONLY FAT
The first cuts every filler word and hedge but keeps the claim, the reason, and the evidence fully intact.
The third only reorders the same claim and reason; nothing is missing, just resequenced.
The fourth still carries a little padding (“in my opinion,” “clearly”) but keeps the full reasoning, so it is an incomplete cut, not a harmful one.
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

All six words below come from a single choral ode you have already read, the chorus’s warning against insolence after the Tiresias confrontation. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

insolence (noun) — proud, contemptuous disregard for others or for what is rightfully owed to them
The chorus names this, not ambition alone, as the actual root of the danger they fear: “of insolence is bred the tyrant.”
tyrant (noun) — a ruler whose power is seized or held through excess and pride rather than earned through right
The chorus warns that a tyrant, once he “scales the precipitous height and grasps the throne,” inevitably “topples o’er and lies in ruin prone.” The play never names Oedipus this word directly; it leaves you to weigh whether it fits.
reverence (verb) — to show proper honor and respect toward the gods, the dead, or rightful authority
The chorus condemns anyone who will “not Justice heed, nor reverence the shrine of images divine.”
profane (adjective) — showing disrespect for what should be held sacred
The chorus pictures a “proud sinner” who, “urged by greed profane,” lays “an impious hand on holiest things.”
blest (adjective, an older form of “blessed”) — favored by fortune, free from suffering
The closing Chorus, looking at Oedipus’s fall, warns Thebans to “wait to see life’s ending ere thou count one mortal blest.” Good fortune early in a life proves nothing about how it ends.
unpurged (adjective) — not cleansed of a defiling guilt or crime
Oedipus himself warns that it would be “a scandal” to leave Laius’s murder “unpurged,” unresolved and unanswered for, a line from his own curse speech in Week 1.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
Before you write Quick Write 16, take your revised argument section from Lesson 8.01 and cut it by at least ten percent by word count, using the three moves from today: redundant pairs, empty intensifiers, and nominalizations. Every argument, every piece of cited evidence, and every reasoning sentence must still be present after the cut; if one disappears, you cut too far. Work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately, somewhere in your full draft. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what is missing, and what remains, in your own writing.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Fifty-four words or fourteen: the claim, the evidence, and the reasoning survived every stage of that cut, because none of them were ever the padding. Carry that same test, and two Word Study words, into your full draft.
