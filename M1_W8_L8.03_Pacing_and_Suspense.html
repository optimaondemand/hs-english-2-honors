Lesson 8.03
Pacing and Suspense
Lesson · Week 8 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
This week’s reading held back the recognition scene again and again. Today you learn how a writer controls that delay on purpose.
Pacing is the speed at which a story reveals what happens next, and suspense is what a reader feels while waiting for it. Neither is an accident. A writer decides, moment by moment, whether to slow down and linger on a small detail or speed up and rush past it, and that decision is what makes a reader’s pulse rise before a climax. This is the last craft lesson before you write the full draft of The Bard’s Hall, so treat it as the tool you most need right now.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify how sentence length and withheld information control the pace of a scene.
2
I will be able to revise a moment of my own draft to slow down or speed up its pacing on purpose.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Your draft notes or scene ideas for The Bard’s Hall so far, since you will revise a moment of your own writing.
📚 PART 1 · TWO WAYS TO CONTROL SPEED

A writer has two simple levers for controlling pace. The first is sentence length: short sentences read fast and feel urgent, while longer sentences slow the reader down and let a moment stretch out. The second is information: a writer who withholds a key fact, letting the reader wait for it, builds suspense, while a writer who reveals everything immediately removes the tension. Neither trick works alone. The strongest suspense usually comes from combining both, slowing the sentence down while still holding back the one detail the reader most wants.

Study the worked example below. It was written for this lesson, not lifted from any published book, so you can take it apart freely.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, ANNOTATED
The key would not turn. Not at first. Rhea worked it slowly, feeling for the notch her father had shown her years ago, the one only their family knew, while the footsteps in the hallway outside grew louder, paused, and then, for a long moment that seemed to stretch far past its actual length, stopped directly outside the door. Then the lock gave.
SHORT, THEN STRETCHED, THEN SHORT
The passage opens with two short sentences (fast, urgent), stretches into one long sentence loaded with detail (slowing the moment down at the worst possible time), then snaps back to a short sentence for the release.
WITHHELD DETAIL
We never learn who is in the hallway, or why Rhea is hiding, until whatever comes next. The pacing move only works because a real question is left open.

Compare this to what this week’s reading actually does with the scar scene. Homer does not rush past Eurycleia’s hand finding the scar. He lingers on it, briefly, the way the worked example lingers on the footsteps, before snapping to the short, sharp action of Odysseus silencing her. The reading and the worked example use the identical move.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Think of the moment in your own Bard’s Hall story that is meant to build the most tension, ideally the moment closest to your climax. It might already exist in an earlier Quick Write, or it might still be an idea in your head.

✍️ In your notebook, rewrite that moment in three to five sentences, using at least one short sentence, one longer stretched-out sentence, and one piece of information you deliberately withhold. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

She did not answer right away. The room was quiet enough that Dae could hear the clock in the hallway, could count the seconds it took her to decide whether to say what she actually knew, whether the truth was worth what it would cost her to speak it. Then she looked up.

Compare it to your own draft. Did you actually vary your sentence lengths, or does everything move at the same speed? Is there a real piece of information you are holding back, or have you already given the moment away? If your draft explains too much too fast, try cutting the explanation and trusting the reader to feel the delay instead.

⚡ TRY IT · PACED FOR SUSPENSE, OR RUSHED?
Sentence length alone will not tell you the answer here. For each passage, decide whether it is paced to build suspense or rushes past the tense moment, and check what is actually withheld, not just how long the sentences are.
1. “The floor was cold. She did not move. Something in the other room had just gone quiet.”
2. “After twenty minutes of careful, deliberate searching through every drawer in the ancient desk, checking beneath the false bottom her grandmother had once shown her, Mara finally found the letter exactly where she expected it to be, read it once, and set it down without any reaction at all.”

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Pacing is a decision, not an accident: vary your sentence lengths on purpose, and hold back the one detail your reader most wants. Carry that control with you into your full draft.
