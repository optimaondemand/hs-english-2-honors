📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The last pass before you call a piece finished is a different kind of reading than any of the others.
Yesterday’s lesson asked you to revise for the big things: structure, voice, pacing. Proofreading is smaller and just as important. It is the pass where you hunt down the specific words that get swapped for each other so often that a writer can miss them even after several careful readings. This lesson teaches you what to look for, and this week’s Word Study words close out the vocabulary you have been building since Book 1.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to correctly choose between commonly confused word pairs, including its/it’s, their/there/they’re, then/than, and effect/affect.
2
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words, drawn from Books 21 through 24.
📚 PART 1 · THE WORDS THAT SLIP PAST YOU

A proofreading pass is not about reading for meaning; you already know what your draft is trying to say, and that is exactly the problem. Because you know what you meant, your eyes tend to skip right past small errors and fill in the correct word automatically. The fix is to slow down and check specific trouble spots one at a time, rather than trying to catch everything at once. Four pairs cause more trouble than almost anything else in student writing.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CAUGHT AND FIXED
The bow had finally done it’s work in Odysseus’s hands.
The bow had finally done its work in Odysseus’s hands.
its/it’s: “it’s” only ever means “it is” or “it has.” This sentence needs the possessive, so it takes “its,” with no apostrophe, exactly like “his” or “her.”
Their gathered outside the hall, waiting for they’re chance at revenge.
They’re gathered outside the hall, waiting for their chance at revenge.
their/there/they’re: “they’re” is the contraction for “they are.” “Their” shows possession. “There” points to a place. Say the full words in your head to check which one you need.
Odysseus was more cautious then reckless when he revealed himself.
Odysseus was more cautious than reckless when he revealed himself.
then/than: “than” compares two things. “Then” shows time or sequence, as in “he strung the bow, then he stood.”
Penelope’s test had a powerful affect on Odysseus, and it effected his patience.
Penelope’s test had a powerful effect on Odysseus, and it affected his patience.
effect/affect: “affect” is almost always the verb, the action of influencing something. “Effect” is almost always the noun, the result itself.

Proofread for one pair at a time. Search your draft for every “its” and “it’s” first, decide each one on its own, then move to the next pair. Checking for all four types of confusion in a single read-through is exactly how they slip past.

⚡ TRY IT · CHOOSE THE RIGHT WORD

1. The suitors’ kinsmen marched toward ___ fallen sons’ house.

3. The covenant had a lasting ___ on the peace between Odysseus and the island.
4. Odysseus named the suitors’ crimes aloud, ___ the household began its reckoning.
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 21 through 24, the final stretch of the poem. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

contending (adjective/verb) — competing or striving against others for something
Penelope uses this word for the suitors competing for her hand in Book 21, and the poem uses it again in Book 24 for the two sides ready to fight over the suitors’ deaths. The same word marks both a marriage contest and a blood feud.
substance (noun, older sense) — a person’s wealth, property, or livelihood
Odysseus names this word first in his charge against the suitors in Book 22, the wealth of his household they wasted while he was gone. It is one of the concrete losses the poem lists before any violence begins.
covenant (noun) — a solemn, binding agreement between two parties
The word the poem uses in Book 24 for the peace made between Odysseus and the people of Ithaca. It is stronger than a simple truce; it is a formal agreement meant to hold.
convincing (adjective) — able to persuade through solid evidence
Used in Book 24 to describe the proofs Odysseus gives Laertes about the orchard, the exact detail that finally convinces his father who he is. The word marks evidence strong enough to overcome real doubt.
marvellous (adjective) — causing wonder, extraordinary
Odysseus’s own word in Book 23 for the bed he built around the living olive tree, a thing that causes wonder because of how it was made, not because of anything magical about it.
wooed (verb) — courted, sought to marry
Part of Odysseus’s charge against the suitors in Book 22: they wooed his wife while he was still living. The word makes clear the suitors were not simply guests overstaying their welcome; they were actively pursuing a married woman.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
Give your finished Bard’s Hall draft one last proofreading pass before you submit it. Check every its/it’s, their/there/they’re, then/than, and effect/affect one pair at a time. Then look back across your whole module: work in at least three Word Study words from across the weeks, used accurately, somewhere in your final draft. That is how grammar and vocabulary get assessed this module: not by a quiz, but by what shows up, correctly, in the writing you actually submit.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A careful proofreading pass is the last, smallest form of respect a writer pays a reader: making sure nothing distracts from the story itself. Give your final Bard’s Hall draft that pass, one word pair at a time, before you call it finished.
