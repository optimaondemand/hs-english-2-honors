📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A city is dying by inches, and the man it trusts most just made a promise he cannot possibly keep safely.
Today you read the opening scene itself: the priest’s desperate plea, Creon’s report from the oracle at Delphi, and Oedipus’s own proclamation and curse. Warm-up first, in your notebook: before reading, jot one sentence answering this: if you were responsible for a whole community and something was making everyone sick, what is the very first thing you would owe the people you led? Keep that answer in mind as you watch what Oedipus actually does.

📚 PART 1 · THE PRIEST’S PLEA

The play opens with a striking image: a crowd of suppliants, led by an old priest, sitting on the steps of the palace, holding olive branches wrapped in wool, a traditional sign of a plea to the gods or a powerful protector. Oedipus comes out to them directly, asking why they have gathered:

“Why sit ye here as suppliants, in your hands branches of olive filleted with wool?”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

The priest answers with a description of the plague that is worth reading slowly, line by line. Notice how it moves from the fields, to the herds, to childbirth itself, growing more intimate and more devastating with each line:

“A blight is on our harvest in the ear, a blight upon the grazing flocks and herds, a blight on wives in travail; and withal armed with his blazing torch the God of Plague hath swooped upon our city emptying the house of Cadmus, and the murky realm of Pluto is full fed with groans and tears.”
The Priest, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Read that progression again: crops fail, then livestock die, then women die in childbirth. The plague is not an abstract inconvenience. It is destroying Thebes at every level a community depends on to survive at all, and the dead are piling up faster than the city can mourn them. This is the community Oedipus steps out to address, and it is already, visibly, on its knees.

📚 PART 2 · CREON’S NEWS FROM DELPHI

Oedipus has already anticipated the crisis. Before the priest even finishes speaking, he reveals that he has sent his brother-in-law, Creon, to consult the oracle at Delphi, the great shrine of the god Apollo, to ask how Thebes can be saved. Creon arrives with the god’s answer, and it is specific and demanding:

“King Phoebus bids us straitly extirpate a fell pollution that infests the land, and no more harbor an inveterate sore.”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Pressed for detail, Creon explains what Apollo actually requires: the killer of Laius, the king who ruled Thebes before Oedipus, has never been found, and his unpunished murder has left a kind of stain on the whole city. Creon reports the god’s command plainly:

“He fell; and now the god’s command is plain: punish his takers-off, whoe’er they be.”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain

So the terms are set before Oedipus says a single word in response: find Laius’s murderer, and drive him out or put him to death, and the plague will lift. It sounds, on its face, like an ordinary problem to solve. Read on, and you will see why it is anything but.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
To an ancient Greek audience, an unpunished murder was not just a private crime; it could bring miasma, a kind of religious pollution, down on an entire city, exactly as Creon describes here. That belief is why the whole community, not just Laius’s family, suffers until the killer is found. The play assumes its audience already understood this connection between hidden guilt and collective disaster.
📚 PART 3 · THE PROCLAMATION AND THE CURSE

Oedipus responds the way a good king should: immediately, publicly, and without hesitation. He issues a formal proclamation to the entire city, demanding that anyone with knowledge of the murder come forward:

“This proclamation I address to all: Thebans, if any knows the man by whom Laius, son of Labdacus, was slain…”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Then he goes further than the oracle strictly required. He pronounces a solemn, sweeping curse on the murderer, whoever that person turns out to be, banning him from every comfort a citizen of Thebes could expect:

“And on the murderer this curse I lay (on him and all the partners in his guilt): wretch, may he pine in utter wretchedness! And for myself, if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth, I pray the curse I laid on others fall on me.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Read that last line again. Oedipus is so confident of his own innocence that he extends the curse to cover himself, in case he unknowingly harbors the killer. He also forbids any citizen from sheltering or even speaking to the murderer once found:

“Let no man in this land, whereof I hold the sovereign rule, harbor or speak to him; give him no part in prayer or sacrifice or lustral rites, but hound him from your homes.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

This is where the play’s central engine starts turning: dramatic irony, meaning the audience knows something the characters onstage do not. You already know, or will soon suspect, what Oedipus cannot: that the man he is cursing so thoroughly, and banishing from every home in Thebes, might be far closer to the throne than anyone in the scene imagines. Every word of that curse will mean something different the second time you read it, once you know how the story ends. That gap, between what Oedipus believes he is doing and what he is actually doing, is the terrible irony this whole module will keep asking you to weigh.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE OPENING ESTABLISH?
More than one option below will sound reasonable. Choose the reading the priest’s plea, Creon’s news, and Oedipus’s proclamation best support, and be ready to say why the others fall short.
1. The priest describes the blight moving from crops, to herds, to women dying in childbirth, rather than simply saying “there is a plague.” What is the strongest reading of that progression?
2. Apollo’s oracle, delivered through Creon, could have simply told Thebes to make a sacrifice or perform a ritual. What does the oracle actually require instead?
3. Oedipus curses Laius’s murderer so thoroughly that he even extends the curse to cover himself, in case he unknowingly shelters the guilty man. Why is this moment an example of dramatic irony rather than simply confidence?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A city is dying, its king has already sent for the god’s answer, and he responds by publicly vowing to find the killer, whoever he may be, and cursing him without mercy. Everything about Oedipus’s response in this scene looks like exactly what a good king owes his suffering community. Keep that thought exactly as it is.
