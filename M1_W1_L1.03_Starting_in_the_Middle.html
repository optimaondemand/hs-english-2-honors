Lesson 1.03
Starting in the Middle
Lesson · Week 1 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
You have seen how the Odyssey opens in the middle of the action. Today you learn to do it yourself.
Reading a technique and using it are two different skills. This lesson is about the second one. You will study a short opening written specifically to demonstrate the move, take it apart to see how it works, and then try your own before you go any further. This is direct preparation for Quick Write 1 and for The Bard’s Hall, the story you will build across this whole module.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify what a strong in medias res opening withholds and what it reveals.
2
I will be able to draft my own opening that begins inside a moment of action, without front-loaded explanation.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Ten quiet minutes, since you will draft two short passages of your own during this lesson.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT A STRONG MIDDLE-START DOES

A writer who begins in the middle of an action is making a bet: that the reader will follow a scene they do not yet fully understand, because something is happening right now that demands attention. The bet pays off when two things are true. First, the opening line is already in motion: someone is doing something, not being introduced. Second, the writer withholds the explanation on purpose. You do not learn why the character is running, or who they are running from, until later. The gap between what you see and what you do not yet know is exactly what pulls a reader forward.

Study the worked example below. It was written for this lesson, not lifted from any published book, so you can take it apart freely without worrying about spoiling anything.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, ANNOTATED
The rope burned through Marisol’s palms before she let it go. Below her, the tide-runner she had spent a whole winter building spun once on the current and drifted free. Her father would not forgive this, not today, not with the harbor watching.
ALREADY IN MOTION
The first verb is something happening to her right now: the rope is burning, she is letting go. There is no “Marisol was a girl who loved boats.” We meet her mid-action.
WITHHELD, NOT EXPLAINED
We do not know what the tide-runner is, why her father matters, or what “the harbor watching” means. Those questions are the hook. The backstory exists; it is simply not handed over yet.

Notice what the example does not do. It never writes a sentence like “Marisol had built a boat over the winter, and she was about to lose it, and her father would be furious.” That version explains everything up front and asks nothing of the reader. The version above trusts you to catch up.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Now try the same move. Think of a moment of tension or motion: an argument mid-sentence, a chase, a decision made at the last second, a door closing on something important. It does not need to relate to the Odyssey. Pick anything you can picture clearly.

✍️ In your notebook, write two to three sentences that begin inside that moment of action, withholding the explanation. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

The second boy’s knuckles were already white on the doorframe when Dae stepped past him into the hallway. Nobody had said his name yet. That was the part that scared him.

Compare it to your own draft. Does your first sentence already have something happening in it? Is there a detail you are deliberately not explaining yet? If your opening spends its first sentence introducing a person or a setting instead of an action, try cutting straight to the moment and see what changes.

⚡ TRY IT · MIDDLE-START OR FRONT-LOADED?
Each opening below is written to be trickier than it first looks: a vivid verb does not always mean the sentence begins in medias res, and a long sentence does not always mean it is front-loaded. Decide what each one is actually doing.
1. “The rain had been falling on the harbor for three days when Mira finally left the house.”
2. “Before Mira could reach the door, the note her brother had hidden years ago fell out of the drawer she’d yanked open.”
3. “Mira always slammed doors when she was angry, and this morning was no exception.”
➡️ WHAT YOU’LL DO NEXT
1
Quick Write 1 (Canvas assignment).
Use the practice draft you just wrote, or start fresh, to write your full narrative opening. Full directions are on the Quick Write 1 assignment page.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A middle-start earns its power from restraint: begin inside the action, and hold back the explanation until the reader has earned it. You have now tried this twice in your notebook. Carry whichever draft felt stronger into Quick Write 1, and keep the habit of cutting your first sentence down to something that is already happening.
