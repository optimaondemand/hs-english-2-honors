Lesson 1.03
What Makes an Argument
Lesson · Week 1 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
“I think Oedipus is a good king” and “Oedipus is acting like a good king in this scene” sound almost the same. They are not the same at all.
You are about to spend the rest of this module building a case, as an advocate, for a verdict on Oedipus. That work depends on a distinction most people never learn to make cleanly: the difference between an opinion and a real argument. Today you learn that difference, and you take on the stance you will hold for the whole Trial of Oedipus project: an advocate who must defend a position, not just feel one.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell the difference between an opinion and a claim.
2
I will be able to explain why a claim needs reasons and evidence, and an opinion does not.
3
I will be able to describe the advocate’s stance: arguing a position you must defend with evidence, whether or not it is the position you feel most strongly.
📚 PART 1 · OPINION VS. CLAIM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever had to convince someone of something you were not fully certain of yourself? What made your case land, or fall flat? (No wrong answer.)

Two different kinds of statement

An opinion is a statement of personal preference or feeling. “I like Oedipus” or “I find this play boring” are opinions: they describe your reaction, and no amount of evidence can prove them right or wrong, because they are not making a claim about the world outside your own taste. A claim is different. It is an assertion that could, in principle, be challenged, supported, or disproven, because it is making a statement about the text, the world, or an idea that other people can test against evidence. A claim without evidence is just an opinion wearing a claim’s clothing. What actually makes a claim into an argument is what you attach to it: reasons (the logic connecting your claim to your evidence) and evidence (the specific facts, quotations, or details that support it).

This distinction is not just an academic technicality. It is the entire foundation of the Trial of Oedipus project. A jury, or a council, does not care what you personally feel about Oedipus. They care whether you can support a verdict with reasons and evidence strong enough to persuade someone who does not already agree with you.

📚 PART 2 · THE SAME SENTENCE, ARGUED TWO WAYS

Watch the same basic idea handled first as an opinion, and then rebuilt as a real claim with evidence.

📝 SAME IDEA, ARGUED TWO WAYS
AS AN OPINION
“Oedipus is a good king in the opening scene because I like how he handles it.”
This cannot be argued further. “I like it” is a fact about the reader, not about the text. There is nowhere for a reader to push back, and nowhere to add evidence.
AS A CLAIM WITH EVIDENCE
“Oedipus acts as a good king in the opening scene because he responds to his people’s suffering immediately and personally, rather than delaying or delegating the problem.”
Evidence: he has already sent Creon to Delphi before the priest finishes speaking, and he addresses the suppliants himself instead of sending an attendant. A reader could test this claim against the text and agree or disagree with real reasons.

Notice what changed. The claim version names a specific, checkable behavior (acting immediately and personally) and backs it with specific evidence from the text (sending Creon before the priest finishes, addressing the suppliants himself). Someone who disagreed could point to a different piece of evidence and argue the opposite claim. That disagreement is exactly what an argument is for.

✍️ TRY IT YOURSELF, IN YOUR NOTEBOOK, BEFORE YOU LOOK AT THE MODEL BELOW
Write one sentence stating an opinion about Oedipus’s curse (how it makes you feel), and then rewrite it as a claim with at least one piece of evidence from the passages in Lesson 1.02. Do this in your notebook first.
Compare your work: a model version (open only after you have written your own)
Opinion: “Oedipus’s curse feels dramatic and a little frightening to me.”
Claim with evidence: “Oedipus’s curse shows a king willing to hold himself to the same standard he demands of his citizens, because he extends the curse to cover himself ‘if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth.’ That is a stronger, more testable claim than a simple reaction, since a reader could check it directly against the wording of the curse.”
📚 PART 3 · THE ADVOCATE’S STANCE

For the rest of this module, you are going to think and write like an advocate. An advocate is someone who takes a position and must defend it with reasons and evidence, whether or not it happens to be the position they feel most strongly about at the start. This is a different mindset than simply sharing your first reaction. An advocate has to imagine a skeptical reader, someone who is not automatically on their side, and build a case sturdy enough to hold up under that reader’s questions.

This is also why your first verdict, in this week’s Quick Write, does not need to be your final one. You are allowed, and expected, to change your mind as more evidence comes in over the next several weeks. What matters right now is that whatever position you take, you support it like an advocate: with a claim, a reason, and a piece of real evidence, not just a feeling.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND ARGUMENT
An AI tool can generate a list of reasons someone might argue for or against Oedipus in seconds. What it cannot do is verify, on its own, whether those reasons actually match the specific evidence in this translation, or whether the reasoning holds up under a real reader’s pushback. If you ever use an AI tool while drafting, treat its output as a starting list to check against the text yourself, not as a finished argument.
⚡ TRY IT · OPINION OR CLAIM?
Decide whether each statement is an opinion or a claim that could be argued with evidence. Do not let the wording alone decide it for you; check whether a reader could actually test the statement against the text.
1. “Oedipus’s curse is more far-reaching than the oracle strictly required, which suggests he wants to appear thorough before his people.”
2. “I think Oedipus’s decision to extend the curse to himself proves he is being honest with the audience.”
3. “I find the priest’s description of the plague upsetting to read.”

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

An opinion tells the reader something about you. A claim, backed by reasons and evidence, tells the reader something about the text, and invites them to check your work. That is the stance you are taking on for the rest of this module: not a fan or a critic, but an advocate who has to make a case stand up.
