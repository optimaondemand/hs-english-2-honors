📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A beggar picks up a king’s bow, and a house that has waited twenty years finally finds out who has been sitting in it.
Everything this poem has been building toward happens in Books 21 and 22: Penelope sets a contest only her true husband could win, and the stranger everyone has overlooked turns out to be Odysseus himself. What follows is violent and final. This lesson does not look away from that violence, but it also does not treat it as spectacle. Instead, it asks the harder question underneath the action: when Odysseus takes his hall back by force, is he delivering justice, or is he simply getting even? The poem gives you the material to argue both.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why Penelope’s contest of the bow is the only test that could prove who the stranger really is.
2
I will be able to identify how Odysseus names the suitors’ specific crimes before he acts, rather than simply attacking them.
3
I will be able to weigh the difference between justice and vengeance in the slaying of the suitors, using evidence from the text.

📚 PART 1 · A TEST ONLY ONE MAN CAN PASS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you saw someone finally held accountable for something they had been getting away with for a long time. What did that moment feel like? (No wrong answer.)

Penelope has run out of ways to stall the suitors, so she sets a contest with rules that only look fair: string the great bow that once belonged to her husband, and shoot an arrow clean through twelve axes set in a row. One by one, the suitors try and fail, their arms too weak or their nerve too shaken to even bend the wood. Then the beggar, whom nobody at the feast has bothered to really look at, asks for his turn.

“Odysseus, when he had taken it up and examined it all over, strung it as easily as a skilled bard strings a new peg of his lyre and makes the twisted gut fast at both ends. Then he took it in his right hand to prove the string, and it sang sweetly under his touch like the twittering of a swallow.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
A body remembers what it has trained to do, even after twenty years. To an ancient audience, the ease of the stringing was itself the proof: this was not a trick or a lucky guess, but a skill built into a man’s body long before he ever left for Troy. The bow does not just identify Odysseus; it answers Penelope’s test on its own terms, with no one having to take the stranger’s word for anything.
📚 PART 2 · A RECKONING, NAMED OUT LOUD

The moment Odysseus reveals himself, he does not attack in silence. He names exactly what the suitors have done, before he does anything about it.

📝 ODYSSEUS’S WORDS, AND WHAT THEY REVEAL
“Dogs, did you think that I should not come back from Troy? You have wasted my substance, have forced my women servants to lie with you, and have wooed my wife while I was still living. You have feared neither God nor man, and now you shall die.”
Odysseus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
CHARGES, NOT JUST RAGE
Before anything else, Odysseus lists specific offenses: property destroyed, servants mistreated, his own living wife pursued as if she were free to be won. He is stating a case, the way a wronged party states what was actually taken from him.
SENTENCE DECLARED
Only after the charges does he pronounce the consequence. Whether that consequence fits the crime is exactly the question worth arguing, but the poem is careful to show reasons given before violence begins.

Odysseus does not fight alone. Telemachus stands with him, along with Eumaeus and Philoetius, the two servants who stayed loyal through years of a king’s absence. That detail matters: a return like this is not just one man’s vengeance, but a household proving, together, that loyalty still meant something while the king was gone.

Here is the genuinely hard question, and the poem does not answer it for you. Naming real crimes before acting looks like justice. But the punishment, the death of every last suitor, is total and irreversible, with no trial, no chance to make restitution, no room for anyone to argue the other side. Is this what a king owes a community that failed him this badly? Or is a hall that runs this red no longer a house of justice at all, but simply a house where the strongest man got his revenge and called it a sentence?

📚 PART 3 · WEIGHING THE TWO READINGS

A reader who says this is simply justice can point to real evidence: the suitors were warned for years, they had every chance to leave, and Odysseus names actual crimes before acting. A reader who says this goes past justice into vengeance can point to different evidence: no suitor is offered a chance to surrender or pay a penalty short of death, and the household servants who merely served the suitors under duress are treated as harshly as the suitors themselves. Both readings take the text seriously. Neither one is a strawman. Decide for yourself which case is stronger, and be ready to say why in your Bard’s Hall project.

⚡ TRY IT · JUSTICE, VENGEANCE, OR BOTH?
Each question offers four readings of a detail from Books 21 and 22. More than one will sound reasonable; choose the one the text best supports, and be ready to say why the others fall short.
1. Odysseus lists the suitors’ specific offenses (wasted goods, mistreated servants, his wooed wife) before he kills anyone. What is the strongest reading of why the poem stages it this way?
2. Every suitor is killed, with no chance to surrender or offer restitution once the bow is strung. What does this detail most support?
3. Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius fight alongside Odysseus rather than Odysseus acting alone. What is the best-supported reading of this detail?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

The bow proves who Odysseus is. What he does next proves what kind of king he intends to be, and the poem leaves that second question open for you to weigh. Justice and vengeance can look identical from the outside; what separates them is whether the punishment answers the crime, or simply outlasts it.
