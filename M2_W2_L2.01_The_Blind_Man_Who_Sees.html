The Blind Man Who Sees

📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Oedipus can see everything except the truth standing in front of him. Tiresias sees nothing, and knows it already.
Last week Oedipus swore a public curse against whoever murdered the former king, Laius, not yet suspecting himself. This week he sends for the one person in Thebes said to know things no one else can see: the blind prophet Tiresias. What happens in this scene is the hinge of the whole play. Read it (or reread it) at the link on this week’s Module Map before you continue, using the scene landmark “the Tiresias confrontation,” not a line number.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to summarize the sequence of the Tiresias confrontation: the summons, the refusal, the provocation, the accusation, and the exit prophecy.
2
I will be able to explain the sight-and-blindness irony this scene establishes for the rest of the play.
3
I will be able to identify why Oedipus’s own rage, not just Tiresias’s words, drives the scene forward.

📚 PART 1 · THE SUMMONS AND THE REFUSAL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time someone clearly knew something important but did not want to say it out loud. What made them hesitate? (No wrong answer.)

A respectful request, met with reluctance

Oedipus opens the scene with real respect. He calls for the one man in Thebes who might see what the rest of the city cannot:

“Teiresias, seer who comprehendest all, lore of the wise and hidden mysteries, high things of heaven and low things of the earth, thou knowest, though thy blinded eyes see naught, what plague infects our city; and we turn to thee, O seer, our one defense and shield.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Notice that Oedipus already names the irony without meaning to: Tiresias’s “blinded eyes see naught,” yet the king turns to him as the one man who can truly see the source of the plague. Tiresias’s answer is not what Oedipus expects. He does not refuse to help out of stubbornness. He asks to leave, plainly and without ceremony:

“Let me go home; prevent me not; ’twere best that thou shouldst bear thy burden and I mine.”
Tiresias, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Read that line again. Tiresias is not withholding the truth to be cruel. He is trying to protect Oedipus from it, and himself from the fallout of saying it. A prophet who can see the future is asking, in effect, to be allowed to see nothing at all today.

📚 PART 2 · PROVOKED INTO THE TRUTH

Oedipus does not accept the refusal. He accuses Tiresias of withholding evidence out of disloyalty, even of having a hand in the murder himself. That accusation is what finally breaks Tiresias’s restraint. He turns the accusation squarely back on the king:

👁️ THE ACCUSATION, ANNOTATED FOR IRONY
“I say thou art the murderer of the man whose murderer thou pursuest.” ... “Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land.”
Tiresias, trans. F. Storr, public domain
A SEEING MAN, BLIND TO HIMSELF
Oedipus has sworn to hunt down “the murderer.” Tiresias tells him, in plain words, that the man he is hunting is the man asking the question. Oedipus has eyes and still cannot see it.
A BLIND MAN WHO ALREADY SEES
Tiresias cannot see with his eyes at all, yet he is the only person on stage who already knows the truth. From this scene forward, the play keeps asking you to notice who actually sees clearly and who only has working eyes.

Later in the same exchange, Oedipus mocks Tiresias directly for his blindness, and Tiresias answers by turning the word back on him:

“Since thou hast not spared to twit me with my blindness… with other men, but not with thee, for thou in ear, wit, eye, in everything art blind.”
Tiresias, trans. F. Storr, public domain

A blind man just told the king of Thebes that he, not Tiresias, is the one who cannot see. That is the central irony this play builds on, and it does not resolve here. It will keep growing until the play’s final scene.

📚 PART 3 · THE CONSPIRACY CHARGE AND THE EXIT PROPHECY

Oedipus cannot accept the accusation on its own terms, so he reaches for an explanation that keeps him innocent: someone must have put Tiresias up to this.

OEDIPUS: “Is this a plot of Creon, or thine own?”
TEIRESIAS: “Not Creon, thou thyself art thine own bane.”
trans. F. Storr, public domain

Notice what Oedipus does here, because it matters for the trial you will eventually argue. Faced with evidence he does not want to be true, he does not examine the evidence. He looks for someone to blame for bringing it to him. Watch for this move again; it returns next week when Oedipus turns the same suspicion on Creon directly.

Before he is led away, Tiresias delivers one last riddle, describing a nameless man who will turn out to be tangled almost impossibly close to his own family:

“Of her who bare him son and husband both, co-partner, and assassin of his sire.”
Tiresias, trans. F. Storr, public domain

In plain terms: the man Oedipus is hunting will turn out to be son and husband to the same woman, and killer of his own father. Tiresias does not spell out that this man is Oedipus himself. He does not have to. The riddle is left standing in the air as he exits, and the chorus is left to make of it what they can.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND DRAMATIC IRONY
An AI tool can summarize this scene accurately and even define dramatic irony. What it cannot do for you is notice, on its own, that Oedipus names Tiresias’s blindness as an insult in the very same breath that Tiresias uses to expose Oedipus’s own blindness. That kind of close noticing is a reading skill, and it is the one this whole play is built to test in you.
⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE MOMENT SHOW?
For each moment in the confrontation, choose what it most reveals. You will get feedback right away.
1. Tiresias asks to go home rather than speak.
2. Oedipus accuses Tiresias of disloyalty and involvement in the murder.
3. Oedipus asks whether this is “a plot of Creon.”
4. Tiresias’s exit prophecy never says Oedipus’s name outright.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A blind man tells a seeing king that the king is the one who cannot see, and the king answers by looking for someone else to blame. That is the whole engine of this scene, and it is the engine of the rest of the play. Carry the phrase “thou the accursed polluter of this land” with you into tomorrow’s lesson, where you will look at the people who have to decide, for now, whether to believe it.
