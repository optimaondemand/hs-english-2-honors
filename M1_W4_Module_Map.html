🎯 THE QUESTION THIS MODULE ASKS
What does a community owe its king, and what does a king owe his community?
This week the Phaeacians answer that question with a feast, a court, and a song. Odysseus arrives as a nameless stranger and is honored before he is even asked his name. Then their own bard sings the story of Troy back to him, and the community discovers, without knowing it yet, exactly who is sitting among them. This is the week the poem is most openly about its own storytelling, which is why it is also the week your own Bard’s Hall tale gets its central tool: the long, careful comparison.
📖 THIS WEEK’S READING — YOUR OWN COPY (FAGLES)
Books 7–8
Scene anchors to watch for: Odysseus led into the city and the hall of Alcinous; his supplication to Queen Arete; the wonders of the palace and its gardens; the bard Demodocus singing of Troy at the feast; Odysseus weeping in secret; the athletic games among the Phaeacian youths; Demodocus’s second song, of the wooden horse.
📋 WHAT’S IN THIS WEEK
Lesson
4.01 · The Phaeacian Court
Odysseus is received by Alcinous and Arete, makes his careful appeal to the queen, and takes in a palace and garden built on a scale he has not seen anywhere else in the poem.
Lesson
4.02 · The Bard’s Song
The blind bard Demodocus sings of Troy, and Odysseus, still unnamed, weeps at hearing his own past sung back to him. The storytelling heart of the module.
Lesson
4.03 · The Long Comparison
A writing-technique lesson: how the epic simile stretches a single comparison out to make a reader feel a moment’s full weight. Builds toward Quick Write 4.
Grammar & Word Study
4.GV · Figurative Language and Parallels
Precise word choice, figurative language, and parallel structure, plus this week’s Word Study words from Books 7–8.
Composition
Quick Write 4 · One Long Comparison
Write an epic simile into a scene of your Bard’s Hall tale. This week’s feeder toward your module project.

💪 BEFORE YOU BEGIN

Open your Module 1 Organizer and keep tracking your reading and Word Study words there. This week, pay special attention to how the poem treats its own bard. Notice what his song does to the room, and what it does to Odysseus alone, and hold onto that noticing for Quick Write 4 and for the recitation ahead. Start with Lesson 4.01.
