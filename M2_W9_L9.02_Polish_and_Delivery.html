📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A brief that is nearly done is not the same as a brief that is finished. Today you close that last gap, on the page and out loud.
Your full draft is written. This lesson has two jobs. First, a concrete final-proofread pass over your brief, the kind an advocate runs before ever walking into a real courtroom. Second, preparing that same brief to be spoken aloud as your recorded closing argument, since a page that reads well silently does not always land the same way out loud. Both feed directly into the Trial of Oedipus, your final submission this week.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to apply a final proofreading checklist built specifically for a formal brief.
2
I will be able to identify what separates a closing sentence that asks the Council for something specific from one that trails off, merely restates, or grandstands.
3
I will be able to rehearse the final sentence of my own closing argument so it actually lands when spoken aloud.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your full brief draft from Quick Write 16.
▶
A quiet space where you can read your closing argument aloud to yourself.
📚 PART 1 · THE LAST PROOFREAD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think about the last piece of writing you were genuinely proud of. What was the very last thing you checked before you called it finished? (No wrong answer.)

A formal brief has its own specific failure points, different from a personal essay or a narrative. Work through this checklist against your own full draft, in order:

✍️ THE FINAL BRIEF CHECKLIST
☐
Your verdict is named in the first paragraph, not buried later. A reader, or a Council member, should know your position and your standard of judgment before your second paragraph begins.
☐
Every quote is actually analyzed, not just dropped in. Check each quotation you use: does a sentence of your own reasoning follow it, connecting it to your claim? A quote sitting alone proves nothing by itself.
☐
Your counterclaim is answered, not mocked. Reread your Counterclaim section as if you agreed with it. Does your brief treat it as a real position deserving a real response, or does it quietly make the other side sound foolish?
☐
Citations are correct. Your play citation and your scholarly source citation both follow the MLA mechanics from Lesson 7’s grammar lesson.
☐
Your Works Cited is complete. The play and your one verified scholarly source both appear, formatted correctly, with nothing missing.

Notice that four of these five items are not sentence-level correctness at all; a brief can be grammatically flawless and still fail every one of them. That is why this checklist comes before, not instead of, an ordinary proofread for spelling and punctuation.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND A FINAL PASS
An AI tool can catch a misplaced comma or a dropped word quickly, and there is nothing wrong with using it for that narrow job. What it cannot do is tell you whether your counterclaim section is treating the other side fairly, or whether a specific quote is doing real analytical work versus just sitting on the page. Those checks require rereading your own argument as a skeptical reader would, which is a job only you can do.
📚 PART 2 · FROM THE PAGE TO THE COUNCIL

Your closing argument is the spoken version of your brief’s conclusion, not a full rereading of the whole document. Three things change when your writing has to be delivered aloud instead of read silently. First, setup: begin by naming your verdict again, clearly, in your first sentence, the way you would if a listener had missed your written brief entirely. Second, pacing: a listener cannot reread a sentence the way a reader can, so slow down at your most important piece of evidence and let a short pause sit before you move on. Third, and most important, the final sentence: it is the last thing the Council hears, and it has to do real work, not just end the noise.

A strong final sentence asks the Council for something specific: a verdict, a reason to act on it, or both. Watch four options for the same closing argument below, and notice which one actually does that.

OPTION 1

“So in the end, there is a lot to think about here, and I hope you will consider everything I have said before you decide what happens next.”

OPTION 2

“Let this trial echo through the ages as the moment justice finally looked tyranny in the eye and refused to blink, for Thebes, for truth, for all mankind!”

OPTION 3

“Therefore, Council of Thebes, I ask you to affirm what Oedipus already carried out on himself: banishment, not as further cruelty, but as the one sentence that finally matches a crime no ordinary court was built to weigh.”

OPTION 4

“In conclusion, Oedipus killed his father and married his mother, and that is why I believe the deed-standard is the right one to use in this case.”

Try it yourself first, in your notebook: write your own final sentence for your closing argument before you open this box to compare.
Compare your sentence to Option 3 above. Does it name a specific verdict and ask the Council to act on it, the way Option 3 asks for affirmation of a specific sentence? If your sentence mostly restates a fact you already argued, trails off into a vague hope, or reaches for grand language without asking for anything concrete, revise it now, before you rehearse it aloud.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH FINAL SENTENCE ACTUALLY WORKS?
Which of the four final sentences above genuinely asks the Council for something specific, rather than trailing off, merely restating an already-argued fact, or grandstanding?
Which final sentence actually asks the Council for something specific?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
Option 1 trails off: “consider everything I have said” asks for nothing in particular and could close almost any argument on any topic.
Option 2 grandstands: the language is loud, but it never names a verdict or gives the Council a concrete reason to rule one way over another.
Option 4 merely restates: it repeats facts already established earlier in the brief instead of using the closing moment to make one final, specific request.

Once your own final sentence names something specific, rehearse it out loud, alone, at least three times. Say it slower than feels natural the first time. Let a short silence sit after it before you stop recording. That pause is doing real work: it is what tells a listener the sentence they just heard was the point of everything that came before it.

2

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A verdict named up front, quotes that actually argue, a counterclaim answered instead of mocked, and citations that hold up: that is a finished brief. A final sentence that asks for something specific, spoken slowly enough to land: that is a finished closing argument. You have both now. Deliver them.
