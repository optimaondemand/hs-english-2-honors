Choosing Your Story
Lesson · Week 2 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Before a teller opens their mouth, they have already made the hardest choice: which story to tell.
Odysseus is away from home for twenty years. Homer does not attempt to tell you all twenty. The poem you are reading picks one narrow slice of that decade, the final weeks of the journey, and tells that slice in full. Today you learn to make the same choice for your own tale, The Bard’s Hall. This lesson is direct preparation for Quick Write 2, where you will pitch the one episode or world your story will actually tell.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify what makes one possible story episode stronger than another for a short telling.
2
I will be able to narrow a large idea down to one bounded episode or world I can actually tell well.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Ten quiet minutes to test a few possible story ideas against each other.
📚 PART 1 · A WHOLE LIFE IS TOO BIG TO TELL

Recall how the Odyssey opens, a technique you studied in Week 1: not with Odysseus’s birth, not with the start of the Trojan War, but years into his journey home, in the middle of trouble. That choice is not just about where the sentences begin. It is a choice about scope: which part of a much larger life is actually worth an audience’s time. A story that tries to hold everything usually ends up holding nothing tightly.

A strong story seed passes three tests. First, it is bounded: it has a clear beginning and end, not an entire lifetime or an entire war. Second, it has stakes: something is genuinely at risk, and the outcome is not certain. Third, it reveals character: by the end, we know something true about someone that we did not know at the start, the same way a host’s treatment of a guest reveals exactly who that host is.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE · TWO POSSIBLE SEEDS, COMPARED
Too big: “The rise and fall of a great kingdom.”
This spans generations and dozens of characters. A short telling could only summarize it, never live inside it. Nothing specific has stakes because nothing specific is ever shown in detail.
Bounded: “A soldier returning from that kingdom’s last war finds a stranger sitting in his father’s chair.”
This is one scene, one moment of tension, one decision to make. It implies the whole kingdom’s history without having to tell it, and it reveals character immediately: how does this soldier react?

Notice what the bounded version does. It does not abandon the big idea (a kingdom, a war, a homecoming). It simply finds the one doorway into that world small enough to walk through in a single telling.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Think of two possible directions for your own Bard’s Hall story: it could be a lost episode of the Odyssey that Homer never tells (a stop on the journey home no one has heard of, or a moment involving a minor character), or it could be a modern retelling of a hero’s return in a world entirely your own. Either path works. What matters is narrowing it down.

✍️ In your notebook, write down two possible story ideas, then test each against the three questions: is it bounded, does it have real stakes, will it reveal character? Do this now, before you open this box to see the test applied to one more example.
THE TEST, APPLIED

Idea: “A watchman on the walls of Troy, on the single night the wooden horse is dragged inside, has to decide whether to raise the alarm he no longer believes in.”

Bounded? Yes: one night, one decision.

Stakes? Yes: a city’s fate turns on what he does next.

Reveals character? Yes: his choice shows whether duty or doubt wins in him.

⚡ TRY IT · BOUNDED OR TOO BIG?
Each idea below is written to make the judgment harder than it first looks. Decide what the seed actually promises to show, not just how it sounds at first read.
1. “A ferryman refuses to carry one last passenger across a flooded river at nightfall, though the passenger begs him.”
2. “The complete history of a seafaring people, from their first voyage to their final settlement centuries later.”
3. “The last hour of a dying king’s reign, told entirely through the three gifts he chooses to give away before he dies.”
➡️ WHAT YOU’LL DO NEXT
1
Quick Write 2 (Canvas assignment).
Use one of the ideas you tested today, or a stronger one it led you to, to pitch the episode or world your Bard’s Hall story will tell. Full directions are on the Quick Write 2 assignment page.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Homer did not try to tell twenty years. He told a few weeks and let the rest live in the background. Hold your own story idea to the same standard: bounded, at stake, and revealing of character. Carry whichever idea passed the test into Quick Write 2.
