Lesson 3.GV
Subordination for Reasoning
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 3
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Certain small words make the logic of an argument visible on the page: because, although, since, if…then.
A sentence that simply states two facts side by side leaves the reader to guess how they connect. A sentence that joins them with the right subordinating word shows the connection outright: which fact is the reason, which is the result, and which is a concession you are willing to grant. This is the single most useful sentence-level tool for the brief you will eventually write, so today’s practice matters more than it might look.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use because, although, since, and if…then clauses to connect a piece of evidence to a claim.
2
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · MAKING REASONING VISIBLE

A subordinate clause has a subject and a verb, like any clause, but it cannot stand alone; it depends on a main clause to complete its thought. When that subordinate clause opens with a word like because, although, since, or an if…then pair, it does more than add information. It names the exact relationship between two ideas: cause, concession, or condition. Compare a flat pair of sentences to the same content joined by a reasoning word, using facts from this week’s reading.

✎ ON THE BOARD · MAKING THE CONNECTION VISIBLE
Creon already shares in every benefit of royal power. Creon has no motive to seize the throne.
Flat: two true statements, side by side. The reader has to guess that the first explains the second.
Because Creon already shares in every benefit of royal power, he has no motive to seize the throne.
Because names the first idea as the reason for the second. The logic is now on the page, not left for the reader to supply.
Although Jocasta means her story as comfort, it terrifies Oedipus instead.
Although grants one fact (her intention) while pointing to a contrasting result. It is how a writer acknowledges something true without letting it undercut the main point.
If the herdsman remembers robbers, plural, then Oedipus cannot be the killer, since he acted alone.
If…then states a condition and its consequence. Notice the sentence also chains in since for a second reason, layering the logic further.

Notice that these words are not interchangeable. Because and since both give a reason, but although concedes a point before pivoting away from it, and if…then states a condition rather than a settled fact. Choosing the right one is part of reasoning honestly: do not dress up a concession as a cause, or a mere possibility as a certainty.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT RELATIONSHIP DOES THE CLAUSE NAME?
For each sentence, choose the relationship the subordinating word signals.
1. “Since Apollo prophesied he would kill his father, Oedipus fled Corinth.”
2. “Although Oedipus is visibly shaken by Jocasta’s story, he still insists on hearing the herdsman before drawing any conclusion.”
3. A student drafts: “Oedipus must be guilty because he is the kind of man who would do something like this.” What is the problem with this reasoning clause, even though it uses ‘because’ correctly?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in this week’s scenes: the Creon quarrel, Jocasta’s oracle story, and Oedipus’s Corinth story. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

probity (noun) — honesty and strong moral principle
Creon appeals to his own probity, known and trusted by all, as part of his case that he has no reason to lie about his innocence.
oath (noun) — a solemn promise, often calling on something sacred to guarantee its truth
Creon offers an oath as further backing for his claim of innocence, a formal way of raising the stakes on his own word.
venomous (adjective) — full of spite or malice; bitterly hostile
Oedipus accuses Creon of venomous hate with no evidence attached, the exact move you learned in Lesson 3.01 to watch for and avoid in your own writing.
roisterer (noun) — a loud, rowdy reveler, especially one who has been drinking
The drunken guest who first taunts Oedipus about his parentage at the Corinthian banquet, setting the whole chain of events toward Delphi in motion.
prophesied (verb, past tense of prophesy) — foretold what would happen, especially through divine or supernatural knowledge
What the Delphic oracle did when Oedipus sought a simple answer about his parents and instead received a terrible warning about his future.
herdsman (noun) — a person who tends a herd of livestock
The sole surviving witness to the attack on Laius, now the one person whose memory could confirm or clear Oedipus.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
Quick Write 11 is where these tools get tested for real. As you draft, use at least two reasoning clauses (because, although, since, or if…then) to connect your evidence to your claim, and work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what shows up in your own writing.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Because, although, since, and if…then are small words carrying a great deal of weight: they are how a writer shows a reader the shape of their thinking instead of leaving it hidden between two separate sentences. Carry two of them, and two Word Study words, into Quick Write 11.
