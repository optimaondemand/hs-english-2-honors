📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Two punctuation marks that hold an argument together: one joins two related claims, the other sets up the evidence behind one.
You just learned that a real argument needs a claim connected to its evidence. The semicolon and the colon are two of the small tools that make that connection visible on the page. You will use both, along with this week’s Word Study words, in Quick Write 9.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use a semicolon to join two closely related independent claims.
2
I will be able to use a colon to set up evidence, an explanation, or a list.
3
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · JOINING CLAIMS, SETTING UP EVIDENCE

A semicolon joins two independent claims (each one able to stand as its own complete sentence) that are closely related in meaning. It signals to the reader: these two ideas belong together, more tightly than two separate sentences would suggest, but not tightly enough to be joined with just a comma. A colon does a different job. It comes at the end of a complete statement and sets up what follows: an explanation, a list, or a specific piece of evidence that proves the claim just made. Think of the colon as a small arrow pointing forward: “here is what I mean, exactly.”

✎ ON THE BOARD · SEMICOLON OR COLON?
Thebes is dying by inches; its king has already sent for the god’s answer.
Semicolon: two complete, closely related claims about the same crisis, joined without a conjunction like “and.”
The oracle’s demand is exact: find the murderer of Laius, and drive him out.
Colon: a complete claim (“the oracle’s demand is exact”) followed by the specific evidence that explains it.
Oedipus does not hesitate; he issues a public proclamation before the priest has even finished speaking.
Semicolon: the second claim gives a closely related, almost proof-like follow-up to the first, so it earns the tighter connection.

A quick test for the semicolon: read each half on its own. If both halves could stand as complete sentences by themselves, and the ideas are closely related, a semicolon is a strong option. A quick test for the colon: the words before it must form a complete statement on their own. You cannot put a colon after “the oracle demands” because that clause never finishes its own thought; it needs the rest of the sentence to become complete first.

⚡ TRY IT · DOES THE PUNCTUATION ACTUALLY WORK?
Each item below looks correctly punctuated at first glance. Look closely at what stands on each side of the mark before you decide.
1. Read this sentence: “Oedipus does not hesitate; because he has already sent Creon to Delphi.” Something is wrong with the semicolon here even though the sentence looks balanced. What is the problem?
2. Read this sentence: “The oracle demands: find Laius’s murderer and drive him out.” Is the colon used correctly here?
3. Which punctuation correctly joins these two complete, closely related claims: “Thebes is dying by inches” and “its king has already sent for the god’s answer”?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in this week’s reading, the opening scene. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

suppliant (noun) — a person who humbly begs or petitions someone with power to help them
Oedipus asks the crowd why they “sit ye here as suppliants”: a whole city, kneeling before its own king.
blight (noun) — a disease or force that destroys plants, animals, or health on a large scale
The priest repeats the word three times, on the harvest, the herds, and childbirth, to show the plague striking every part of Theban life.
oracle (noun) — a sacred shrine where a god’s will is revealed, or the message given there
Creon travels to the oracle at Delphi to learn how Thebes can be saved; his report of what he learns there sets the whole plot in motion.
pollution (noun) — here, a religious stain or defilement on a community, not chemical waste
Creon reports that Apollo demands the city “extirpate a fell pollution,” meaning the unsolved murder itself has spiritually contaminated Thebes.
proclamation (noun) — an official public announcement made by someone in authority
Oedipus addresses one directly “to all” Thebans, demanding that anyone with knowledge of Laius’s murder come forward.
curse (noun/verb) — a solemn call for harm or misfortune to fall on someone
Oedipus lays this on Laius’s murderer, whoever he may be, and even extends it to cover himself if he unknowingly shelters the guilty man.
