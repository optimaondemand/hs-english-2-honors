📚 PART 1 · WHAT A RECITATION IS, AND WHY

A recitation is the memorization and spoken delivery of a short passage. No notes, no script, no page to glance at, just a passage you have learned well enough to say aloud in your own voice. It sounds old-fashioned, but it is the exact skill the Odyssey itself is built on. Demodocus does not read his song about Troy from a page. He carries it in memory and delivers it live, shaping his pace and his voice to the room in front of him.

This connects directly to your module project. The Bard’s Hall asks you to tell your own story aloud in Week 9. The recitation is your rehearsal for that skill, using someone else’s words first so that the pressure of inventing a story is not also on you yet. Get comfortable delivering a memorized passage now, and telling your own story later will feel far more familiar.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
In ancient Greece, professional performers called rhapsodes traveled from festival to festival reciting Homeric epic from memory, sometimes across several days of performance. Memorized delivery was not a shortcut or a lesser version of the poem. It was a genuine, respected craft, and reciting well was itself proof of skill and care for the story being told.
📚 PART 2 · YOUR PASSAGE MENU (CHOOSE ONE)

Read both passages below aloud once before you decide. Notice which one feels natural in your mouth and voice.

📜 PASSAGE MENU
OPTION A · THE PROEM
“Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
Why choose this one: it is the poem’s very first words, an invocation to a goddess and the epithet that defines its hero, all in a single sentence. Short, self-contained, and instantly recognizable.
OPTION B · ODYSSEUS’S SELF-NAMING (BOOK 9)
“Firstly, then, I will tell you my name that you too may know it, and one day, if I outlive this time of sorrow, may become my guests though I live so far away from all of you. I am Odysseus son of Laertes, renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety, so that my fame ascends to heaven.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
Why choose this one: it is the moment the hidden guest finally reveals who he is, at the very feast where Demodocus has just sung his own story back to him. A declaration, not a description.
Both passages come from the public-domain Butler translation. That translation originally used Roman names for the gods and hero (Ulysses instead of Odysseus); the Greek forms are restored here, as they are throughout this course.

You do not need to decide alone right now. Say each one aloud once. Whichever passage you can already say without stumbling is probably your passage.

📚 PART 3 · HOW TO PRACTICE

You have several weeks of ungraded practice before the recording is due. Use them. Here is a routine that works:

1
Memorize in chunks. Break your passage into two or three small phrases instead of trying to learn the whole thing at once. Master one chunk before linking it to the next.
2
Always say it aloud. Reading it silently will not build the same memory that speaking it out loud will.
3
Watch your pace. Rushing is the most common mistake. Speak a little slower than feels natural, and pause where a comma or a period would fall.
4
Eyes up. Each time you practice, try to look up from the page a little longer than the time before. By Week 8, you should not need it at all.
5
Let your voice carry the meaning. A proem asks a goddess for help before a grand tale begins. A self-naming is a declaration after twenty years of concealment. Decide what your passage is doing, then let your delivery show it.
