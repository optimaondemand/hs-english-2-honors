📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
This play was never meant to be read quietly at a desk. It was meant to be watched, in daylight, by thousands of citizens at once.
Before you meet the plague-stricken city of the opening scene, you need to know what kind of event you are stepping into. Greek tragedy was a civic occasion, not a private entertainment, performed for the whole community at a public festival. Today you learn how that theater was built and staffed, and you get the backstory the play assumes you already know: who Oedipus is, and why an entire city once owed him everything.

📚 PART 1 · A PLAY FOR THE WHOLE CITY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a large public event your whole town or school attends together, a game, a ceremony, a concert. What feels different about watching something with thousands of other people, compared to watching it alone? (No wrong answer.)

A festival, not a night out

Sophocles wrote Oedipus the King for a specific occasion: the City Dionysia, an annual religious festival in Athens honoring the god Dionysus, held in the open air in daylight before an audience that could number in the thousands. Attendance was a civic act, something closer to a citizen showing up to vote or serve on a jury than to buying a ticket for a show. Playwrights competed for a prize judged by the city itself, and the plays they wrote wrestled openly with the questions a community cared about most: justice, guilt, the gods, and the burdens of power. A play about a king whose choices might be poisoning his own city was not an escape from public life. It was public life, examined in front of everyone at once.

That civic setting matters for how you read the play now. Every choice Oedipus makes onstage was, in its first performance, being weighed by an audience of his fellow citizens, together, out loud, in real time. You are going to do something similar this module: weigh his choices yourself, and eventually argue a verdict.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Sophocles wrote Oedipus the King in Athens in the 400s BCE, likely in the years around 429, when the city itself was gripped by a real plague during the Peloponnesian War. An audience watching a fictional Thebes overwhelmed by disease had almost certainly watched real neighbors sicken and die not long before. This translation, by the English classicist F. Storr, is in the public domain, which is why you can read the whole play free online and why the quotations in this module come straight from the text itself.
📚 PART 2 · THE SHAPE OF A TRAGEDY

Two kinds of performers shared the stage, and they did very different jobs. A small handful of masked actors, often as few as two or three, played all the named characters, sometimes switching masks and roles within the same performance. Alongside them stood the chorus, a group of performers who sang, chanted, and danced between scenes. The chorus does not usually drive the plot. Its job is to represent the ordinary people of the city, reacting to events the way any citizen might: with fear, hope, confusion, or grief. When you read a choral passage in this play, you are hearing the voice of the community itself, watching its king from the outside.

🎬 THE ANCIENT THEATER, LABELED
SKENE
A backdrop building representing a palace or temple. Actors entered and exited through its doors and could change masks out of sight.
ORCHESTRA
A round, level performance space where the chorus sang and danced, closest to the audience, at the center of the whole design.
THEATRON (AUDIENCE)
Tiered stone seating built into a hillside, curving around the orchestra so thousands of citizens could see and hear at once, in open daylight.

Notice what this design makes impossible: privacy. The orchestra sits at the center of a bowl of citizens. Whatever happens there happens in full view of the whole community, which is exactly the pressure Oedipus is under in this play. He cannot solve his city’s crisis quietly, off to the side. Every step he takes as king happens in public, in front of the very people whose lives depend on the outcome.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT THE STAGING MEANS

3. The priest opens the play by reminding Oedipus of the Sphinx, rather than simply asking for help. Why does the play establish this backstory first?
📚 PART 3 · WHY THIS KING? THE SPHINX BACKSTORY

The play opens without explaining who Oedipus is or why the city trusts him, because its first audience already knew. Before the action begins, Thebes had been terrorized by the Sphinx, a monster with a riddle, who killed every traveler unable to answer it. Oedipus, a stranger arriving from Corinth, solved the riddle and freed the city. In gratitude, Thebes made him king and gave him the hand of its recently widowed queen, Jocasta. That single act, years before the play starts, is the entire foundation of Oedipus’s authority: he did not inherit the throne, he earned it by saving the city once already.

The priest who opens the play reminds Oedipus of exactly this debt, addressing him as the man who once freed Thebes from what the text calls, in its own poetic phrase, the “fell songstress,” a riddling reference to the Sphinx herself:

“Art thou not he who coming to the town of Cadmus freed us from the tax we paid to the fell songstress?”
The Priest, trans. F. Storr, public domain

That is why the opening scene shows a city turning to Oedipus again, not to a general or a priest, but specifically to him. He is not merely their king by birth. He is the man who has already proven, once, that he can save Thebes when nothing else can. Hold onto that fact as you read what happens next: a community that trusts its king completely, based on real evidence, is about to ask him to save it a second time, from a danger no one yet understands.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND BACKGROUND FACTS
An AI tool can summarize the Sphinx story and the layout of a Greek theater accurately, since both are well documented. What it cannot do is decide, for you, why Sophocles chooses to have the priest bring up the Sphinx in the play’s very first speech, rather than somewhere later. That is a reading decision, and it stays yours to make no matter how good the summary is.
