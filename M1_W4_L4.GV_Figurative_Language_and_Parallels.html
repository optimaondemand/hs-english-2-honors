📚 PART 1 · PRECISE WORDS AND MATCHED LISTS

Precise diction means choosing the exact word a moment calls for instead of a vague, all-purpose one. “Nice palace” tells a reader almost nothing; “a palace with bronze walls and gold doors” tells them something they can picture. A parallel series lists three or more items in the same grammatical form, so the list reads smoothly instead of lurching between different shapes of phrase.

✎ ON THE BOARD · VAGUE VS. PRECISE, AND A PARALLEL SERIES
The hall was really impressive.
Vague: “really impressive” could describe almost anything. It gives the reader no actual image.
The hall’s bronze walls and gold doors caught the firelight.
Precise: specific materials replace the vague label, and the reader can now picture the actual room.
Demodocus could move a hall to tears, command a king’s attention, and turn a stranger’s own war into song.
Parallel series: three matched verb phrases, each the same shape (verb plus object), keep the list moving instead of stumbling over mismatched grammar.

The fastest test for diction: could this word describe almost anything? If so, replace it with the one specific detail that could only describe this. The fastest test for a parallel series: read each item on its own after the shared verb or lead-in. If one item suddenly needs different grammar to make sense, it is breaking the pattern and needs to be reshaped to match the others.

⚡ TRY IT · PRECISE OR JUST DRESSED UP? PARALLEL OR SUBTLY BROKEN?
Read each item carefully. A fancier word is not automatically a more precise one, and a list can look parallel even when one item quietly breaks the pattern.
1. Which phrase is more precise: “the palace radiated resplendent grandeur” or “the palace’s bronze walls and gold doors caught the firelight”?
2. Which list is truly parallel: “Demodocus could move a hall to tears, commanding a king’s attention, and turn a stranger’s war into song” or “Demodocus could move a hall to tears, command a king’s attention, and turn a stranger’s war into song”?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 7 and 8. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

suppliant (noun) — a person who humbly and earnestly asks for help or protection
Odysseus becomes exactly this at Arete’s feet: a person with no standing of his own, asking to be taken in.
lofty (adjective) — very high, grand, or noble
Used to describe the walls of the Phaeacian city, and just as fitting for the palace Odysseus is about to enter.
minstrel (noun) — a singer or musician who performs stories and songs, especially in verse
The Phaeacians name their own minstrels among their proudest skills, alongside sailing and dancing.
muse (noun) — a source of poetic inspiration
The poem credits Demodocus’s gift for song to inspiration from a muse, treating the ability to move an audience as something given, not only practiced.
prowess (noun) — skill and courage, especially shown in a contest or a difficult task
What the Phaeacian youths dare Odysseus to show in the games, not expecting exactly how much of it he has.
nimbly (adverb) — quickly and lightly, with easy control of movement
Describes the young Phaeacian dancers during the games, in sharp, graceful contrast to the discus Odysseus throws with raw strength instead.
