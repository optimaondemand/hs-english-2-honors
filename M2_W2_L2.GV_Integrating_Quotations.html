📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A quotation dropped into an essay with no lead-in reads like a stray sentence. A quotation folded into your own sentence reads like proof.
You already know how to find strong evidence and state it fairly. This lesson gives you the grammar to fold that evidence smoothly into your own writing, with lead-in phrases, honest brackets, and honest ellipses. You will use both today’s Word Study words and this grammar in Quick Write 10.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to introduce a quotation with a lead-in phrase and blend a quotation grammatically into my own sentence.
2
I will be able to use brackets and ellipses honestly, without changing what a quotation actually says.
3
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words, drawn from the Tiresias scene.
📚 PART 1 · FOLDING A QUOTATION INTO YOUR SENTENCE

A quotation dropped into your writing with no setup forces your reader to guess why it is there. A lead-in phrase names who is speaking and why the line matters, before the quotation arrives. A blended quotation goes further: it fits the quoted words directly into the grammar of your own sentence, so the sentence still reads smoothly if you read it straight through.

Compare the three versions below, all built from real lines in the Tiresias scene.

✎ ON THE BOARD · THREE WAYS TO USE A QUOTATION
DROPPED QUOTATION (AVOID THIS)
Tiresias is angry at Oedipus. “Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land.”
The quotation just appears with no grammatical connection to the sentence before it. A reader has to work to see why it is there.
LEAD-IN PHRASE
Provoked past patience, Tiresias finally declares: “Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land.”
The lead-in (“Provoked past patience, Tiresias finally declares”) tells the reader who is speaking and why, right before the words arrive.
BLENDED QUOTATION
Tiresias names Oedipus as “the accursed polluter of this land,” reversing the very search Oedipus himself began.
Here the quoted words (“the accursed polluter of this land”) are fitted directly into the grammar of the writer’s own sentence, as its object. Read the whole sentence aloud: it flows as one sentence, not two stitched together.

The fastest test for a blended quotation: read your full sentence aloud, quotation marks and all. If it does not sound like one grammatical sentence, the quotation is not blended yet.

📚 PART 2 · BRACKETS AND ELLIPSES, USED HONESTLY

Sometimes a quotation needs a small edit to fit your sentence. Two tools let you edit honestly, without changing what the text actually says.

Square brackets [ ] replace or clarify a word, usually a pronoun, so the quotation makes sense outside its original sentence.
Original: “Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land.”
With a bracket: Tiresias insists that “[Oedipus] art the man… the accursed polluter of this land.”
An ellipsis (…) shows you cut words from the middle of a quotation, honestly, without changing its meaning.
Original: “Sore perplexed am I by the words of the master seer. Are they true, are they false? I know not and bridle my tongue for fear.”
Honestly shortened: “Sore perplexed am I by the words of the master seer… I know not and bridle my tongue for fear.”

Remember what you learned in Lesson 2.03: a cut that changes the sentence’s actual meaning is not a shortcut, it is a misquotation. An honest ellipsis only ever removes words that were not doing meaning-carrying work.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in the Tiresias confrontation scene. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

seer (noun) — a person believed able to see or foretell the future
The title Oedipus himself gives Tiresias: “Teiresias, seer who comprehendest all.” The word becomes ironic once Tiresias proves he sees more than the seeing king.
prophet (noun) — one who speaks with special, often divinely given, insight into hidden or future truth
Oedipus mockingly demands proof of “the prophet’s art” that solved the Sphinx’s riddle, forgetting that the answer he mocks is about to be proven true about himself.
riddle (noun) — a puzzling question or statement requiring cleverness to solve
Oedipus is famous for solving the Sphinx’s riddle. Tiresias leaves him a new one on his way out, and this time Oedipus cannot solve it.
blind (adjective) — unable to see; here, also unable to perceive an obvious truth
Tiresias is blind in his eyes. He tells Oedipus that Oedipus is blind “in ear, wit, eye, in everything,” turning a physical word into a judgment about the king’s mind.
bane (noun) — a cause of ruin, harm, or persistent trouble
Tiresias’s exact word for Oedipus when the king blames Creon: “thou thyself art thine own bane.” Oedipus is, in this telling, his own worst enemy.
polluter (noun) — one who contaminates or defiles, here a person whose guilt is believed to bring a curse on a whole community
Tiresias’s direct accusation: Oedipus is “the accursed polluter of this land.” The word ties one man’s guilt to the whole city’s suffering, which is exactly the standard your Trial of Oedipus brief will have to weigh.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
In Quick Write 10, integrate at least two quotations using a lead-in phrase or a blended quotation, not a dropped quotation, and work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what shows up in your own Evidence Log.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A blended quotation and an honest ellipsis both do the same job: they let the text speak for itself without distorting it. Carry both habits, and today’s six words, into your Evidence Log.
