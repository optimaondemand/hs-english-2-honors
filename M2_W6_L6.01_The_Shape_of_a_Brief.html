📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A verdict without a structure is just an opinion with better vocabulary. Today you learn the structure.
You have read the whole play, and you have a sense of where you stand on Oedipus. That is not the same as having a brief. A real formal argument has a shape, six moves that always appear in the same order, and today you learn that shape on a small, low-stakes question before you ever touch the big one. This is a reread week: no new scenes, just the evidence you already have, organized on purpose.

📚 PART 1 · THE SIX MOVES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a disagreement, in class or out of it, where naming the actual point of dispute (not just restating opinions louder) helped settle it. What made the real question clear? (No wrong answer.)

Six moves, always in this order

A formal brief is not a pile of evidence. It is a structure, and every real brief you will ever write, in this course or beyond it, uses some version of these six moves, in this order:

1 · ISSUE
What exactly must be decided, and under which standard of judgment. Not just “is Oedipus guilty,” but guilty under what test: does intent matter, or only the deed itself?
2 · VERDICT
Your thesis. Precise, defensible, and committed, naming the standard it rests on. Not a shrug, and not a slogan.
3 · ARGUMENTS
Each one is a claim, backed by evidence, connected by reasoning. Not evidence alone; the reasoning has to do real work.
4 · COUNTERCLAIM
The strongest case against your verdict, stated fairly, the way its own advocate would state it. Never a strawman.
5 · RESPONSE
Why the counterclaim, real as it is, does not overturn your verdict.
6 · CONCLUSION
What should actually happen, and what that decision means for the community that has to live with it.

Notice where the standard of judgment lives: it belongs in the Issue, stated before you ever announce a Verdict. Skip that step, and your whole brief quietly assumes an answer to the one question it was supposed to argue.

📚 PART 2 · A FULL BRIEF, ON A SMALL QUESTION FIRST

Before you build the real brief, watch the whole structure work on a much smaller question the play already answers for you: did Creon conspire with Tiresias to falsely accuse Oedipus? You already have every piece of evidence this needs, from the Tiresias confrontation and the Creon quarrel.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE · DID CREON CONSPIRE WITH TIRESIAS?
ISSUE
Under a standard that requires actual evidence of coordinated intent (not mere convenient timing), did Creon plot with Tiresias to accuse Oedipus falsely?
VERDICT
“Under a standard requiring evidence of coordinated intent, Creon did not conspire with Tiresias; his own defense rests on motive alone, and nothing in the text shows contact between the two men beyond suspicious timing.”
ARGUMENT
Claim: Creon had no rational motive to want the throne.
Evidence: “Now all my needs are satisfied through thee, / And I have naught to fear; but were I king, / My acts would oft run counter to my will” and “I am not so infatuate as to grasp / The shadow when I hold the substance fast.” (Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain)
Reasoning: A conspiracy needs a motive strong enough to risk exposure. Creon names, specifically, why the crown would cost him more freedom than it would ever hand him, so the premise underneath the accusation, that he wanted power badly enough to plot for it, does not hold.
COUNTERCLAIM, FAIRLY STATED
Oedipus’s suspicion is not baseless. Tiresias’s accusation lands right after Oedipus’s own aggressive proclamation, and a genuinely convenient piece of timing deserves real scrutiny, not automatic dismissal.
RESPONSE
Convenient timing is not the same charge as secret collusion. Wanting a prophet to check a king’s overreach is not the same act as arranging a false accusation with him. No line in the confrontation or the quarrel shows the two men in contact beforehand; the Issue asked for coordinated intent, and timing alone cannot supply it.
CONCLUSION
Under the standard this brief committed to at the start, the conspiracy charge fails. What it actually exposes is Oedipus’s own pattern: demanding proof only when a result is convenient. That pattern is itself evidence worth carrying into the larger trial.

Notice what made this brief work on a small canvas: the Issue named a standard before anyone reached a verdict, the Argument connected a specific quote to a specific piece of reasoning instead of just dropping the quote and moving on, and the Counterclaim was a real, fair version of the other side, not a strawman built to lose.

A model Issue: “Under a standard that weighs what Oedipus intended, rather than the plague’s indifference to intent, is Oedipus guilty of the death of Laius?” Notice this names the standard being argued for (intent) before any verdict is reached. If you named the deed-standard instead, that is equally valid; the point is that your Issue names a standard at all.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND BRIEF STRUCTURE
An AI tool can hand you a six-part outline shape in seconds: issue, verdict, arguments, counterclaim, response, conclusion. What it cannot do is check, on its own, whether the actual evidence you plan to use fits the verdict you actually want to argue. That match, evidence to claim to standard, is the part only you can verify against the text.

WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
“Creon is completely innocent” is bold but vague: it names no standard and gives a reader nothing to test it against.
“Might have been… hard to say for certain” is hedged to nothing: it never actually commits to a position an advocate could defend.
“Secretly met before the confrontation” is precise but unsupported: it names a standard and commits to a claim, but invents a meeting the text never shows. Precision without real evidence is not an advantage.
