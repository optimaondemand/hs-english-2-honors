📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Two small habits, correctly punctuating a citation and cutting your own throat-clearing, make a draft look like a finished brief instead of a rough one.
You learned MLA citation forms yesterday. Today you learn exactly where the punctuation goes around them, plus a first-pass revision habit: cutting the wordy openers that delay your actual claim. You will use both, and this week’s Word Study words, in Quick Write 15.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to place a sentence’s final period correctly when a parenthetical citation follows a quotation.
2
I will be able to keep a quoted question mark inside the quotation marks, even when a citation and a new period follow it.
3
I will be able to cut a wordy opener so a sentence starts on its actual claim, and define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · WHERE THE PERIOD GOES

When a parenthetical citation follows a quotation, the sentence’s final period moves to after the closing parenthesis, never inside the quotation marks. The parenthetical citation is still part of your sentence; the sentence is not finished until it is.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PERIOD PLACEMENT WITH A CITATION
WRONG
Oedipus warns the murderer he will face “utter wretchedness.” (Sophocles)
The period sits inside the quotation marks, before the citation, making the citation look like it starts a brand-new sentence.
RIGHT
Oedipus warns the murderer he will face “utter wretchedness” (Sophocles).
The period moves to after the parenthesis, since the citation is still part of the same sentence.
WRONG (A QUOTED QUESTION)
Creon demands to know, “What then’s thy will? To banish me the land” (Sophocles)?
Moving the question mark outside the quotation marks makes it look like the writer is asking the question, when it actually belongs to Creon’s own words.
RIGHT (A QUOTED QUESTION)
Creon demands to know, “What then’s thy will? To banish me the land?” (Sophocles).
The question mark stays inside the quotation marks, because it belongs to Creon’s own words, not the writer’s. The writer’s own sentence still needs its own final period, placed after the citation.

Remember the ellipsis and bracket rules from Week 2: any cut you make inside a quotation still has to preserve what the line actually says. That honesty rule does not change just because a citation now follows the quotation; it simply means your citation should point to the same honestly-quoted words.

⚡ TRY IT · IS THE PUNCTUATION ACTUALLY RIGHT?
Each sentence below cites a real line from the play. Look closely at the punctuation around the quotation marks and the citation before you decide.
1. Oedipus calls himself “the miscreant by heaven itself declared unclean” (Sophocles).
2. Creon asks Oedipus, “What then’s thy will? To banish me the land” (Sophocles)?
📚 PART 2 · CUTTING THE THROAT-CLEARING

A “throat-clearing” opener is a phrase that delays your actual claim without adding anything: “In my opinion, it seems clear that…” or “I think that it could be argued that…” An advocate’s brief has already established, in Lesson 1.03, that you are arguing a claim, not sharing a feeling; these openers quietly undo that work by hedging every sentence before it starts.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CUTTING THE OPENER
BEFORE
In my opinion, it seems clear that Oedipus’s curse is an example of dramatic irony.
AFTER
Oedipus’s curse is an example of dramatic irony.
Nothing is lost. The claim was always the point; the opener was only delaying it.

A quick test: read your sentence without the opening phrase. If the claim still stands, fully intact, the opener was throat-clearing, and it should go.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in the play’s final scenes, the ones you are rereading this week as you gather evidence. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

accursed (adjective) — under a curse; doomed or condemned
Tiresias’s exact charge against Oedipus: “thou the accursed polluter of this land.” The word ties Oedipus’s own curse back onto himself.
banish (verb) — to officially expel someone from a place, especially a country or city
Creon names it as one of two possible punishments early on (“Banishment, or the shedding blood for blood”), and it becomes Oedipus’s actual fate by the play’s final scene.
witness (verb) — to see an event happen and be able to attest to it firsthand
In his final speech, Oedipus addresses the crossroads themselves, asking whether they recall “those deeds of mine ye witnessed,” the killing that started everything.
abhor (verb) — to regard with disgust and hatred
Oedipus uses it of the still-unknown murderer, vowing his “heart shall still abhor” him, not yet aware whom he is condemning.
miscreant (noun) — a villain or wrongdoer
Oedipus’s own word for himself once the truth is known: “the miscreant by heaven itself declared unclean.”
canker (noun) — a spreading corruption or decay, often hidden beneath a healthy surface
Oedipus describes his own hidden guilt this way, looking back on his youth in Corinth: “how foul the canker that lay festering in the bud.”
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
As you draft Quick Write 15, correctly punctuate at least one citation following a quotation, tighten at least one sentence by cutting a throat-clearing opener, and work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what shows up in your own draft.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A citation’s period belongs after the parenthesis, a quoted question mark stays inside the quotation marks, and a claim is stronger the moment it stops clearing its throat. Carry all three, and today’s six words, into your draft.
