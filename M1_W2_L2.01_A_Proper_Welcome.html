📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Telemachus finally reaches a household where the rule of the guest is still honored.
Last week you watched the suitors break every rule of guest and host in Odysseus’s own hall. This week, Telemachus travels to Pylos and meets Nestor, an old friend of his father’s from the Trojan War, and finds a household that does the opposite of everything the suitors did. Nestor feeds him, honors him, and lets him tell his story, all before he ever asks who Telemachus is or why he has come. Watching Nestor do this correctly will teach you more about the suitors’ failure than any list of their crimes could.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe the correct order of hospitality that Nestor follows: welcome, feast, honor, story, and only then the guest’s errand.
2
I will be able to contrast Nestor’s household with the suitors’ behavior in Odysseus’s hall.
3
I will be able to point to a moment in Book 3 where Telemachus acts with more confidence than he showed in Book 2.

📚 PART 1 · A FEAST ON THE SHORE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time an adult you barely knew went out of their way to make you feel welcome somewhere new. What did they actually do? (No wrong answer.)

Telemachus and Mentor (Athena in disguise) land at Pylos in the middle of a public sacrifice. The whole community is gathered on the beach, honoring Poseidon with an enormous feast, before any stranger has even been noticed:

“Now the people of Pylos were gathered on the sea shore to offer sacrifice of black bulls to Poseidon, lord of the Earthquake. There were nine guilds with five hundred men in each, and there were nine bulls to each guild.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Nestor’s son Pisistratus spots the newcomers and, without asking a single question, brings them into the feast already underway. He hands them meat and a golden cup of wine and asks them to join the prayer to Poseidon before anything else:

“Then he gave them their portions of the inward meats and poured wine for them into a golden cup, handing it to Athena first, and saluting her at the same time. ‘Offer a prayer, sir,’ said he, ‘to King Poseidon, for it is his feast that you are joining.’”
Pisistratus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
A public sacrifice like this one was not just a meal. It was a religious act meant to secure a community’s safety and good fortune, performed in full view of everyone. That Nestor folds two total strangers into a ceremony this important, without hesitation, tells an ancient audience everything it needs to know about the kind of king he is.
📚 PART 2 · THE ORDER NESTOR GETS RIGHT

Once the feast is finished, Nestor still does not ask his guests’ names. He talks with them, tells stories of the Trojan War, and only afterward invites Telemachus to say who he is and what he wants. When Telemachus does explain that he needs shelter for the night, Nestor will not hear of anything less than his own home and his own bedding:

📝 NESTOR’S WORDS, AND WHAT THEY REVEAL
“Heaven and the immortal gods forbid that you should leave my house to go on board of a ship. Do you think I am so poor and short of clothes, or that I have so few cloaks and rugs as to be unable to find comfortable beds both for myself and for my guests?”
Nestor, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
A HOST WHO TAKES OFFENSE AT LESS
Nestor treats it as almost insulting that his guest might sleep somewhere less comfortable than his own household provides. Compare that to the suitors, who eat someone else’s food and offer their host’s own son nothing.
GENEROSITY OFFERED FREELY
Nothing here is grudging. Nestor offers his best without being asked twice, which is the opposite of a guest helping himself to what was never offered at all.

Line the two households up side by side. In Odysseus’s hall, uninvited men take a host’s cattle and never leave. In Nestor’s hall, invited strangers are given a place at a sacred feast and a bed in the king’s own house, and no one has to demand it. The same custom, xenia, produces opposite behavior depending entirely on the character of the people involved. That is the point this poem keeps making: a rule is only as good as the person keeping it.

📚 PART 3 · A LESS HESITANT TELEMACHUS

Before he ever reaches Nestor, Telemachus admits to Mentor that he is nervous about approaching a king so much older and more experienced than himself: “dare I go up to Nestor, and how am I to address him? I have never yet been used to holding long conversations with people.” This is the same young man who stood silent through most of Book 1.

But something has shifted since Book 2. Once he is seated and fed as an honored guest, Telemachus does speak, asking Nestor directly for news of his father and even correcting himself partway through his own question, catching his nerves and pushing past them. It is a small moment, but it is a real one: a young man who was silent among the suitors finds his voice the moment he is treated as though his words matter.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES IT REVEAL?
Each moment below has more than one plausible reading. Choose the one Book 3 best supports, and be ready to say why the others overreach or miss the mark.
1. Pisistratus feeds Telemachus and Mentor at the sacrifice and invites them into the prayer to Poseidon, all before he has asked either of them a single question. What does this moment most reveal?
2. Nestor insists Telemachus sleep in his own house, invoking “Heaven and the immortal gods forbid” and asking whether Telemachus thinks him too poor to provide proper bedding. What does this moment most reveal?
3. Telemachus admits to Mentor that he is nervous about addressing Nestor, then still asks him directly for news of Odysseus once he has been fed and honored as a guest. What does this moment most reveal?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Nestor gets the order right: welcome first, feast and honor next, the guest’s own story invited, and only then his errand. Every step is offered before it is demanded. Set that beside the suitors from last week, and the poem’s point becomes plain: the same custom can build a household or wreck one, depending entirely on who is keeping it.
