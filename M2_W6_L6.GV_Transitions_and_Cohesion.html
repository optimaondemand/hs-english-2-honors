📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A transition word is a promise. “Therefore” promises a real consequence is coming. Broken promises read as logic even when they are not.
You just built a mini-brief and chose a standard of judgment. The last piece holding an argument together is the smallest one: the words that connect one sentence to the next. Today you learn to tell a transition that carries real logic from one that only sounds like it does, and you meet six more Word Study words attested in the play.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use transitions (therefore, however, moreover, by contrast) that match what the sentences around them actually do.
2
I will be able to build cohesion across a paragraph by echoing key terms instead of switching vocabulary at random.
3
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · TRANSITIONS THAT PROMISE, AND TRANSITIONS THAT KEEP THE PROMISE

Every transition word makes a small promise about the logical relationship between what comes before it and what comes after. Therefore promises: what follows is a real consequence of what I just said. However promises: what follows genuinely contrasts with what I just said. Moreover promises: what follows adds real, additional support to the same point. By contrast promises: what follows is a real, meaningful difference, not just a different topic. A transition fails, even when it is grammatically correct, the moment the sentence after it does not keep the promise the word just made. That failure is easy to miss, because the sentence still sounds logical. Readers, and readers who grade briefs, notice it anyway.

Cohesion is the related skill of making a paragraph feel like one continuous argument rather than several disconnected sentences. One reliable way to build it: keep echoing your key terms (your standard, your central claim’s key word) instead of swapping in a new synonym every sentence just to sound varied. A reader should never have to work to figure out that “the standard,” “the test,” and “the measure” in three consecutive sentences are all supposed to mean the same thing.

✎ ON THE BOARD · THE SAME ARGUMENT, TWICE
FAKE CONNECTIVE TISSUE
Oedipus fled Corinth to escape the prophecy. However, he killed a man at the crossroads. Therefore, Jocasta married her own son.
Each transition sounds formal, but neither promise is kept. The second sentence is not really a contrast with the first; the third is not a consequence of the second. The logic is only decoration.
REAL CONNECTIVE TISSUE
Oedipus fled Corinth to escape the prophecy; however, the road he chose to escape it led him directly into the very crossroads the prophecy warned him about. He killed a stranger there in anger, not yet knowing the man was his father. Therefore, the same flight meant to prove his innocence became, without his knowledge, the first act of his guilt.
Here “however” introduces a genuine contrast (his intention versus the outcome), and “therefore” introduces a genuine consequence that the previous sentence actually earns.

A quick test for any transition: cover it up and ask what relationship actually holds between the two sentences on either side. Then uncover the transition and ask whether it names that same relationship. If they do not match, the fix is almost never a fancier word; it is usually a plainer, more honest one, or a rewritten sentence that actually earns the one you wanted.

⚡ TRY IT · DOES THE TRANSITION KEEP ITS PROMISE?
Each item below uses a transition that looks correctly placed. Check what relationship the sentences actually have before you decide.
1. “Oedipus swore to find Laius’s murderer with his own hands. However, he pursued the case relentlessly, even after Jocasta begged him to stop.” Is “however” the right transition here?
2. A draft brief reads: “Oedipus deserves acquittal on the standard of intent. Moreover, the plague devastated Thebes at every level of society.” Why does “moreover” fail here, even though both sentences are true and well supported elsewhere in the play?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

This is a reread week, so these six words are drawn from across the whole play, not just one scene. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

sentence (noun/verb) — a formal judgment or punishment handed down for wrongdoing
Oedipus uses this exact word against himself: “By my own sentence am cut off, condemned by my own proclamation ‘gainst the wretch,” realizing the curse he pronounced on Laius’s killer now falls on him.
judge (verb) — to form and deliver an authoritative decision after weighing evidence
Creon, defending himself against the conspiracy charge, tells Oedipus plainly: “Then having heard me, judge,” asking to be evaluated on his reasoning, not simply believed or dismissed.
arraign (verb) — to formally accuse someone before a judging authority
The chorus uses this word as Tiresias is brought in: “But here is one to arraign him,” expecting the prophet to formally name Laius’s killer.
guilt (noun) — responsibility for a wrongdoing
In his opening curse, Oedipus extends his sentence to “all the partners in his guilt,” not realizing how directly the word will come to apply to himself.
banishment (noun) — formal, forced exclusion from a community or land
Creon reports the oracle’s exact two options for the killer of Laius: “Banishment, or the shedding blood for blood,” setting the terms Oedipus will eventually apply to himself.
expiation (noun) — an act that makes amends for wrongdoing or removes its stain
Oedipus asks Creon directly, “What expiation means he? What’s amiss?”, wanting to know exactly what the god requires to lift the plague.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
Quick Write 14 is where these tools get tested for real. As you draft your thesis and outline, use at least two real logical transitions (therefore, however, moreover, or by contrast) that actually match what your sentences do, and work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what shows up in your own writing.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A transition word is a promise about logic, not a decoration on top of it. Before you use therefore, however, moreover, or by contrast in Quick Write 14, check that the sentences on either side actually keep the promise the word makes.
