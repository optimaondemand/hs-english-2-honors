Lesson 2.03
Evidence That Holds
Lesson · Week 2 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
An advocate who hides the evidence that hurts their case does not win. They just lose later, and lose worse.
Last week you took a first position on Oedipus’s verdict. This week you start building the evidence file that will actually support (or challenge) that position. This lesson teaches you what counts as real evidence from a play, how to quote it honestly, and why the strongest advocates are the ones who state evidence fairly, including the pieces that do not favor them.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell the difference between a quotation and a paraphrase, and cite a line from a play in my own prose.
2
I will be able to spot the difference between evidence stated fairly and evidence quoted out of context to mislead.
3
I will be able to state, for one piece of evidence, what it proves and what it does not prove.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT COUNTS AS EVIDENCE

In a literary argument, evidence is something the text actually says or does, not a feeling you have about a character. A quotation is the exact words of the text, set off in quotation marks. A paraphrase restates the same idea faithfully in your own words, without quotation marks, and is useful when the exact wording matters less than the content. Both need a citation telling your reader where the evidence comes from.

Since a play does not have page numbers the way a novel does, you cite it by naming the speaker and, where useful, the moment: (Tiresias, in the confrontation scene) rather than a line number. That is enough for a reader to locate the evidence and trust that you have represented it accurately.

📝 QUOTE VS. PARAPHRASE, SIDE BY SIDE
Quotation: Tiresias tells Oedipus, “Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land” (Tiresias, in the confrontation scene).
Paraphrase: Tiresias states directly that Oedipus himself is the source of the plague on Thebes (Tiresias, in the confrontation scene).
📚 PART 2 · STRONG EVIDENCE VS. CHERRY-PICKED EVIDENCE

Here is the real line from the play, spoken by Tiresias when Oedipus accuses him and Creon of conspiring together:

“Not Creon, thou thyself art thine own bane.”
Tiresias, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Now look at two different ways a student could use this same line in a brief.

CHERRY-PICKED · QUOTES ONLY PART OF THE LINE
“Even Tiresias says, ‘Not Creon…’ so Creon must be the real conspirator here, not Oedipus.”
This quotes three real words and still misleads. The full line says the opposite: Tiresias is clearing Creon of blame and pointing straight at Oedipus (“thou thyself art thine own bane”). Cutting the sentence off midway reverses its meaning.
STATED FAIRLY · THE WHOLE LINE, IN CONTEXT
“Tiresias responds to Oedipus’s conspiracy accusation directly: ‘Not Creon, thou thyself art thine own bane’ (Tiresias, in the confrontation scene). He clears Creon and names Oedipus as the cause of his own downfall.”
This gives the reader the whole sentence and states plainly what it proves: Tiresias directly rejects the conspiracy theory. It also does not overclaim. It proves what Tiresias says; it does not, by itself, prove that Tiresias is right, since the chorus has already told you an accusation still needs “sign assured.”

This is the habit that separates a real advocate from someone just trying to win an argument: state exactly what your evidence proves, and be equally honest about what it does not prove yet.

📚 PART 3 · YOUR TURN

Reread this line, spoken by Tiresias earlier in the same confrontation: “I say thou art the murderer of the man whose murderer thou pursuest.”

✍️ In your notebook, write two sentences using this line as evidence: one that states it fairly, in full, with a citation, and one that names what it does and does not prove. Do this now, before you open this box.
ONE WAY TO WRITE IT

Tiresias states without hedging, “I say thou art the murderer of the man whose murderer thou pursuest” (Tiresias, in the confrontation scene). This proves that a respected prophet directly names Oedipus as the killer he is hunting. It does not, on its own, prove Oedipus committed the murder, since no supporting fact or witness has been offered yet.

Compare it to your own draft. Did you quote the full sentence rather than a fragment? Did you name what the evidence proves, and separately, what it does not?

⚡ TRY IT · FAIR OR CHERRY-PICKED?
Decide whether each use of evidence is fairly stated or cherry-picked.
1. “Tiresias tells Oedipus, ‘Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land’ (Tiresias, in the confrontation scene). This shows the prophet’s direct accusation, though it is not yet proof on its own.”
2. “Tiresias himself admits, ‘Not Creon…’ which proves Creon is innocent and someone else is the real threat.”
3. “Tiresias tells Oedipus he is blind ‘in ear, wit, eye, in everything.’ This proves Oedipus is completely innocent of intending any wrongdoing.”

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Quote the whole sentence, cite where it comes from, and say plainly what it does and does not prove. That habit, more than any clever phrase, is what makes an advocate trustworthy, and a trustworthy advocate is the only kind who actually wins a hard case.
