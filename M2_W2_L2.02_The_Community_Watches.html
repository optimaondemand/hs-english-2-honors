📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The chorus just heard a blind prophet accuse their king of murder. What do you do with an accusation like that?
In a Greek tragedy, the chorus stands in for the ordinary citizens watching events unfold, reacting the way a crowd of Thebans actually might. Once Tiresias exits, the chorus sings an ode processing what it just heard, and that ode is worth close attention. It is the closest thing this play has to a jury thinking out loud, and thinking through a hard case in public is exactly the discipline your future Trial of Oedipus brief will require of you.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what pulls the chorus in two directions after Tiresias’s accusation.
2
I will be able to connect the chorus’s undecided position to the future jury you will argue in front of in the Trial of Oedipus.
📚 PART 1 · TWO REASONS PULLING IN TWO DIRECTIONS

The chorus opens by admitting, in plain terms, that it does not know what to think:

“Sore perplexed am I by the words of the master seer. Are they true, are they false? I know not and bridle my tongue for fear.”
Chorus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

That is not indecision for its own sake. The chorus has two real reasons pulling against each other, and both are legitimate. On one side stands Tiresias’s authority: he is “the master seer,” a man whose whole role in this city is to know things no one else can. On the other side stands Oedipus’s record, and the chorus states that reason just as plainly:

“But that a mortal seer knows more than I know, where hath this been proven? Or how, without sign assured, can I blame him who saved our State when the winged songstress came, tested and tried in the light of us all, like gold assayed?”
Chorus, trans. F. Storr, public domain (“the winged songstress” is the Sphinx)
⚖️ THE CHORUS, CAUGHT BETWEEN TWO CLAIMS
CLAIM ONE · THE PROPHET’S AUTHORITY
Tiresias speaks with a god-given gift for knowing what mortals cannot. His office alone gives his accusation real weight.
CLAIM TWO · THE KING’S RECORD
Oedipus solved the Sphinx’s riddle and saved Thebes when no one else could. A single accusation, unproven, does not erase that record.
Neither claim is foolish. The chorus is not confused; it is genuinely weighing two real, competing reasons to believe or doubt, which is exactly what an honest juror is supposed to do.
📚 PART 2 · YOUR FUTURE JURY

Hold onto this moment. In the Trial of Oedipus, you will argue a verdict in front of a council built to weigh both sides fairly, the way this chorus is trying to. A council like that will not be moved by volume or by picking the side that feels satisfying. It will be moved by whichever advocate shows the clearest, most honestly stated evidence, the kind that survives being looked at closely.

Notice, too, what the chorus does not do here. It does not pretend the prophet’s words do not exist, and it does not pretend Oedipus’s record does not exist. It holds both in mind at once and waits for “sign assured,” real proof, before it will condemn. That is the standard you will be held to as well.

⚡ TRY IT · READING THE CHORUS’S REASONING
Answer based on what the chorus actually says in its ode.
1. Why does the chorus refuse to take a side right away after Tiresias’s accusation?
2. What does the chorus’s standard (waiting for “sign assured” before it judges) suggest about how to win your future Trial of Oedipus argument?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

The chorus will not condemn its king on one accusation alone, and it will not dismiss the accusation either. It waits for “sign assured.” That is your future jury, and that is the standard your own evidence will have to meet.
