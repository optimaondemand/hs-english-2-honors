Why Does an Epic Begin in the Middle?
Lesson · Week 1
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
“Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.”
Those are the first words of a poem roughly three thousand years old. Before the story starts, the poet stops to ask a goddess for help remembering it. This year opens with the question that will run through all four of our works: what does a community owe its king, and what does a king owe his community? The Odyssey begins that conversation with a king who has been gone twenty years and a household that has fallen apart without him. Today you learn the tools the poem uses to tell that story: the conventions of epic.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify four conventions of epic poetry: the invocation, the epithet, in medias res, and the epic simile.
2
I will be able to explain how these conventions grew out of oral performance, when poems were sung from memory rather than read.
3
I will be able to read the poem’s opening for what it tells us about a king’s absence and a household in disorder.

📚 PART 1 · A POEM THAT WAS SUNG, NOT READ
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before we begin: when you hear the word hero, what comes to mind first? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

The poet asks for help

The Odyssey was not written to be read silently. For generations before it was ever written down, poets performed it aloud from memory, singing or chanting to a listening crowd at feasts and festivals. A performance could last for hours across many nights. That single fact explains almost everything strange about how the poem opens. When the poet begins, “Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero,” he is doing two things at once: he is praying to the goddess of memory and inspiration to carry him through an enormous act of recall, and he is announcing his subject in the very first line. The Muse is the poet’s source; the “ingenious hero” is the promise of what the audience is about to hear.

Because the poem lived in performance, it is built out of repeatable, memorable parts. Poets leaned on fixed phrases, familiar patterns, and set ways of opening and comparing. Scholars call these repeated building blocks the conventions of epic. They are not decoration. They were the working tools that made a poem this long possible to hold in a human memory and deliver to a live audience. When you learn to spot them, you are reading the way the poem was actually made.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
We speak of “Homer” as the author, but the Odyssey grew out of a long oral tradition in which many singers reshaped the same stories over centuries before the version we have was fixed in writing, likely in the 700s BCE. Our course uses the Greek names for everyone in the poem: the hero Odysseus, his wife Penelope, his son Telemachus, and the gods Zeus, Athena (his protector), and Poseidon (the sea-god who opposes him). One note for your reading: older public-domain translations sometimes use the gods’ Roman names, so a few short quotations on these pages come from a public-domain version and are given here with the Greek names restored. If you ever see Ulysses, Minerva, Jove, or Neptune elsewhere, those are Odysseus, Athena, Zeus, and Poseidon under Latin labels.
📚 PART 2 · FOUR CONVENTIONS OF EPIC

Four conventions do most of the work in these opening lines. Read the definitions, then study how the invocation itself shows two of them at once.

📝 THE INVOCATION, ANNOTATED
“Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.”
INVOCATION
The poet calls on the Muse for inspiration and memory before telling the tale. It marks the poem’s beginning and names its subject.
EPITHET
A fixed descriptive phrase attached to a person or thing (“ingenious hero”). Repeatable phrases like this helped a performing poet compose on the beat.

A note as you read your own copy: translators render Odysseus’s epithet differently. The public-domain version quoted above calls him the “ingenious hero”; others call him a man of many turns, or of many wiles. The original Greek word is polytropos, literally “of many turns.” However your edition phrases it, watch for it in the opening lines: cunning is his defining trait, and it is the first thing the poem tells you about him.

In medias res is Latin for “into the middle of things.” The Odyssey does not start at the beginning of its hero’s story. It opens years after the Trojan War, with the hero already stranded far from home and his household already in crisis. The earlier events reach us later, out of order, once we are hooked.

The epic simile (sometimes called a Homeric simile) is an extended comparison that runs for several lines, opening a small window onto everyday life before returning to the action. Where an ordinary simile is a quick flash (“brave as a lion”), an epic simile lingers: it might compare a warrior to a lion among the sheepfolds, describe the lion and the frightened flock in detail, and only then turn back to the fighter. You will meet these often once the voyage begins.

⚡ TRY IT · NAME THE CONVENTION
Each line below is a new example, written for this exercise, not a line from the poem. More than one convention will seem to fit at first glance. Choose the one the passage best demonstrates, and be ready to say why the others fall short.
1. “The runner broke from the pack like a hawk stooping on a hare, wings tucked, talons already reaching, indifferent to the hare’s last desperate zigzag, and did not look back until the finish line was behind her.”
2. “She answered him coldly, like ice.”
3. “Wherever the swift-footed messenger went, doors opened before he arrived.”
4. “The clever fox of a captain slipped through the harbor guards like smoke through a cracked door.”
📚 PART 3 · A HOUSE WITHOUT ITS KING

Watch what the poem shows us the moment the goddess arrives at the hero’s home in Book I. A crowd of suitors has moved into the palace, pressing the hero’s wife to remarry and consuming his wealth while they wait:

“There she found the lordly suitors seated on hides of the oxen which they had killed and eaten, and playing draughts in front of the house.”

Read that picture closely before you read on. The suitors sit on the hides of the host’s own cattle, which they have slaughtered and eaten, and they pass the day playing a board game in the doorway. In a world where a guest was sacred and a host’s generosity was a measure of honor, this is the image turned upside down: guests who devour the house instead of honoring it. The king’s absence has left more than an empty chair. It has left a community without order, living off a kingdom it is slowly draining. Hold onto that image. The whole poem drives toward the day the king returns and the house is set right, and it will keep asking you whether that setting-right is justice, vengeance, or both.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND THE CONVENTIONS
If you ask an AI tool to list the conventions of epic poetry, it will give you a fast, mostly accurate list. What it cannot do is tell you what this opening chooses to foreground, or why the poet shows us the suitors eating the king’s cattle before we ever meet the king. Naming a convention is recall; deciding what it means here is the reading only you can do. This stays true no matter how good the tools get.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A poem sung from memory taught its singers to open with a prayer, to tag its people with fixed phrases, to plunge into the middle of the story, and to pause for long comparisons drawn from ordinary life. Those tools are still doing their work in the first lines you read. And already the poem has shown you its real question: a house without its king, and a community that no longer knows how to behave. Keep your eye on that house. Everything returns to it.
