📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
You have argued the trial. Now ask what arguing it actually taught you that simply reading the play never could.
No new scenes this week. Instead, this lesson asks you to look back across the whole play with the question you have spent eight weeks refining: what does a community owe its king, and what does a king owe his community, when the king himself is the source of the sickness? You already know how Oedipus answers that question for himself. Today you look at how Creon answers it differently, and how the chorus refuses to answer it at all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what Oedipus’s self-sentencing actually establishes for a court, and what it leaves unsettled.
2
I will be able to contrast Creon’s measured rule with Oedipus’s earlier rage, and explain what the chorus’s closing warning adds to that contrast.
3
I will be able to state, in my own words, why a community might reasonably choose the deed-standard or the intent-standard, and what each choice costs.

📚 PART 1 · A SENTENCE PASSED BEFORE ANY TRIAL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Now that you have actually argued a verdict on Oedipus, has your own sense of what he deserves changed since Quick Write 9, your first verdict back in Week 1? (No wrong answer.)

What the self-sentencing actually settles

In Week 5 you read the moment Oedipus judges and punishes himself before Creon or any court in Thebes ever rules. The messenger reports it plainly:

“He tore the golden brooches that upheld her queenly robes, upraised them high and smote full on his eye-balls, uttering words like these: ‘No more shall ye behold such sights of woe, deeds I have suffered and myself have wrought.’”
the Second Messenger, reporting Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

He explains his own reasoning afterward, and it is worth rereading now that your brief is finished:

“For, had I sight, I know not with what eyes I could have met my father in the shades, or my poor mother, since against the twain I sinned, a sin no gallows could atone.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Notice what this reasoning is actually built on: not the bare fact that he killed a man and married a woman, but his own knowledge of who they were. A sentence a man passes on himself, using his own standard, is real evidence about him. It is not the same thing as an answer to the question your whole brief had to argue: which standard, deed or intent, a court, a community, or you, should actually judge by. Oedipus answering that question for himself does not answer it for Thebes.

📚 PART 2 · TWO KINDS OF AUTHORITY, SIDE BY SIDE

Set Oedipus’s rage against the man who takes charge of Thebes after him. Back in Week 2, Oedipus met a threat to his authority with fury, accusing Tiresias of conspiracy on nothing more than suspicion. Compare that to how Creon greets the blinded king at the play’s end:

“Not in derision, Oedipus, I come nor to upbraid thee with thy past misdeeds. (To bystanders) But shame upon you! If ye feel no sense of human decencies, at least revere the Sun whose light beholds and nurtures all.”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Creon has every reason to gloat; Oedipus once accused him of plotting his own murder. He does not. His one sharp word lands on the gawking crowd, not on the ruined king in front of him. That restraint is itself an answer to half of this module’s question: a king, or whoever holds power once a king has fallen, owes his community a kind of authority that does not need cruelty to be firm.

⚖️ RAGE, THEN MEASURE
OEDIPUS, WEEK 2
Accuses a loyal seer of conspiracy on suspicion alone; reaches for a plot to explain away evidence he does not want to hear.
CREON, WEEK 5
Takes charge of a ruined city, and directs his one rebuke at the crowd staring at a broken man, not at the man himself.

Now set both men beside the chorus’s final word, which you also close-read and, for some of you, recited, in Weeks 4 through 8:

“Look ye, countrymen and Thebans, this is Oedipus the great, he who knew the Sphinx’s riddle and was mightiest in our state. Who of all our townsmen gazed not on his fame with envious eyes? Now, in what a sea of troubles sunk and overwhelmed he lies! Therefore wait to see life’s ending ere thou count one mortal blest; wait till free from pain and sorrow he has gained his final rest.”
the Chorus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

The chorus does not choose a side in your trial. It steps back from Oedipus specifically and warns every citizen watching: the man Thebes once envied is the same man lying in ruin now, and fortune, not merit, decided the difference. That is not a verdict. It is a warning that the whole city, not just its king, lives closer to reversal than it likes to admit.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND THE TEMPTATION OF A CLEAN ANSWER
An AI tool can summarize both standards of judgment accurately, deed versus intent, and even list arguments for each. What it will tend to do, if you let it, is smooth the tension away and hand you a single tidy answer. This lesson is asking you to do the harder thing: hold both readings as genuinely live, and say honestly what each one costs a community that adopts it.
📚 PART 3 · THE QUESTION THAT OUTLIVES THE TRIAL

Here is the question your brief had to answer, restated one last time, without a play to hide behind: is a community’s health served better by judging its members on the deed alone, or on what they intended? Neither answer is free. A pure deed-standard protects a city from having to untangle every actor’s private mind, the way the plague itself cannot wait for a jury, but it risks punishing people, Jocasta included, for harms they never knowingly chose. A pure intent-standard protects the innocent-minded from a punishment they never deserved, but it asks a suffering city to trust that it can actually know what was in someone’s heart, which is a much harder thing to verify than a body in the road.

This lesson is not going to tell you which cost is worth paying. You already argued that in your brief, and you may still be right to have argued it the way you did. What this lesson asks is that you hold the other side’s cost in your mind honestly, one more time, before you deliver your closing argument this week.

What does Oedipus’s self-sentencing actually establish?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
A self-imposed sentence settling “the whole case” would make your entire brief unnecessary; your Council still had to weigh a standard, evidence, and a counterclaim, which a private act of self-punishment cannot do for them.
Treating severity of self-punishment as proof of guilt confuses two different questions: what a man believes about himself, and what actually happened. A wrongly guilt-ridden person could punish himself just as harshly.
The chorus’s warning is not about Oedipus’s guilt at all; it is a claim about fortune and every listener’s own exposure to reversal, which this scene does not overturn.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Oedipus sentenced himself before any court could. Creon governed with a measure Oedipus never found. The chorus refused to hand down a verdict at all, and warned every listener instead. None of that decides, for you, whether a community should judge the deed or the heart. That decision is still yours, and it is the one you are about to deliver aloud.
