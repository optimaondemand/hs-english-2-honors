Say It Faithfully, Then Weigh It
Lesson · Week 3 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Before you can argue about what a speech proves, you have to be able to say what it actually says.
An advocate who skips straight to arguing about a passage, without first restating it accurately, often ends up arguing with a version of the speech that never existed. Today you practice restating a passage faithfully in your own words before you analyze it. This sounds simple. Done honestly, it is one of the harder habits a writer builds, because it is tempting to shade a paraphrase toward whatever you already want it to prove.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to distinguish a faithful paraphrase from a slanted one.
2
I will be able to restate a passage accurately in my own words, then state separately what it proves.

▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.

📚 PART 1 · TWO PARAPHRASES OF THE SAME LINES

A faithful paraphrase restates what a passage says, in your own words, without adding a judgment the original did not make and without leaving out a detail that complicates your point. A slanted paraphrase quietly does one or both: it adds a verdict the original never stated, or it drops the part that would get in the way. The difference rarely shows up as an outright lie. It shows up as a shift in emphasis.

Look at Jocasta’s account of the exposed child, quoted directly from the play:

“As for the child, it was but three days old, / When Laius, its ankles pierced and pinned / Together, gave it to be cast away / By others on the trackless mountain side.”
Jocasta, trans. F. Storr, public domain
📝 TWO PARAPHRASES, WORKED EXAMPLE
FAITHFUL PARAPHRASE
When the baby was only three days old, Laius had its ankles pierced and tied together and had it left on an isolated mountainside to die.
Restates every detail Jocasta gives: the age, the pierced ankles, the mountain, and who ordered it. Adds no judgment beyond what the lines themselves state.
SLANTED PARAPHRASE
Laius cruelly murdered his own newborn son out of pure fear, proving he was a monstrous father.
Adds a verdict (“monstrous father”) the lines do not state, changes exposure into a claim of direct murder, and drops the detail that Laius was acting to stop a prophecy, information a fair reader needs before judging him.

Notice that the slanted version is not obviously false at first glance. It sounds confident and even persuasive. That is exactly the danger: a slanted paraphrase can feel like analysis while it is actually deciding the case before the evidence has been fairly stated.

📚 PART 2 · RESTATE, THEN WEIGH, IN TWO SEPARATE STEPS

Keep the two jobs separate. First, restate the passage faithfully. Only after that is done, in a clearly separate step, say what it proves and what it does not prove. Mixing the two steps together is how a paraphrase quietly becomes an argument in disguise.

Try this yourself now, before you look at one more worked example. Choose a short passage (three to five lines) from this week’s reading: Creon’s defense, Jocasta’s oracle story, or Oedipus’s Corinth story.

✍️ In your notebook, write a faithful paraphrase of your chosen passage, then, on a new line, write one sentence stating what it proves and one sentence stating what it does not prove. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

Passage: Creon argues that because he already shares in every benefit of royal power without carrying its burdens, he has no reason to want the throne itself.

Faithful paraphrase: Creon says he already enjoys the safety and influence a king has, without having to make the unpopular decisions a king must make, so trading that comfortable position for the crown itself would only cost him freedom.

What it proves: It gives Creon a real, logical reason to have no motive for the crime he is accused of.

What it does not prove: A lack of motive is not the same as proof of innocence. Someone can have no obvious motive and still be guilty; this argument makes the charge less likely, not impossible.

⚡ TRY IT · FAITHFUL OR SLANTED?
Each pair restates the same line from Oedipus’s Corinth story: “I struck him, and the old man… brought down / Full on my head the double-pointed goad. / Yet was I quits with him and more.” Decide which restatement is faithful.
1. Which restatement is faithful to the passage?
2. The herdsman originally told his story as: “Robbers, he told us, not one bandit but / A troop of knaves, attacked and murdered him.” Which restatement is faithful to this detail?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Restate first, honestly and completely. Weigh second, in a clearly separate step. Every argument you build for the rest of this module rests on that order, because a case built on a slanted paraphrase collapses the moment someone checks it against the actual text.
