📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Jocasta tells a story meant to prove prophecy false. It hands Oedipus the one detail he cannot ignore.
Today’s reading is the hinge of the whole play. Jocasta tries to calm Oedipus by telling him an old oracle about Laius never came true. Instead, one phrase in her story, a place where three roads meet, stops Oedipus cold, and he answers with a story of his own. Watch how much you, the audience, can see that neither character can yet see. That gap has a name: dramatic irony, when the audience knows more than a character on stage.

📚 PART 1 · JOCASTA’S STORY, MEANT TO REASSURE

Jocasta wants to prove to Oedipus that prophecy cannot be trusted, so she tells him about an old oracle given to her first husband, Laius:

“An oracle / Once came to Laius (I will not say / ‘Twas from the Delphic god himself, but from / His ministers) declaring he was doomed / To perish by the hand of his own son, / A child that should be born to him by me.”
Jocasta, trans. F. Storr, public domain

She explains that she and Laius made certain the prophecy could never come true:

“As for the child, it was but three days old, / When Laius, its ankles pierced and pinned / Together, gave it to be cast away / By others on the trackless mountain side.”
Jocasta, trans. F. Storr, public domain

To Jocasta, the story proves the oracle wrong twice over: the son was left to die on a mountainside, and Laius himself was killed years later a different way entirely, by strangers:

“Now Laius—so at least report affirmed— / Was murdered on a day by highwaymen, / No natives, at a spot where three roads meet.”
Jocasta, trans. F. Storr, public domain
📚 PART 2 · ONE PHRASE STOPS HIM

Jocasta means every word of this as comfort. It has the opposite effect. Oedipus reacts at once:

“What memories, what wild tumult of the soul / Came o’er me, lady, as I heard thee speak! […] Methought I heard thee say that Laius / Was murdered at the meeting of three roads.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain
📝 WHAT THE AUDIENCE SEES THAT THE SPEAKERS CANNOT
WHAT JOCASTA BELIEVES
She believes the story proves prophecy false and will calm her husband. She has no reason yet to connect “three roads” to anything Oedipus has done.
WHAT THE AUDIENCE ALREADY SUSPECTS
An audience that already suspects Oedipus killed Laius hears “three roads” land like a struck bell, long before either character says the word “father.”

This gap, where the audience holds knowledge the characters on stage do not yet have, is called dramatic irony. It is not a coincidence of plotting. The playwright builds every line of Jocasta’s reassurance so that it reads as comfort to her and as a countdown to us. Keep watching for it as the two stories in this lesson converge.

📚 PART 3 · OEDIPUS TELLS HIS OWN STORY

Oedipus answers Jocasta’s story with a story of his own, one he has never told her before. He grew up in Corinth believing he was the son of King Polybus and Queen Merope. At a banquet, a drunken guest called him no true son of his father. Unsettled, he went in secret to the oracle at Delphi, and heard something far worse than an answer about his parents:

“A roisterer at some banquet, flown with wine, / Shouted ‘Thou art not true son of thy sire.’ […] So privily without their leave I went / To Delphi, and Apollo sent me back / Baulked of the knowledge that I came to seek. / But other grievous things he prophesied, / Woes, lamentations, mourning, portents dire; / To wit I should defile my mother’s bed / And raise up seed too loathsome to behold, / And slay the father from whose loins I sprang.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

To escape that fate, Oedipus fled Corinth and never went home again. On the road, at a place he describes precisely, he ran into an old man’s carriage and a fight broke out:

“As I drew near the triple-branching roads, / A herald met me and a man who sat / In a car drawn by colts—as in thy tale— / The man in front and the old man himself / Threatened to thrust me rudely from the path […] I struck him, and the old man, seeing this, / Watched till I passed and from his car brought down / Full on my head the double-pointed goad. / Yet was I quits with him and more […] And so I slew them every one.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Two stories, told by two people who love each other and cannot see what the other has just handed them, have now landed on the same crossroads.

📚 PART 4 · ONE HOPE LEFT

There is one detail standing between Oedipus and certainty, and both he and Jocasta reach for it. The witness who survived the attack on Laius, a herdsman, reported it a specific way at the time:

“Robbers, he told us, not one bandit but / A troop of knaves, attacked and murdered him.”
trans. F. Storr, public domain

If the herdsman is remembered rightly, a band of robbers killed Laius, not one man alone, and Oedipus was alone that day at the crossroads. That single discrepancy, one attacker against many, is the last thread the truth might still hang from. Oedipus orders the herdsman found:

“Fetch him at once. I fain would see the man.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

As an advocate building a case, notice what this scene teaches you about evidence: a single surviving witness, and a single word, plural instead of singular, is now the whole hinge of the case. That is how much can rest on one detail, remembered correctly or not.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES IT SHOW?

1. Jocasta mentions “three roads” while trying to prove prophecy false. What does this moment in the play primarily illustrate?
2. The herdsman originally reported that “robbers,” not one man, killed Laius. Why does this detail matter so much to the case?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Jocasta reaches for comfort and hands her husband a countdown. Oedipus reaches for proof of his own innocence and instead finds himself standing at the same crossroads twice. Only one witness, and one word, still stands between them and the truth. Hold onto that image, one detail carrying the whole weight of a case, for your own work as an advocate.
