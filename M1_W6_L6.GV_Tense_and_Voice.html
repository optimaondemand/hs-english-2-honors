📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Two choices a writer makes in almost every sentence: when it happens, and who does it.
A turning-point scene loses its force fast if the verbs wander between past and present, or if the sentence hides who is actually doing the acting. This lesson covers both: keeping your tense consistent across a scene, and choosing active voice over passive on purpose, especially at the moment your story’s stakes are highest. You will apply both, along with this week’s Word Study words, directly in Quick Write 6.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to catch and fix a verb tense that shifts without reason inside a scene.
2
I will be able to tell active voice from passive voice, and choose active voice deliberately at a moment of high stakes.
3
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · KEEPING YOUR TENSE STEADY

Once you choose a tense for a scene, usually past tense for a narrative, every verb describing that scene’s action should stay in it, unless you have a real reason to shift (like a character remembering something earlier, or looking ahead to what is still to come). An unintentional tense shift, where a sentence starts in the past and slides into the present with no reason, is one of the fastest ways to make controlled, confident prose start to feel careless.

Compare the two versions below, both describing the same moment from Book 12. The verb tense is marked in each.

✎ ON THE BOARD · TENSE AND VOICE
The crew grew desperate with hunger, so they slaughtered the sacred cattle while Odysseus sleeps.
Inconsistent: “grew” and “slaughtered” are past tense, but “sleeps” slides into present tense for no reason. The tense wanders mid-sentence.
The crew grew desperate with hunger, so they slaughtered the sacred cattle while Odysseus slept.
Consistent: every verb in the sentence holds the same past tense. The fix is often this simple, one verb corrected to match the rest.
The sacred cattle were slaughtered by the starving crew.
Passive voice: the subject of the sentence (the cattle) receives the action instead of doing it. Who actually acted is pushed to the end of the sentence, almost as an afterthought.
The starving crew slaughtered the sacred cattle.
Active voice: the subject (the crew) does the action directly. At a moment of high stakes, this version puts the responsibility, and the drama, exactly where it belongs.

Neither tense nor voice is about a rigid rule you must follow every time. Passive voice is sometimes the right choice, for instance when the doer of an action truly is not the point. But a writer who chooses passive voice by accident, especially at a story’s highest-stakes moment, often ends up burying exactly the sentence that should hit hardest. At your turning point, put the person making the choice in the driver’s seat of the sentence, the same way the crew, not the cattle, belongs at the front of that last sentence above.

⚡ TRY IT · TENSE, VOICE, OR NEITHER?
Read each example and identify the issue described. One of these hides its answer better than it looks.
1. “Circe warns Odysseus about the Sirens before the ship sailed, and the crew bound him to the mast.” What is the problem with this sentence?
2. “The bag of winds got opened while Odysseus slept.” Is this sentence active or passive?
3. “Odysseus bound himself to the mast and ordered his men to row past the island. The Sirens’ voices reach him first, low and sweet over the water. He strains against the ropes, desperate to answer them.” What is the problem with this passage?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 11 and 12. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

seer (noun) — a person believed able to see and foretell the future
Tiresias is described this way in Book 11, the only one among the dead whose mind still reasons clearly enough to be consulted.
phantom (noun) — a ghost or a faint, dreamlike image of something not really there
The word used for the ghosts of the dead in Book 11, thin and hard to hold onto, the way Anticlea slips from Odysseus’s embrace three times.
forewarn (verb) — to warn someone in advance of a coming danger
The exact word Tiresias uses about the cattle of Helios, which is what makes the crew’s choice in Book 12 a broken warning rather than an ordinary accident.
famine (noun) — extreme, prolonged hunger; a severe shortage of food
The pressure a crew member names as worse than any risk, right before persuading the others to kill the sacred cattle.
prodigious (adjective) — enormous, remarkable in size or degree
Used to describe the length of Scylla’s six necks, a detail that makes the monster’s threat concrete rather than abstract.
invincible (adjective) — impossible to defeat or overcome
The word used for Scylla herself, which is exactly why Circe’s advice is not to fight her but to accept a smaller, chosen loss instead.
