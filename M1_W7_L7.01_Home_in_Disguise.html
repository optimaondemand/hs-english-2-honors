📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Odysseus finally reaches Ithaca, and the poem will not let him arrive as himself.
After ten years and six books of storytelling, Odysseus sets foot on his own island in Book 13, asleep and unaware, carried there by the Phaeacians who then sail off without ever seeing him wake. Athena greets him not with a triumphant homecoming but with a disguise: she turns her own favorite hero into an old, ragged beggar nobody would look at twice. In Book 14, that disguised stranger walks straight into the hut of Eumaeus, the swineherd who has kept faith with an absent master for twenty years. Watch what Eumaeus does for a man he does not recognize, and ask why the poem makes its king hide before it lets him rule again.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe how and why Athena disguises Odysseus as a beggar before he ever reaches his own house.
2
I will be able to explain how Eumaeus’s hospitality toward a stranger contrasts with the suitors’ treatment of Odysseus’s own household.
3
I will be able to identify what a disguise lets a story reveal that a plain arrival could not.

📚 PART 1 · A KING MADE INTO A BEGGAR
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you showed up somewhere and no one recognized you right away, or a time you deliberately kept quiet about who you were. What did you learn about how people treated a stranger versus how they treated you? (No wrong answer.)

The Phaeacians set the sleeping Odysseus on the shore of Ithaca and sail home, so that when he wakes he does not even recognize his own island; Athena has covered it in a mist. Once she reveals herself and the two of them agree on a plan against the suitors, she transforms him on the spot:

“She covered him with wrinkles, took away all his yellow hair, and withered the flesh over his whole body… she changed his clothes and threw an old rag of a wrap about him, and a tunic, tattered, filthy, and begrimed with smoke.”
Athena, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
In this culture, hospitality toward strangers was treated as a sacred duty owed to any traveler, since the gods themselves were said to sometimes visit in disguise. A household’s true character showed most clearly in how it treated someone who could offer nothing in return. Odysseus does not choose disguise to trick his friends for sport; it lets him find out, without warning anyone, exactly who has kept faith with him and who has not.
📚 PART 2 · THE SWINEHERD WHO NEVER STOPPED WAITING

Eumaeus does not know the ragged old man at his door is his own king. He takes him in anyway, feeds him, and makes him a bed by the fire:

📝 EUMAEUS’S WORDS, AND WHAT THEY REVEAL
“Old man, come inside, and when you have had your fill of bread and wine, tell me where you come from. I have lost the best of masters, and am in continual grief on his account… it is the loss of Odysseus that grieves me most.”
Eumaeus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
WELCOME BEFORE ANY QUESTION
Eumaeus feeds a total stranger before he has any idea who the man is or whether he can repay the kindness. He does this out of habit, not calculation.
LOYALTY WITH NO AUDIENCE
Eumaeus grieves for Odysseus to a man he thinks is a nobody, with nothing to gain by saying it. That is the surest sign his loyalty is real.

Set that beside how Eumaeus describes the suitors, still speaking to the man he does not know is his king: “The fat pigs have to go to the suitors, who eat them up without shame or scruple… they waste his estate by force, without fear or stint.” One household’s poorest servant treats a stranger better than its wealthiest guests treat their absent host. The disguise does not create this contrast; it simply lets Odysseus, and the reader, see it clearly for the first time.

📚 PART 3 · TRY IT

Sort what you have read so far into what disguise reveals about each character in Book 14.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE DISGUISE REVEAL?
Each question offers four readings of a moment in Book 14. More than one will sound reasonable; choose the one the scene best supports.
1. Eumaeus, who owns little beyond the hut and pigs he tends for another man, still feeds and shelters the ragged stranger before asking his name. What is the strongest reading of what this scene asks you to weigh?
2. Eumaeus tells the disguised beggar, a man he believes to be a homeless stranger, that he grieves more for the loss of Odysseus than for anything else. What does the poem most want you to take from a servant confessing this to someone who cannot reward him for it?
3. Even safely inside a loyal household, Odysseus keeps the disguise instead of telling Eumaeus the truth. What is the best-supported reason for this choice, based on what the scene shows?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A disguise strips away rank and reputation and leaves only how people actually treat someone who seems to be nobody. Eumaeus passes that test without knowing he is being tested at all, which is exactly what makes his loyalty worth something.
