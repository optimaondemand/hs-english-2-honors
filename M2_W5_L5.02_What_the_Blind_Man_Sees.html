📚 PART 1 · THE MESSENGER’S REPORT

Once Oedipus understands the truth, he rushes into the palace. What happens there is never shown to the audience. A messenger comes out and reports it to the chorus instead, which is the standard practice of Greek tragedy: violence happens offstage, and the audience receives it through a witness’s controlled, grieving account. First, Jocasta:

“When in her frenzy she had passed inside the vestibule, she hurried straight to win the bridal-chamber, clutching at her hair with both her hands, and, once within the room, she shut the doors behind her with a crash.”
the Second Messenger, trans. F. Storr, public domain

She has understood the truth before Oedipus does, and she cannot bear it. The messenger reports what is found behind that shut door:

“Then we beheld the woman hanging there, a running noose entwined about her neck.”
the Second Messenger, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Jocasta’s death is reported in a single, plain sentence. The play does not linger on it, and neither should you. Its weight comes from what it means, not from any description of the act itself.

📚 PART 2 · A PUNISHMENT CHOSEN, NOT IMPOSED

Oedipus finds her body himself. The messenger reports what Oedipus does next, and what he says while he does it:

“He tore the golden brooches that upheld her queenly robes, upraised them high and smote full on his eye-balls, uttering words like these: ‘No more shall ye behold such sights of woe, deeds I have suffered and myself have wrought.’”
the Second Messenger, reporting Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

No one orders this. No court has convened, no verdict has been read, and Creon has not yet even entered. Oedipus judges his own case and carries out his own sentence, alone, in the space of a single scene. When he is questioned about it afterward, he explains his reasoning plainly:

“For, had I sight, I know not with what eyes I could have met my father in the shades, or my poor mother, since against the twain I sinned, a sin no gallows could atone.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Read that reasoning carefully. He does not say the blinding will restore his innocence, or that it repays a debt. He says explicitly that no ordinary punishment, not even execution, could answer what he did. He chooses a punishment matched, in his own mind, to a crime that has no proper penalty.

⚖️ A PUNISHMENT IMPOSED, VERSUS A PUNISHMENT CHOSEN
HOW A TRIAL USUALLY WORKS
An accusation is made. Evidence is weighed by someone other than the accused. A verdict is reached. Only then is a sentence imposed, by a court, on the person judged.
WHAT OEDIPUS ACTUALLY DOES
He hears the evidence, reaches his own verdict in a single cry, and carries out his own sentence, blinding and a plea for exile, before any court, including Creon, has ruled on anything.

Keep that gap in mind. Whatever verdict you plan to argue in your brief, you are arguing it for a defendant who has already judged and sentenced himself. That is a genuine complication for an advocate, not a detail to skip past.

📚 PART 3 · CREON, THE DAUGHTERS, AND THE CLOSING CHORUS

The blinded Oedipus enters, and Creon, now the only figure left with any real authority in Thebes, arrives to take charge. His first words are careful, neither cruel to Oedipus nor indulgent toward the crowd gathering to stare:

“Not in derision, Oedipus, I come nor to upbraid thee with thy past misdeeds. (To bystanders) But shame upon you! If ye feel no sense of human decencies, at least revere the Sun whose light beholds and nurtures all.”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Oedipus, for his part, does not ask for mercy. He asks for exile, in terms that go well beyond simple banishment:

“Forth from thy borders thrust me with all speed; set me within some vasty desert where no mortal voice shall greet me any more.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

His one remaining request is not for himself. It is for his daughters:

“But for my daughters twain, poor innocent maids, who ever sat beside me at the board sharing my viands, drinking of my cup, for them, I pray thee, care.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

The play ends with the chorus addressing the audience directly, over Oedipus’s ruin:

“Look ye, countrymen and Thebans, this is Oedipus the great, he who knew the Sphinx’s riddle and was mightiest in our state. Who of all our townsmen gazed not on his fame with envious eyes? Now, in what a sea of troubles sunk and overwhelmed he lies! Therefore wait to see life’s ending ere thou count one mortal blest; wait till free from pain and sorrow he has gained his final rest.”
the Chorus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

The chorus does not pronounce a verdict on Oedipus at all. It steps back from judging him specifically and offers a warning to everyone watching instead: no life can be called happy until it is over. That refusal to hand down a final verdict is itself worth noticing, given the trial you are about to argue.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND TONE
An AI tool can summarize this ending accurately. What it will not reliably do is match the restraint the play itself uses: Sophocles never describes the deaths directly, and neither should your own writing about them. If you ever use AI to help you summarize this scene, check that its language keeps the same gravity the original does; sensational retellings misrepresent the source.
