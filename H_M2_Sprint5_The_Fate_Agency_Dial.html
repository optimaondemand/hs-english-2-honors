🎮 DESIGN FOCUS
What does your game leave to chance, and what does it hand to the player?
The Designer’s Table · Q2 Systems & Balance · feeds your prototype v1
Thebes runs on a mix of forces its citizens cannot fully separate: a plague no one chose, a prophecy laid down before Oedipus was born, and a king who nonetheless keeps investigating, deciding, and acting his way toward an answer. Your own game runs on a similar mix, whether you have named it yet or not. This sprint asks you to find every place your game leaves an outcome to chance and every place it hands the outcome to a player’s decision, and then to decide, on purpose, where the balance between the two should sit.
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
Inventory every element of your game as either determined (dice, cards, a fixed event) or chosen (a decision, a trade, a choice of timing). Decide deliberately where your game’s fate/agency dial should sit, and explain why that ratio actually carries your design question. Build prototype v1 to reflect that decision. Then have an AI tool simulate a three-turn playthrough from your written rules alone, and check its simulation against what your rules actually say.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
If a stranger played your game ten times, how much of the outcome would come from what they decided, and how much from what the dice or the deck decided for them, and does that ratio actually match the question your game is asking?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Inventory the whole game. List every mechanic in your current design and sort each one into one of two columns: determined (a die roll, a drawn card, a fixed event that happens regardless of player choice) or chosen (a decision, a trade, a choice of timing or target). If a mechanic mixes both, note which part is which.
2
Set your dial, and defend it. Looking at your two columns, decide deliberately where your game’s balance between chance and choice should sit. Write a short paragraph explaining why that particular ratio, not a heavier or lighter one, actually carries your design question. A game about a world where nothing anyone does matters needs a very different dial than a game about a world where a single decision can cost everything.
3
Build prototype v1. Revise or rebuild your prototype so it actually reflects the dial you just set. Photograph it, or describe it in enough detail that a reader could picture exactly how it plays.
4
Have AI simulate a three-turn playthrough. Give an AI tool your written rules exactly as they stand and ask it to play three turns, showing its reasoning at each turn.
5
Verify the simulation against your actual rules. Compare what the AI did, turn by turn, against what your written rules actually say. Where the AI got something wrong or made something up, do not just correct it. Ask yourself whether your rules were clear enough to prevent that mistake in the first place. A place where AI goes wrong is very often a place where your rules are ambiguous, and this is one of the fastest ways to find those gaps before a human tester ever sits down.
6
Log your AI use. Add one complete row to your AI-Use Log: what you asked the AI to simulate, what it did turn by turn, where its play diverged from your rules, and what you changed or clarified as a result.
⚠️ AI-USE REMINDER
An AI simulation cannot tell you whether your dial is set correctly. It can only show you where your written rules failed to constrain its play the way you intended. Treat every divergence as a clarity problem in your rules, not as evidence about whether your design choice itself was good.
