A Beggar in His Own Hall
Lesson · Week 8 · Books 17–18
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The king of Ithaca walks into his own hall, and not one person there knows him.
Disguised as an old beggar, Odysseus enters the palace he has not seen in twenty years and stands in the middle of the men eating his household out of house and home. Every insult he absorbs and every kindness he receives happens because no one recognizes him, which is exactly the tension this lesson asks you to notice. The reader knows something almost every character in the room does not, and Homer stretches that gap out, scene after scene, before he ever lets it snap.

2

📚 PART 1 · ANTINOUS AND THE STOOL

Odysseus enters his own hall as a ragged old beggar and asks for alms, the same way any traveler might. Most of the suitors give grudgingly. Antinous does not. When Odysseus presses his case, Antinous turns on him:

“What god can have sent such a pestilence to plague us during our dinner? Get out, into the open part of the court, or I will give you Egypt and Cyprus over again for your insolence.”
Antinous, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Then, without warning, Antinous acts on the threat:

“With these words he threw a footstool at him, and hit him on the right shoulder blade near the top of his back.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

A man just struck his own king with a piece of furniture and does not know it. That is dramatic irony at its sharpest: the reader flinches at what Antinous is actually doing, while Antinous himself feels nothing but irritation at a stranger. The poem does not rush past this moment or explain it. It lets the insult sit there, plainly, and trusts the reader to feel the weight Antinous cannot.

👁️ WHO KNOWS WHAT, IN THIS SCENE
The reader
Knows the beggar is Odysseus, the rightful king, and feels every insult land twice as hard.
Antinous and the suitors
See only a nuisance in rags. Their cruelty is careless because they believe there is no cost to it.
Odysseus himself
Knows exactly who he is and chooses to absorb the insult without breaking his disguise, a test of his own self-control.
📚 PART 2 · THE FIGHT WITH IRUS

A local beggar named Irus, who is used to working the hall for scraps, sees Odysseus as competition and orders him off the doorstep:

“Be off, old man, from the doorway, or you shall be dragged out neck and heels.”
Irus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

The suitors, delighted at the prospect of a spectacle, egg the two beggars into a fistfight. Odysseus, still holding back his full strength, lands a single blow that ends it instantly. He could have killed every man in that hall already, and the suitors have no idea how close they are standing to that fact. The scene plays as comedy on the surface: a scuffle between two ragged old men. Underneath, it is a warning the suitors cannot read.

📚 PART 3 · ARGOS

Before any of this, as Odysseus first approaches his own gates, an old hunting dog lying neglected on a heap of manure lifts his head. This is Argos, the dog Odysseus raised and trained himself before he ever sailed for Troy. No servant, no suitor, no member of the household has recognized the king. The dog does, instantly:

“As soon as he saw Odysseus standing there, he dropped his ears and wagged his tail, but he could not get close up to his master.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Odysseus has to hide even this. He wipes away a tear without letting Eumaeus see it, because grief for a dog would give him away just as surely as grief for anything else. Then, having accomplished the one thing he was waiting twenty years to do, Argos dies. Homer places this scene at the very threshold of the palace, before a single human inside recognizes the king. Notice what that placement does: it tells the reader, before the suitors’ abuse even begins, exactly how much loyalty and how much time have been lost, and it raises the emotional stakes of everything that follows in this hall.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
An audience of Homer’s day would have known hospitality, xenia, as a sacred obligation owed to every stranger, rich or poor. Antinous’s stool-throwing was not merely rude by their standards; it was an offense against the gods, who were believed to sometimes travel disguised as beggars to test a household’s decency. A listener would have felt the danger in Antinous’s cruelty even before learning who the beggar truly was.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND STORY STRUCTURE
An AI tool can quickly summarize what happens to Argos or list the insults the suitors throw at the beggar. It is far less reliable at explaining why a writer places a scene exactly where it is, since that judgment depends on reading the whole surrounding structure, not just isolated facts. If you use an AI tool to check a plot detail, verify it against your own reading before treating any claim about the story’s structure or meaning as settled.
⚡ TRY IT · WHAT THE SCENE IS DOING
Each question offers four readings of a moment in Books 17–18. More than one will sound reasonable; choose the one the text best supports.
1. Antinous throws a footstool and strikes his own disguised king, without any idea what he has just done. What effect does this moment create?
2. Homer places Argos’s recognition of Odysseus, and his death, at the very threshold of the palace, before any human inside recognizes the king. What is the strongest reading of why the scene sits exactly there?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A disguise only works because it hides something the audience already knows, and that gap is where tension lives. Antinous’s stool, the fight with Irus, and Argos’s death all draw their power from the same source: we can see the king where the household sees only a beggar. Carry this technique, tension built from what a reader knows and a character does not, into your own draft.
