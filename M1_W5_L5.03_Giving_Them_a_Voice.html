Giving Them a Voice
Lesson · Week 5 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A character does not have to be described as clever or crude. Give them the right words to say, and the reader figures it out.
This week you have watched Odysseus become the narrator of his own story, and you have heard Polyphemus, Aeolus, and Circe each speak in their own way. Today you study how the specific words a character says, and the way they say them, reveal who that character is, without a narrator ever needing to explain it. Then you try it yourself, before Quick Write 5 asks you to do it for real.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify what a line of dialogue reveals about a character’s state of mind or personality, beyond its literal meaning.
2
I will be able to draft dialogue for my own characters that reveals who they are through word choice, rather than through direct explanation.
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Ten quiet minutes to draft a short exchange of your own.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT DIALOGUE CAN DO THAT DESCRIPTION CANNOT

Compare two ways of introducing the same character. A writer can simply state a trait: “She was suspicious of strangers.” Or a writer can let the character speak, and trust the reader to notice the suspicion for themselves. The second method almost always lands harder, because a reader who figures something out feels smarter and more involved than a reader who is simply told. Dialogue works this way whenever the words a character chooses, the questions they ask or refuse to ask, and even what they leave unsaid, do the characterizing instead of the narrator.

Study the worked example below. It was written for this lesson, not lifted from any published book, so you can take it apart freely.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, ANNOTATED
“You’re not from the harbor,” the woman said, not looking up from her nets. “I know everyone who works these docks, and you’re not one of them.”

“I could be new,” he said.

“You could be.” She still hadn’t looked at him. “But new men ask where to find work. You asked where to find the harbor-master’s house. That’s a different question.”
CONTROL, NOT DESCRIPTION
Her clipped, declarative lines and her refusal to look up show a woman entirely in control of the conversation. No sentence ever says “she was suspicious,” yet the suspicion is obvious.
THE DETAIL THAT GIVES HIM AWAY
She never accuses him of anything. She simply repeats his own question back to him, and lets the reader notice what it reveals: he is not who he claims to be.

Notice that nobody in this exchange announces what they are feeling. Every clue comes from the specific words chosen and the specific words withheld. That is the target for your own dialogue: let word choice do the work a label would otherwise have to do.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Think of two characters in a moment of tension: one who is hiding something, and one who is starting to suspect it. It does not need to relate to the Odyssey. Pick a scene you can hear clearly in your head.

✍️ In your notebook, write four to six lines of dialogue between your two characters, in which neither says exactly what they mean, but a reader can tell what each one is really thinking. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

“Long trip?” the guard asked, eyes on the bag more than the face.
“Not too long,” Dae said.
“Funny. Most people answer that with a number.”

Compare it to your own draft. Does any line of your dialogue give away more than the character intends? Does a reader get to notice the tension themselves, or does a stray line explain it outright? If your dialogue explains too much, try cutting the explanatory line and trusting the exchange itself.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE VOICE REVEAL?
For each line of dialogue from this week’s reading, choose what it most reveals about the speaker.
1. Polyphemus: “Then I will eat all Noman’s comrades before Noman himself, and will keep Noman for the last.”
2. Odysseus: “My name is Noman; this is what my father and mother and my friends have always called me.”
3. Odysseus, from the ship: “say it was the valiant warrior Odysseus, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca.”
