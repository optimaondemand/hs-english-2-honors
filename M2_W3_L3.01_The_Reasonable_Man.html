📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The most reasonable speech in the play so far belongs to the man on trial, not the king questioning him.
After Tiresias named Oedipus as the source of Thebes’s plague, Oedipus concluded that Creon put the old prophet up to it, hoping to seize the throne for himself. Today Creon answers that charge directly, and you will study his answer the way an advocate studies any argument: not by asking whether you like the speaker, but by asking whether the reasoning holds. This is the same test you will apply to every piece of evidence in your own brief.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the parts of a formal argument: a claim, the reasons that support it, and an appeal to probability or character.
2
I will be able to explain why Creon’s defense counts as a stronger argument than Oedipus’s accusation, even though Oedipus is the king.
3
I will be able to summarize how Jocasta ends the quarrel between the two men.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT AN ARGUMENT NEEDS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time someone accused you of something you did not do. What made your defense convincing, or what made it fall flat? (No wrong answer.)

Claim, reason, and an appeal to probability

A formal argument has moving parts. A claim is the position being argued: I am innocent. A reason is evidence or logic offered to support that claim. An appeal to probability asks the listener to judge which explanation is more likely, given everything else known about the situation, rather than simply asserting innocence and hoping to be believed. A strong advocate uses all three, in order, instead of relying on emotion or on the force of their own position.

Creon is accused of plotting with Tiresias to take the throne. Rather than simply denying it, he reasons his way through the accusation. Consider his central claim, quoted directly from the play:

“Now all my needs are satisfied through thee, / And I have naught to fear; but were I king, / My acts would oft run counter to my will. / How could a title then have charms for me / Above the sweets of boundless influence?”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Notice the shape of the reasoning. Creon’s claim is that he has no motive to seize the crown. His reason is that he already shares in every benefit of royal power, safety, comfort, respect, without carrying any of the burdens a king must carry: the unpopular decisions, the blame when things go wrong, the constant demands on his will. His appeal to probability follows directly: which is more likely, that a man would trade a comfortable share of power for the exhausting whole of it? He sums up the point in a single memorable line:

“I am not so infatuate as to grasp / The shadow when I hold the substance fast.”
Creon, trans. F. Storr, public domain
📝 THE ANATOMY OF CREON’S ARGUMENT
Now all my needs are satisfied through thee, / And I have naught to fear; but were I king, / My acts would oft run counter to my will. How could a title then have charms for me / Above the sweets of boundless influence?
THE REASON
He already has everything a king has, without the danger. This is offered as fact, something the audience already knows to be true of his position.
THE APPEAL TO PROBABILITY
Kingship would only cost him freedom he currently enjoys. He is asking Oedipus to judge which choice is more likely for a rational man.
📚 PART 2 · AN ACCUSATION IS NOT AN ARGUMENT

Now set Oedipus’s response beside it. Oedipus does not answer Creon’s reasoning with reasoning of his own. He answers with certainty about Creon’s character, unsupported by any new evidence:

“Thou art glib of tongue, but I am slow to learn / Of thee; I know too well thy venomous hate.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

This is worth sitting with as a writer building your own brief. Oedipus dismisses Creon’s reasoning as mere cleverness, “glib of tongue,” and replaces it with his own certainty about Creon’s hatred. He never actually answers the claim that Creon has no motive. A confident tone is not the same thing as a supported claim, and an advocate who confuses the two leaves the strongest argument unanswered on the table. That gap is exactly what an opposing advocate will exploit.

The quarrel escalates until Jocasta, Oedipus’s wife and Creon’s sister, comes out of the palace and physically parts the two men, demanding to know why they are shouting in a city already suffering from plague. She sends Creon away and turns to calm her husband, which is where next lesson’s reading begins. Notice what she does not yet know: she is about to try to soothe Oedipus with a story that will do the opposite.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Creon builds a claim, gives it a reason, and closes with an appeal to probability. Oedipus answers with anger and an unsupported judgment of character. As you build your own brief in the weeks ahead, that contrast is the standard to measure yourself against: does every claim you make carry an actual reason underneath it, or only your certainty that you are right?
