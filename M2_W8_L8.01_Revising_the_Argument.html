Lesson 8.01
Revising the Argument
Lesson · Week 8 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A finished-looking sentence is not the same as a finished argument. Today you learn to tell the difference in your own draft.
You have a full draft of your brief from Quick Write 15. Most students, left alone with a draft, fix typos and swap a few words for fancier ones and call it revised. That is not what a real advocate does before facing a Council. Today you run your own draft through three deliberate passes: does the logic actually hold, is every piece of evidence actually analyzed, and does the tone sound like someone the Council should trust. This is exactly the same discipline behind the recitation you are polishing and submitting this week: saying something so that it holds up under real scrutiny, not just so that it sounds finished.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to check whether each argument’s reasoning actually connects its evidence to its claim, and whether my Response actually answers my Counterclaim.
2
I will be able to find any quotation in my draft that is dropped in without analysis, and revise it so the quote’s wording actually does work.
3
I will be able to revise sneering or dismissive language into a confident, formal tone without softening my actual position.

📚 PART 1 · THE LOGIC PASS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of an argument you have heard that sounded confident but, when you actually thought about it, did not really prove what it claimed. What tipped you off? (No wrong answer.)

What the logic pass actually checks

The logic pass asks two blunt questions of every paragraph in your brief: does this argument’s evidence actually support the size of claim I am making with it, and does my Response actually answer my Counterclaim, or does it just change the subject back to my own side? A Response that restates your verdict instead of engaging the counterclaim’s real premise is the single most common logic failure in a brief like this, because it feels like an answer without doing an answer’s job.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE · DOES THE RESPONSE ACTUALLY ANSWER THE COUNTERCLAIM?
COUNTERCLAIM (STATED FAIRLY)
“Oedipus acted entirely without knowledge of Laius’s identity, and ignorance of this kind should excuse a killing that any reasonable person could have committed in his position.”
BEFORE · RESTATES, DOES NOT ANSWER
“This is wrong. Oedipus is guilty, and the city suffered terribly under the curse he brought on it.”
Notice what this does not do: it never engages the counterclaim’s actual premise (that ignorance should excuse the act). It simply reasserts the verdict and changes the subject to the city’s suffering, which the counterclaim never disputed.
AFTER · ENGAGES THE PREMISE DIRECTLY
“Ignorance excuses an act only when nothing warned the actor to look closer. Oedipus received a direct oracle naming this exact danger before he ever left Corinth; rather than exercising more caution at an unfamiliar crossroads, he answered a blow with escalating force, by his own account ‘quits with him and more.’ The warning he already carried, not the identity he lacked, is what the standard should weigh.”
This version takes the counterclaim’s own premise (ignorance excuses) and shows exactly why it fails on these specific facts, rather than talking past it.

Run this test on your own Response section right now: cover up everything except your Counterclaim and your Response. Read only those two. If your Response could be swapped in after almost any counterclaim without changing a word, it is not actually answering this one. Revise it until it only makes sense as an answer to the specific objection you stated.

📚 PART 2 · THE EVIDENCE PASS

The evidence pass checks a narrower thing: is every quotation in your brief actually analyzed, or does it just sit on the page doing nothing? A dropped quote, one followed only by a bare claim like “this shows he was violent,” asks the reader to do the analytical work you were supposed to do yourself.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE · IS THE QUOTE ANALYZED, OR JUST DROPPED?
BEFORE · QUOTE DROPPED, NOT ANALYZED
“Oedipus’s temper is part of the problem. ‘Then jostled by the charioteer in wrath I struck him… yet was I quits with him and more.’ This shows he was violent.”
The quote (Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain) sits there. “This shows he was violent” never explains what in the wording actually proves it.
AFTER · THE WORDING ITSELF IS ANALYZED
“Oedipus does not describe a single defensive blow. By his own account he answers the old man’s strike and then goes further, ‘quits with him and more,’ language that frames the retaliation as exceeding what defense alone required. That excess does not prove premeditation, but it does complicate any claim that the killing was purely reactive.”
Notice the move: the analysis points at a specific phrase (“and more”) and explains exactly what work it does for the argument.

Go through your own draft and put a mark next to every quotation. For each one, check whether the sentence right after it names something specific in the wording, a word choice, a sequence, an omission, rather than just asserting what the quote supposedly proves. If a quote has no such sentence, that is not evidence yet; it is decoration.

📚 PART 3 · THE TONE PASS

An advocate who mocks the other side loses the bench, even when the underlying argument is sound. The tone pass is not about softening your verdict. It is about cutting the sneer, the phrases that dismiss an opposing view instead of defeating it, while keeping every bit of your actual confidence and precision.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE · CONFIDENT VS. SNEERING
BEFORE · SNEERING
“Only someone who has not actually read the play carefully could still believe Oedipus deserves mercy; that reading is embarrassing and does not deserve a serious response.”
Notice what this sentence never does: it never actually engages the mercy argument. It attacks the person who might hold it instead.
AFTER · CONFIDENT, STILL DIRECT
“The case for mercy rests on a real and appealing premise: that ignorance should excuse the act entirely. That premise does not survive contact with the standard this brief has committed to, which asks not what Oedipus knew, but what he did and how the city must answer for it.”
Same conclusion, same confidence, and the same firm rejection of the opposing view. The difference is that this version defeats the argument instead of insulting the person who might make it.
✍️ TRY IT YOURSELF, IN YOUR NOTEBOOK, BEFORE YOU LOOK AT THE MODEL BELOW
Rewrite this sneering sentence so it keeps its full confidence and its firm rejection of the opposing view, but cuts the insult: “Anyone who thinks Creon had a real motive to conspire clearly was not paying attention to the play.” Write your revision in your notebook first.
Compare your work: a model version (open only after you have written your own)
A model revision: “The theory that Creon had a real motive to conspire does not hold up against his own words: he names, specifically, why the crown would cost him more freedom than it would ever hand him. A motive this thin cannot carry the weight a conspiracy charge requires.” Notice the rejection is just as firm; it simply argues the point instead of mocking whoever might raise it.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND REVISION
An AI tool can flag wordy sentences and suggest a calmer tone in seconds. What it cannot do is verify, on its own, whether your Response actually answers your specific Counterclaim, or whether a quote you cite actually supports the size of claim you are making with it. Those two checks require rereading your own draft against your own evidence, not running it through a tool.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH REVISION FIXES THE LOGIC?
Here is a weak paragraph from a draft brief: “Oedipus is guilty because he killed a man at the crossroads, and that man turned out to be his father. Anyone who has read the play can see that this proves he is a violent person who was never fit to rule Thebes in the first place, and it is honestly a little embarrassing that anyone would argue otherwise.” Below are four revision attempts. Each one fixes something real. Only one actually repairs the broken logic, the unsupported leap from “killed a man who turned out to be his father” to “a violent person unfit to rule.” Which one?
Which revision attempt actually fixes the logic failure, rather than only a cosmetic problem?
WHY THE OTHER THREE ARE ONLY COSMETIC
The first softens the sneer (“anyone who has read” becomes “careful readers”) but keeps the exact same unsupported leap to “violent” and “unfit to rule.”
The second adds a real citation, which the evidence pass would want, but keeps both the sneer and the same overreaching claim the citation does not actually support.
The fourth only trims a few words for length. The sneer and the logical leap both survive intact, just in a shorter sentence.
