Lesson 7.01
The Quote That Earns Its Place
Lesson · Week 7 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A brief crowded with long quotations does not look more thorough. It looks like an advocate who has not decided what the evidence proves.
You already know how to find strong evidence, integrate it grammatically, and cite it fairly. This lesson is about a choice you make every time evidence goes into your brief: which words to quote, how much of the passage to include, and how to fold the quote into a sentence that argues something specific. A quote is not proof by itself. It becomes proof only once you show a reader exactly what it proves.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to choose a short, sharp quotation over a long, undigested block when both are available.
2
I will be able to explain why a quotation must be analyzed, never simply dropped in, to earn its place in an argument.
3
I will be able to tell the difference between a quotation that genuinely supports a claim and one that only decorates, distorts, or overreaches it.

📚 PART 1 · THE SAME EVIDENCE, HANDLED THREE WAYS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time someone tried to prove a point to you by reading an entire long passage aloud, instead of pointing to the one sentence that actually mattered. Did it convince you more, or less? (No wrong answer.)

Every argument section needs the same three ingredients: a claim, evidence from the text, and reasoning that connects them. Where advocates go wrong is usually not the claim or the evidence itself; it is how the evidence gets placed on the page. Watch the same argument, built from evidence you already verified in Weeks 1 and 2, handled three different ways below.

The claim being argued: Oedipus’s curse on Laius’s murderer reveals a king so confident of his own innocence that the curse becomes devastating dramatic irony once the truth is known.

📝 THE SAME CLAIM, PROVED THREE WAYS
VERSION A · THE UNDIGESTED BLOCK QUOTE
Oedipus is very confident he is innocent. He says: “And on the murderer this curse I lay (on him and all the partners in his guilt): wretch, may he pine in utter wretchedness! And for myself, if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth, I pray the curse I laid on others fall on me.” This shows he is a good king.
What goes wrong: the entire curse is dropped in whole, then followed by a vague, unrelated conclusion (“this shows he is a good king”) that never actually engages the specific words just quoted. The reader has to do all the analytical work the writer skipped.
VERSION B · THE PARAPHRASE WITH NO ANCHOR
Oedipus curses whoever killed Laius, and he says the curse would apply to him too if he turned out to be sheltering the killer without knowing it. This proves he believes he is innocent.
What goes wrong: this is an accurate summary, but it never quotes the play’s actual words. A reader cannot check this claim against the text itself, and the argument loses the specific, striking wording (“with my privity”) that made the claim about irony possible in the first place.
VERSION C · THE SHORT INTEGRATED QUOTE, ANALYZED
Oedipus is so certain of his own innocence that he extends the curse to himself, praying it will fall on him “if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth.” The word “privity,” meaning knowledge, is the hinge the irony turns on: Oedipus is confident because he assumes guilt requires knowing, and he does not yet know what he has done.
Why this works: the quotation is short, exact, and blended into the writer’s own sentence. It is followed immediately by analysis of the one word (“privity”) doing the real work, connecting the specific wording to the specific claim about irony. Nothing is left for the reader to reconstruct.

Notice the pattern. Version A gives the reader everything and explains nothing. Version B explains but gives the reader nothing to check. Version C gives the reader exactly enough of the text to verify the claim, then does the analytical work a reader cannot do without you: naming which word or phrase matters, and why.

📚 PART 2 · TRY IT YOURSELF

Here is a second piece of evidence, already verified in your Week 2 Evidence Bank: Tiresias’s accusation, “Thou art the man, thou the accursed polluter of this land.” The claim it can support: Tiresias reverses the very search Oedipus began, turning the king’s own proclamation back on himself.

✍️ TRY IT YOURSELF, IN YOUR NOTEBOOK, BEFORE YOU LOOK AT THE MODEL BELOW
Write one sentence that integrates a short piece of Tiresias’s accusation, “the accursed polluter of this land,” blended into your own grammar, followed by one sentence of analysis explaining specifically what the phrase reveals. Do this in your notebook first.
Compare your work: a model version (open only after you have written your own)
Tiresias names Oedipus “the accursed polluter of this land,” using the same religious language Oedipus himself used in his proclamation, so that the man hunting a polluter is accused, in his own terms, of being one. The quotation is short enough to blend directly into the sentence, and the analysis names exactly what makes the accusation devastating: it turns Oedipus’s own vocabulary against him.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND QUOTE SELECTION
An AI tool can shorten a long quotation for you in seconds. What it cannot do reliably is choose the one phrase that actually proves your specific claim, or write the analysis connecting that phrase to your argument; that judgment call depends on knowing exactly what you are trying to prove, which only you know. If you use an AI tool to help trim a quotation, check the shortened version against the original text yourself before it goes in your brief.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH INTEGRATION ACTUALLY SUPPORTS THE CLAIM?
Claim: Oedipus’s curse shows a king so confident of his innocence that the curse becomes devastating dramatic irony. Four sentences below try to support that claim with the same evidence. Only one actually does, without decorating, distorting, or overreaching.
Which sentence actually supports the claim?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
The first quotes real words, but the quote and the conclusion do not actually connect; “utter wretchedness” is decoration for a claim about the plague, not evidence for a claim about confidence and irony.
The second quietly drops the word “privity” from the middle of the quotation. That cut distorts the meaning: without “privity” (knowledge), the line no longer shows Oedipus excluding only knowing guilt, and the sentence ends up arguing the opposite of what the full line actually supports.
The third overreaches: the line shows Oedipus is confident he is innocent, not that he secretly suspects otherwise. Claiming the opposite of what the quote plainly shows is not a stronger reading; it is an unsupported one.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A quote does not prove anything by sitting on the page. It proves something once you show, in your own words, exactly what it proves. Choose the short, sharp phrase over the long block, and never let a quotation stand where analysis should be doing the work instead.
