Lesson 7.03
What the Reader Knows
Lesson · Week 7 · Writing Technique
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
You have spent this week reading with information the suitors do not have. Now build that same gap into your own story.
This week’s reading gave you something rare: a poem that lets you know a secret its own characters do not know. That gap between what a reader knows and what a character knows is one of the oldest and most reliable tools a storyteller has. This lesson breaks the tool down into its parts, shows you a worked example, and asks you to try it yourself before you draft Quick Write 7.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the three ingredients a scene needs to create a gap between what the reader knows and what a character knows.
2
I will be able to draft a short scene of my own that lets the reader in on something a character in the scene does not know.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Your Bard’s Hall draft so far, since you will look for a scene in it to revise.
📚 PART 1 · THREE INGREDIENTS FOR THE GAP

Letting a reader know more than a character does is not a trick played on the reader; it is a gift given to them. Three things make the gift work. First, the reader has to be told a piece of information clearly, usually before the scene that matters begins, the way you were told in Book 15 that the suitors were planning an ambush. Second, a character in the scene has to be genuinely unaware of that same information, and has to act, speak, or make decisions as if it were not true. Third, the writer has to let the gap sit there without announcing it. You never write “little did he know” and explain the joke; you simply let the reader notice, on their own, that a character is walking toward something the reader can already see.

Study the worked example below. It was written for this lesson, not lifted from any published book, so you can take it apart freely.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, ANNOTATED
The letter Priya had hidden inside the lining of her coat had not moved in three days, but Dominic kept talking as though nothing in the room could possibly know more than he did. “You can trust me completely,” he said, refilling her cup before she had finished it.
THE READER IS TOLD FIRST
We learn about the hidden letter before Dominic speaks a single word, so we already know something is being concealed from him.
THE CHARACTER ACTS UNAWARE
Dominic’s confident line about trust reads completely differently to a reader who already knows what Priya is hiding. The irony comes from his own words, not from any narrator pointing it out.

Notice what the example does not do. It never adds a line like “Dominic had no idea what she was hiding, which was funny, because…” That kind of explaining flattens the effect. The version above trusts the reader to feel the gap without being told it exists.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Think of a moment in your own Bard’s Hall story, or a brand-new moment if you prefer, where one character could plausibly not know something another character (or the reader) does know: a hidden identity, a secret plan, a danger just out of sight, a piece of news that has not reached them yet.

✍️ In your notebook, write two to three sentences of a scene where the reader is told something clearly, and a character in the scene then speaks or acts as though it were not true. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

Malik’s crew had already spotted the second ship trailing them at dusk. “We’re alone out here,” Malik told the new recruit, checking the horizon in the one direction nothing was coming from.

Compare it to your own draft. Is there a fact stated plainly before the character speaks? Does the character’s line or action only feel ironic because the reader already knows more? If your scene explains the irony out loud, try cutting the explanation and trusting the gap to speak for itself.

⚡ TRY IT · IRONY OR JUST SUSPENSE?
Each setup could plausibly be read as either technique. Decide which the passage actually creates: dramatic irony (the reader has been given information a character does not have) or suspense (reader and character are finding out together).
1. A letter left open on the kitchen table reveals that Dad’s promotion fell through last week. Mia gets home from school and launches straight into her plan for a celebration dinner that night.
2. Mia notices the light under her parents’ bedroom door is already off, though it is only eight o’clock. She knocks once, waits, then eases the door open.

Quick Write 7 (Canvas assignment).
Use the practice draft you just wrote, or revise an existing scene from your Bard’s Hall tale, to build in this gap between reader and character. Full directions are on the Quick Write 7 assignment page.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A gap between what the reader knows and what a character knows earns its power the same way a disguise does in this week’s reading: by staying quiet about itself. Tell the reader the truth early, let the character act without it, and trust the distance between the two to do the work.
