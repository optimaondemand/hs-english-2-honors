📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The restless old king in the poem you are about to read is the same hero you are following through Calypso’s island and the Phaeacian court right now, Odysseus, imagined many centuries later by an English poet under his Roman name, Ulysses. This lesson is an honors-only addition, meant to sit alongside your Book 5–8 reading, not replace anything in your regular week. Tennyson picks up an idea Homer never quite settles: what happens to a hero once the journey is officially over, once he is home, safe, and a king again?
📝 A NOTE ON THE NAME
Everywhere else in this course you have used the Greek name Odysseus. Tennyson’s poem is titled “Ulysses,” the Roman form of the same name, and it uses that name throughout its own lines. Because this is Tennyson’s actual text, we keep his word exactly as he wrote it. Just remember as you read: Ulysses and Odysseus are the same hero.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to place Tennyson’s poem in its historical moment and its literary form.
2
I will be able to identify how Tennyson imagines this hero’s story continuing after Homer’s ending.
3
I will be able to explain what a restless, aged hero reveals about purpose and legacy, a question your own game could take up.
🕑 PART 1 · A POEM WRITTEN AFTER A LOSS

Alfred Tennyson wrote “Ulysses” in 1833, shortly after the sudden death of Arthur Hallam, his closest friend since university, and published it in 1842. Grief runs underneath the poem even though it never mentions Hallam directly: a poem about a man who refuses to sit still and wait out the rest of his life reads differently once you know it was written by someone freshly reminded how short a life can be.

The poem is a dramatic monologue: a single speaker, Ulysses himself, addresses an audience inside the poem (first his own restless thoughts, then his old sailors) while the poet stays silent and lets the character’s own words do all the work. It is written in blank verse, unrhymed lines of ten syllables falling into a steady rise-and-fall rhythm. Read a few lines aloud and you can hear that measured, unhurried beat under the old king’s restlessness.

📜 PART 2 · THE POEM, IN FULL

Homer’s epic ends with Odysseus restored: home on Ithaca, reunited with Penelope and Telemachus, peace remade in his own hall. Tennyson picks the story up from there and imagines what an old king does with a peace he cannot enjoy. Read the whole poem once through before you look closely at any one part of it.

It little profits that an idle king, By this still hearth, among these barren crags, Match'd with an aged wife, I mete and dole Unequal laws unto a savage race, That hoard, and sleep, and feed, and know not me. I cannot rest from travel: I will drink Life to the lees: all times I have enjoy'd Greatly, have suffer'd greatly, both with those That loved me, and alone; on shore, and when Thro' scudding drifts the rainy Hyades Vext the dim sea: I am become a name; For always roaming with a hungry heart Much have I seen and known; cities of men And manners, climates, councils, governments, Myself not least, but honour'd of them all; And drunk delight of battle with my peers, Far on the ringing plains of windy Troy. I am a part of all that I have met; Yet all experience is an arch wherethro' Gleams that untravell'd world, whose margin fades For ever and for ever when I move. How dull it is to pause, to make an end, To rust unburnish'd, not to shine in use! As tho' to breathe were life. Life piled on life Were all too little, and of one to me Little remains: but every hour is saved From that eternal silence, something more, A bringer of new things; and vile it were For some three suns to store and hoard myself, And this gray spirit yearning in desire To follow knowledge, like a sinking star, Beyond the utmost bound of human thought.

This is my son, mine own Telemachus, To whom I leave the sceptre and the isle— Well-loved of me, discerning to fulfil This labour, by slow prudence to make mild A rugged people, and thro' soft degrees Subdue them to the useful and the good. Most blameless is he, centred in the sphere Of common duties, decent not to fail In offices of tenderness, and pay Meet adoration to my household gods When I am gone. He works his work, I mine.

There lies the port: the vessel puffs her sail: There gloom the dark broad seas. My mariners, Souls that have toil'd, and wrought, and thought with me— That ever with a frolic welcome took The thunder and the sunshine, and opposed Free hearts, free foreheads—you and I are old; Old age hath yet his honour and his toil; Death closes all: but something ere the end, Some work of noble note, may yet be done, Not unbecoming men that strove with Gods. The lights begin to twinkle from the rocks: The long day wanes: the slow moon climbs: the deep Moans round with many voices. Come, my friends, 'Tis not too late to seek a newer world. Push off, and sitting well in order smite The sounding furrows; for my purpose holds To sail beyond the sunset, and the baths Of all the western stars, until I die. It may be that the gulfs will wash us down: It may be we shall touch the Happy Isles, And see the great Achilles, whom we knew. Tho' much is taken, much abides; and tho' We are not now that strength which in old days Moved earth and heaven; that which we are, we are; One equal temper of heroic hearts, Made weak by time and fate, but strong in will To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.

Alfred, Lord Tennyson, “Ulysses,” 1842. Public domain; text verified against Wikisource.
⛵ PART 3 · A KING WHO WON’T STAY HOME

Notice where Ulysses places himself in his own story. He has already returned; the throne, the wife, the household are all his again, exactly what the whole Odyssey drove toward. And yet his opening word for his own peace is “idle,” and he calls the people he now rules a “savage race” who “know not me.” He hands the kingdom’s actual, patient work to Telemachus without much visible regret, then turns to his old crew and proposes one more voyage, out past the edge of anything either of them has charted, with no promise of return.

This raises a genuinely open question, one careful readers answer differently. Is Ulysses admirable here, refusing to let age and comfort end a life still capable of “some work of noble note”? Or is he abandoning a real, present duty, a kingdom and a family, to chase a feeling he cannot get any other way? The poem gives you real evidence for both readings and does not settle the question for you. As you reread, mark one line that makes Ulysses sound most admirable to you, and one that makes him sound most like he is making excuses. You do not have to resolve the tension; noticing it clearly is the point.

🎮 PART 4 · A SEED FOR YOUR OWN GAME

Tennyson is doing something worth noticing as a designer, not just as a reader: he takes a story everyone already considers finished and asks what the main character does the day after the ending. A game can do the same thing. What does a character, a household, or a whole community look like once the official story is over? Do they settle, or does something in them keep pulling toward one more risk? Whichever world you build your game in, this poem is worth holding onto as one shape a character’s life after the ending might take.

⚡ SELF-CHECK
⚡ TRY IT · THE BEST-SUPPORTED READING
Each question offers four readings of specific lines. More than one will sound reasonable; choose the one the poem’s actual language best supports, and be ready to say why the others overreach or fall short.
1. Ulysses says of Telemachus, “He works his work, I mine.” What is the best-supported reading of this line?
2. “Old age hath yet his honour and his toil... Death closes all: but something ere the end, / Some work of noble note, may yet be done.” What is the best-supported reading of this stance toward old age?
3. Ulysses opens by calling his own peaceful rule “idle” and describing his people as a “savage race” that “hoard, and sleep, and feed, and know not me.” What is the best-supported reading of this opening?
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Tennyson gives the Odyssey’s hero a life after the ending, and it is not a restful one. Keep both readings of Ulysses in mind as you finish Books 5–8: a man still capable of greatness, or a man unwilling to accept that the story is supposed to be over. Either way, notice how a single poem, decades after Homer, kept this hero’s story alive by imagining past its edge.
