Honors Sprint 1
Using AI to Support Your Game, Not Replace You
Lesson · Week 1
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Building a game all year means a lot of thinking out loud: brainstorming, drafting rules, researching a period, sketching an aesthetic. AI tools are genuinely useful for that kind of thinking-out-loud work. They are not useful for deciding whether your game is any good, and they cannot be trusted on historical detail without checking. This lesson sets the ground rules you will use for the rest of the year, and introduces the log you will keep every time you use AI on this project.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name what an AI assistant is and identify at least one tool available to me through my school account, plus one other common, free, credible option.
2
I will be able to apply the verify-and-critique stance and basic privacy habits any time I use AI on this project.
3
I will be able to explain two specific things AI cannot do for a game designer, and keep an AI-Use Log across the year.
🤖 PART 1 · WHAT THESE TOOLS ARE, AND WHICH ONE IS YOURS

An AI assistant is a program that generates text, ideas, or images in response to what you type, based on patterns it learned from enormous amounts of writing. It does not know your specific game, your specific classroom, or whether a rule you wrote is actually fun to play. It is fast at producing options and drafts, and it is unreliable at judgment calls that depend on real testing or real, verifiable facts.

You do not have a Claude account for this course, and you do not need one. Your school Microsoft 365 account already gives you Microsoft Copilot, built into Word, Edge, and the rest of your school apps; that is the default tool for this class because it is already yours. Other common, free, credible assistants exist too, such as Google Gemini or a school-approved alternative, and you are welcome to use whichever you have real access to. No specific tool is required. What matters is not which tool you pick, but what you do with what it gives you.

🔒 PRIVACY BASICS
Never paste personal information into an AI tool: your full name and school together, addresses, other students’ names, photos of yourself or classmates, or anything you would not want stored on a company’s server. For this project, you are almost always asking about a game concept, a historical period, or a rule, none of which requires any personal data at all.
🧠 PART 2 · THE STANCE: VERIFY AND CRITIQUE, NEVER SUBSTITUTE

Every use of AI on this project follows the same shape: you ask, it answers, and then you check the answer against something real and decide what to keep. The AI never makes the design decision, never confirms a historical fact on its own authority, and never stands in for the thinking you are supposed to be doing. Across the year you will use this stance to brainstorm design questions, summarize a period so you can choose a world, red-team your rules for clarity, and catch anachronisms in your aesthetic research. Each time, the pattern is the same: prompt, then verify, critique, and improve.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If an AI tool gave you a confident, detailed answer about a historical period, how sure would you want to be before you used it in your game? (No wrong answer.)
⚠️ PART 3 · TWO THINGS AI CANNOT DO FOR A GAME DESIGNER

This project happens to expose AI’s limits with unusual clarity. Two of them matter enough to name outright, before you start building.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · LIMIT ONE: FUN AND FAIR
AI cannot tell you whether your game is fun or fair. It can describe what “fun” usually means in general, but it has never played your specific rules against a real opponent who got bored, frustrated, or delighted. Only playtesting, with real people and your actual materials, can answer that. This is why the Designer’s Table requires three real playtest cycles later this year; there is no AI shortcut around them.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · LIMIT TWO: PERIOD DETAIL
AI gets historical and period detail wrong more often than it sounds like it does, and it states wrong details with the same confident tone as right ones. Once you declare your world and start real research, every AI-surfaced historical claim you plan to use gets checked on JStor, where you confirm it exists and says what the AI claimed. An unverifiable claim gets cut, no matter how good it sounded.
📝 PART 4 · YOUR AI-USE LOG

Starting with the Design Question assignment, you keep one running log of every substantive AI use on the Designer’s Table project. Keep it in your Module 1 Organizer, and carry it forward for the rest of the year. An honest, complete log is required before your final portfolio can be assessed at all; it is not optional polish.

Date What I asked AI to do What it gave me How I verified or critiqued it What I changed and why
Example: 9/2 Brainstorm 10 design questions about community and belonging. A list of 10 questions, several very generic. Checked each against what actually interests me and what a game could show through play, not just tell. Rejected 8 as too broad for a game; combined ideas from 2 into my own question.

The example row shows the format only; your own rows will look different because your own thinking will be different.

⚡ SELF-CHECK · WHICH LIMIT IS THIS?
⚡ TRY IT
Each situation demands a judgment call, not just a label. Read it closely and choose what actually follows.
1. An AI tool explains, in general terms, why games often feel like they drag in the middle, and the explanation seems reasonable. You use it to revise a rule. What can this explanation NOT tell you, even after you make the revision?
2. AI tells you confidently that duels in Elizabethan England were always fought with rapiers, never any other weapon. You have not yet checked this. What is the correct move?
3. You ask AI to generate ten possible design questions about community and belonging. It gives you a list, several fairly generic. What should happen next?
➡️ WHAT YOU’LL DO NEXT
1
Use this stance on the Design Question assignment.
When you brainstorm candidate design questions with an AI tool, record that use as your first AI-Use Log entry.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Use AI to generate options, drafts, and first drafts of a period summary; never to decide if your game works or to settle a historical fact on its own word. Playtesting tells you what AI cannot, and JStor confirms what AI might have gotten wrong. Keep the log honest, and it will do real work for you all year.
