Lesson 8.02
The Closing Argument
Lesson · Week 8 · Oral Advocacy
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A brief on paper and a closing argument spoken aloud are not the same object. Today you build the second one from the first.
Your brief runs several pages and carries every argument you have. A spoken closing has sixty to ninety seconds and one job: leave the Council certain of your verdict. You have already been training the exact muscle this lesson needs. Every week you spent practicing your recitation, learning to carry a passage in memory and deliver it so a listener can follow, was rehearsal for standing in front of the Council and doing the same thing with your own words instead of Sophocles’s.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify which features of written argument survive being spoken aloud, and which ones need to change.
2
I will be able to structure a 60 to 90 second closing argument: verdict, best argument, counterclaim dispatched, and the ask.
3
I will be able to apply the pace, pause, and eye-contact habits from my recitation practice to a spoken closing argument.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT SURVIVES THE JUMP FROM PAGE TO VOICE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a speech, a toast, or even a coach’s pep talk that actually stuck with you afterward. What made it easy to follow with your ears instead of your eyes? (No wrong answer.)

What survives, and what dies, in the jump to speech

A reader can pause, reread a dense sentence, and flip back to check a citation. A listener cannot. That single difference changes what makes writing work.

SURVIVES THE JUMP
Short sentences. One idea per breath. A listener can hold one claim in mind at a time, not three stacked inside a single sentence.
Concrete evidence. A specific image or fact (the double-pointed goad, the sea of troubles) lands in the ear the way it lands on the page.
One repeated key phrase. Saying your verdict, or a phrase built from it, more than once gives a listener something to hold onto and remember after you stop talking.
DIES IN THE JUMP
Subordinate-clause chains. A sentence with three nested “which” and “because” clauses is readable on paper. Spoken aloud, a listener loses the main claim before you reach the end.
Parenthetical citations read aloud. Saying “open parenthesis, Sophocles, trans. Storr, close parenthesis” mid-sentence breaks the spell a spoken argument depends on. Name your source in a spoken phrase instead: “as Oedipus himself admits…”

This is not a demand to write less carefully. It is a translation problem: your brief already has the material. Your job now is choosing which parts of it can survive being heard instead of read, and rebuilding the rest.

📚 PART 2 · FOUR MOVES, SIXTY TO NINETY SECONDS

Your full brief has six moves. A closing argument compresses that down to four, and drops straight into the verdict instead of building up to it:

1 · VERDICT, STATED FIRST
Open with your verdict, not a summary of the plot. The Council already knows the story; they need to know where you stand.
2 · YOUR SINGLE BEST ARGUMENT
Not all of them. The one piece of evidence and reasoning that carries the most weight, spoken in short sentences.
3 · THE COUNTERCLAIM, DISPATCHED
Name the opposing case fairly in one sentence, then answer it in one or two more. This is your Response from the brief, compressed.
4 · THE ASK
Tell the Council exactly what you want them to do. End on it; do not trail off into summary after you have made the ask.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE · A FULL 60 TO 90 SECOND CLOSING
“Council of Thebes: under a standard that weighs what a man intended, not merely what his hands eventually did, Oedipus is not guilty of the murder of Laius.

Here is the strongest reason. By his own account, the crossroads was a mutual, escalating struggle, a blow met with a blow, not a plan carried out against a chosen target. He did not know the man in front of him. He could not have intended to kill his father, because he did not know his father stood there.

You will hear that ignorance is no excuse, that this city has suffered regardless of what Oedipus knew. That names a real harm. But the standard before this Council asks what Oedipus intended, not only what the world later revealed, and no warning he carried that day named this man at this crossroads.

Intent, not outcome, is the standard this Council must apply. Judge Oedipus by what he knew. Under that standard, this evidence supports one verdict: not guilty of murder.”
Illustrative model closing, written for this lesson, not a real student submission.

Notice the repeated phrase doing its work: “what a man intended” opens the closing, returns in the middle, and anchors the final line. A listener who remembers nothing else will remember that this case is about intent.

📚 PART 3 · DELIVERY: THE SAME SKILLS AS YOUR RECITATION

You have been building these delivery habits since Week 4, and this closing argument is where they get put to a new use:

PACE
Slower than feels natural, especially on the verdict sentence. A rushed verdict sounds unsure even when the reasoning behind it is solid.
PAUSE
A short, deliberate pause right before the ask. Silence signals that what comes next matters most.
EYES UP
Know it well enough to look at the Council, not a script, the same standard your recitation already holds you to.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND ORAL DELIVERY
An AI tool can compress a written brief into a shorter script in seconds. What it cannot do is deliver it, or judge whether your pacing, your pause before the ask, or your eye contact actually land with a listener. That is a rehearsal skill, built the same way your recitation was: by saying it out loud, more than once, before it counts.

WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
“It could be argued… reasonable people can disagree” hedges to nothing. It never actually tells the Council where the advocate stands.
“Oedipus was the king…” summarizes the plot the Council already knows instead of stating a position on it. A closing is not a synopsis.
“How much longer will we let a so-called king…” grandstands: it sounds forceful, but it mocks rather than commits, and it never actually names a verdict or a standard.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Your brief has to survive careful rereading. Your closing has to survive being heard exactly once. Short sentences, one strong piece of evidence, a phrase that returns, and a verdict stated plainly and early: that is the whole translation, and it is the same discipline your recitation has been training all module.
