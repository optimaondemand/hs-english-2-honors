📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A story about disguise and mistaken identity is exactly the kind of story where a careless pronoun can confuse a reader.
This week two men, a father and a son, stand in the same small hut, and a scene built on recognition and confusion only works if the reader always knows exactly who “he” means. That is a useful accident: it is also exactly when your own writing is most likely to lose a reader with an unclear pronoun. This lesson gives you a quick test for catching that problem, plus this week’s Word Study words, both of which you will use in Quick Write 7.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the exact word or phrase a pronoun refers back to, and revise a sentence where that reference is unclear.
2
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · WHO DOES “HE” MEAN?

A pronoun like he, she, they, or it stands in for a noun that came before it, called its antecedent. A pronoun is only doing its job if a reader can point to exactly one word it refers back to. The moment a sentence has two people it could reasonably mean, the pronoun stops communicating and starts confusing. A separate but related problem is agreement: a pronoun has to match its antecedent in number, one for one and many for many, or the sentence quietly stops making sense even when the reference itself is clear.

Compare the sentences below, all describing this week’s reunion between father and son. The unclear or mismatched pronoun is marked in the first line of each pair, and the fix in the second.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CLEAR OR UNCLEAR?
Odysseus greeted Telemachus warmly, and he could hardly believe his eyes.
Unclear: both Odysseus and Telemachus are men in this sentence, so “he” could technically point to either one. A first-time reader has to guess.
Odysseus greeted Telemachus warmly, and the young man could hardly believe his eyes.
Clear: naming “the young man” instead of repeating a pronoun removes the guesswork entirely. This is often the simplest fix of all: name the person again.
Every one of the suitors made their excuse to Penelope.
Mismatched: “every one” is singular, so it needs a singular pronoun to agree with it, not a plural one.
Every one of the suitors made his excuse to Penelope.
Agrees: singular antecedent, singular pronoun. If you want to avoid assuming gender, you can also rewrite the whole sentence in the plural: “All of the suitors made their excuses to Penelope.”

The fastest test: read the sentence and ask, could this pronoun honestly point to more than one noun I have already named? If yes, either rename the person or restructure the sentence so only one reading is possible.

⚡ TRY IT · CORRECT, OR NEEDS REVISION?
Each sentence has a pronoun worth checking closely. Some look suspicious but are actually fine; some read smoothly but hide a real problem. Decide whether each is correct as written or needs revision, and be ready to say why.
1. “Odysseus asked Eumaeus whether he could trust his own household.”
2. “Telemachus told Penelope that she should wait a little longer before the suitors grew bolder.”
3. “Neither of the suitors would admit that they were afraid of Telemachus’s new resolve.”
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 13 through 16. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

wrinkles (noun) — creases or folds, especially in skin, that come with age
Part of the exact disguise Athena gives Odysseus in Book 13, covering him with wrinkles so no one will know his true age or face.
begrimed (adjective) — dirtied or blackened, especially with soot or smoke
Describes the ragged tunic Athena puts on Odysseus in Book 13. The same word later describes a household grown neglected, as if dirt itself can mark what has gone wrong in Odysseus’s absence.
tramp (noun) — a person who wanders from place to place, often assumed to beg or lie
The word Eumaeus uses in Book 14 for wandering strangers whose stories he distrusts, not realizing the very man he is sheltering is no tramp at all.
ambuscade (noun) — an ambush; an attack launched from a hidden position
Used in Book 14 for a surprise attack, and the exact danger Athena warns Telemachus to sail around in Book 15, the suitors lying in wait along the strait.
imposing (adjective) — impressive in size, appearance, or presence
Describes Odysseus in Book 16 the instant Athena restores him, making him “of more imposing presence” right before Telemachus’s eyes.
lawless (adjective) — having no respect for law or rightful authority
Odysseus’s own word for the suitors in Book 16, spoken the moment he reveals his identity to his son and names exactly what has been done to his household.
✍️ PUT IT TO WORK
As you draft Quick Write 7, run the pronoun test on every sentence with more than one person in it: could “he,” “she,” or “they” honestly point to more than one character you have named? If so, rename or restructure until only one reading is possible. Also work in at least two of today’s Word Study words, used accurately. That is how this lesson gets assessed: not by a quiz, but by what shows up in your own writing.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A scene about disguise and recognition only works if the reader never loses track of who is who. Carry the pronoun test and two Word Study words with you into Quick Write 7.
