You will use both moves in Quick Write 2, along with this week’s Word Study words from Books 3 and 4.

2

📚 PART 1 · THREE SHAPES A SENTENCE CAN TAKE

Every complete sentence is built from at least one independent clause: a subject and verb pair that could stand on its own as a full sentence. What changes from sentence to sentence is how many clauses are joined together, and what kind of clause each one is.

A simple sentence has exactly one independent clause. A compound sentence joins two independent clauses, usually with a comma and a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so, for, nor, yet) or with a semicolon. A complex sentence joins an independent clause to a dependent clause, a clause that cannot stand alone, using a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, since, while, if).

✎ ON THE BOARD · SIMPLE, COMPOUND, COMPLEX
Simple: Nestor welcomed the strangers at once.
One independent clause. One subject, one verb, nothing joined to it.
Compound: Nestor fed his guests generously, and he refused to let Telemachus sleep on a ship.
Two independent clauses, each one able to stand alone, joined here by a comma and the coordinating conjunction and.
Complex: Because Menelaus honored his guests before questioning them, Telemachus felt free to speak.
The underlined part cannot stand alone; it depends on the rest of the sentence. The subordinating conjunction because attaches it to the independent clause that follows.

Notice what each shape is good for. A run of simple sentences can feel sharp and direct. A compound sentence balances two equal ideas side by side. A complex sentence can show cause and effect, or set a condition, in a single move. A writer who reaches for the same shape every time is leaving tools unused.

⚡ TRY IT · SIMPLE, COMPOUND, OR COMPLEX?
These are trickier than they look: a joining word does not always mean two independent clauses, and a missing comma does not always mean a sentence is simple. Analyze the structure of each one before you decide.
1. “Nestor welcomed the strangers and fed them a hearty meal.”
2. “Telemachus felt free to speak because Menelaus honored his guests first.”
3. “Menelaus welcomed his guests warmly, yet his grief for lost companions never fully left him.”
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 3 and 4. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

hospitable (adjective) — generous and welcoming toward guests or strangers
Diocles, a host Telemachus and Mentor stay with on the road to Sparta, is described as entertaining them hospitably. The word marks exactly the quality Nestor and Menelaus share.
splendor (noun) — great brightness or magnificence, especially of wealth or beauty
The poem compares the splendor of Menelaus’s palace to the sun and moon. It is the clearest sign yet of just how far this household’s wealth outpaces Nestor’s.
cunning (adjective) — clever in a sly or resourceful way, hard to outwit
Menelaus admits that even Proteus, a shape-shifting sea god, found Odysseus too cunning to trap easily. The word follows Odysseus everywhere he goes, even in stories told about him by others.
mourned (verb, past tense of mourn) — felt or expressed deep sorrow, especially for the dead or the lost
Grief runs under the wealth in these two books. Even at Menelaus’s glittering court, the losses of the Trojan War are still mourned openly, years later.
discretion (noun) — good judgment in what to say or do, especially for someone’s age
Menelaus praises Telemachus’s discretion, noting that his judgment is unusual for someone so young. It is a small but real sign that Telemachus is maturing into someone worth taking seriously.
astonished (adjective) — greatly surprised or amazed
Telemachus and Pisistratus are astonished at their first sight of Menelaus’s palace. The word captures just how far this household’s wealth exceeds anything they have seen before.
