📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Your brief needs a source beyond the play itself. Today you learn how to find one, and how to tell a real one from a weak one.
You now have the whole story of the play in your Evidence Bank. Your brief also requires one researched scholarly source, because a strong verdict rests on more than your own reading; it engages what scholars have argued about the very question your case turns on. Today’s lesson walks you through accessing a research database, searching it well, judging what you find, and using it honestly. Quick Write 13 is where you log the source you choose.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to access JStor and search it effectively for a research question.
2
I will be able to evaluate a source’s reliability and read it strategically before deciding whether it earns a place in my brief.
3

📚 PART 1 · GETTING INTO JSTOR

JStor is a large digital library of scholarly journal articles across the humanities and sciences, including classics and literary studies. Optima Academy Online provides a shared school login so every student can search it directly.

This is the same shared login every Optima student uses to access JStor. Go to jstor.org, sign in, and you can search and read articles directly.

Once you are signed in, search terms matter as much as the login itself. A vague search returns thousands of results with no clear direction. A well-built search gets you to relevant scholarship fast. Try search terms like these:

“miasma Greek tragedy”
“pollution Oedipus”
“moral luck Oedipus”

If your first search returns too much, narrow it (add “Sophocles” or “guilt”). If it returns too little, broaden it (drop “Oedipus” and search just “miasma pollution guilt”). Searching well is its own skill, and it usually takes more than one try.

📚 PART 2 · IS THIS SOURCE ANY GOOD?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you had to decide whether an article or video was trustworthy before you used it for something that mattered. What made you trust it, or not? (No wrong answer.)

Not everything JStor returns is equally useful. Some results are peer-reviewed scholarship (reviewed by other experts before publication); some are book reviews, popular commentary, or older material that has since been built on by newer research. Before you log a source, run it through four tests.

✅ FOUR QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE YOU TRUST A SOURCE
1. Peer-reviewed?
Is this a journal article that went through expert review, or a magazine piece, blog post, or opinion essay?
2. Credentials?
Who wrote it, and what makes them qualified to argue this claim (a scholar in classics or philosophy, or someone without relevant expertise)?
3. Date?
Is it reasonably current, or does it predate scholarship your question actually needs (like modern intent-based legal thinking)?
4. Relevance?
Does it actually address YOUR verdict question, or a related-but-different topic (staging, meter, biography)?

A source can pass three of these tests and still fail the fourth. An excellent, current, peer-reviewed article on Sophoclean stagecraft is still the wrong source for your brief if it never touches guilt, pollution, or intent.

📚 PART 3 · READING IT WELL, THEN USING IT HONESTLY

You do not have to read a scholarly article start to finish to know whether it is useful. Read strategically: the abstract first (a short summary at the top, if the article has one), then the introduction (which states the argument), then the conclusion (which restates what was actually shown). Only once those three convince you the source is worth your time should you read the body in full.

Once you have a source worth using, the way you use it matters just as much as finding it. A source should inform your reasoning, not replace it. That means: restate its claim faithfully in your own words (the same faithful-paraphrase habit from Week 3), then explain in your own words how it bears on your verdict. Never let a quotation stand in for your own argument; the source is evidence you reason with, not a substitute for reasoning.

📚 PART 4 · THE QUESTION YOUR RESEARCH ACTUALLY ANSWERS

Your search terms are not random; they point at one real scholarly disagreement your verdict depends on. To an ancient Athenian audience, an act could pollute a person or a city (the Greek concept is miasma) whether or not the person meant to do it. Killing your father and marrying your mother would defile Thebes even if you had no idea, at the time, who either of them was. A modern court works differently: guilt usually depends on intent (what a defendant meant to do, sometimes called mens rea, a guilty mind). Oedipus did not know Laius was his father or Jocasta his mother. Under a pollution standard, that ignorance would not matter; the deed itself is the problem. Under an intent standard, it matters enormously; a person who acts without knowledge, and without any reasonable way to have known, is judged very differently from one who acts on purpose.

Your verdict, whichever one you argue, depends on which standard you believe the Council of Thebes should apply, and your researched source should help you argue that choice with more than your own opinion behind it.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND YOUR RESEARCH
An AI tool can suggest search terms and give you a decent plain-language summary of what miasma means. What it cannot reliably do is cite real sources: AI tools regularly invent article titles, authors, and journals that sound entirely convincing but do not exist, or misdescribe what a real article actually says. Every source an AI tool surfaces for you must be independently verified on JStor itself: confirm the article actually exists, and confirm it actually says what the AI claimed. If you cannot find and confirm it yourself, on JStor, it does not go in your Research Log.
⚡ TRY IT · LOG IT, OR CUT IT?
Your search for “miasma Greek tragedy intent” turns up four possible sources. For each one, decide whether it belongs in your Research Log.
1. A peer-reviewed classics journal article on choral meter and staging in Sophocles, with no real discussion of guilt, pollution, or intent.
2. A widely shared magazine essay published this month, written by a novelist, arguing passionately that ancient Greeks were obsessed with fate.
3. A classics journal article from 1962 by a respected scholar, directly analyzing pollution in Sophocles, written decades before most modern scholarship on intent-based guilt existed.
4. A peer-reviewed article in a classics or philosophy journal, published within the last twenty years, directly comparing ancient pollution-based guilt to modern intent-based guilt in Oedipus specifically.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A real source does four things at once: it is peer-reviewed, it comes from someone qualified to make the claim, it is reasonably current, and it actually answers your question. Find one that passes all four tests, and use it to sharpen your own reasoning about miasma and intent, not to replace it.
