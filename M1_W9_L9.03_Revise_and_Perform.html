📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Drafting gets a story onto the page. Revision is what makes it worth someone else’s time.
Every week this module has added a new tool to your Bard’s Hall draft: an in medias res opening, a fully built setting, a figurative comparison, real dialogue, rising stakes, dramatic irony. This lesson is about the different, harder skill of standing back from all of that and asking whether it actually works as one whole piece. You will learn a concrete way to check your draft, watch a before-and-after example, and then apply the same checklist to your own writing before you prepare it to be shared aloud for your final project.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to apply a concrete revision checklist to a finished narrative draft.
2
I will be able to identify what changes when a piece of writing is prepared to be read aloud rather than read silently.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your full Bard’s Hall draft from Quick Write 8.
▶
A notebook or notes document, and a quiet space to read your draft aloud to yourself.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT REVISION ACTUALLY CHECKS

Revision is not the same as fixing typos; that comes later, in this week’s grammar lesson. Revision means reading your whole draft as a stranger would, looking for places where the story as a whole does not yet hold together, even if every individual sentence is fine. A finished draft can still have an ending that no longer matches how the story opened, a narrator who sounds like a different person from one scene to the next, or a big moment that lands flat because it was never properly set up. Use this checklist as you reread your full Bard’s Hall draft:

✍️ THE REVISION CHECKLIST
☐
Does your opening still match how the story now ends? A draft often changes direction as it grows; make sure the first paragraph still promises the story you actually told.
☐
Is your narrator’s voice consistent scene to scene? A teller who sounds wry and clever in one scene should not suddenly sound flat and formal in the next, unless that shift is doing something on purpose.
☐
Have you cut any scene that only repeats what another scene already showed? If two moments make the same point about a character, keep the stronger one and cut the other.
☐
Does the reader feel the stakes you built up actually pay off at the climax? If you raised a threat or a promise earlier in the draft, the ending needs to answer it.
☐
Read the whole draft aloud once. Mark every place where you stumble, run out of breath, or have to reread a line to understand it. Fix those lines first; they are almost always the weakest ones.

Now watch the checklist applied to a short passage written for this lesson. Notice what actually changes between the two versions.

BEFORE

Aris was a young sailor who had always wanted to see the northern islands, and now he finally had his chance. He walked into the hall where the council was meeting. The hall was big and had a lot of old banners on the walls. He was nervous, but he decided to speak anyway, even though he had never spoken in front of the council before, and he cleared his throat.

AFTER

Aris cleared his throat once, twice, and the old banners along the council hall seemed to sway with the sound. He had never spoken here before. He spoke anyway.

The before version front-loads backstory the reader does not need yet (Aris wanting to see the northern islands has nothing to do with this scene), repeats the same idea twice (nervous, and never having spoken before), and buries the actual action, clearing his throat, deciding to speak, under a pile of explanation. The after version keeps only what the scene needs and trusts three short sentences to carry the tension.

📚 PART 2 · PREPARING TO PERFORM IT

Your Bard’s Hall project ends with a recorded telling, not just a written page, so revision has one more job to do: preparing the piece to be heard, not only read. A story meant to be read silently can survive a long, winding sentence, because a reader can pause and reread it. A story meant to be spoken aloud cannot. Long sentences need to be broken into pieces a voice can actually carry in one breath, and any moment where the listener needs to know who is speaking has to be completely clear without a page to glance back at.

✍️ Find one sentence in your own draft that would be hard to say aloud in a single breath. Rewrite it in your notebook as two or three shorter sentences. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

Long, hard to say aloud: “Mira had been planning the escape for weeks, ever since she overheard the guards talking about the shipment, and now that the night had finally come she felt both terrified and strangely calm as she reached for the door.”

Broken for the ear: “Mira had been planning this for weeks. She reached for the door. Terrified, and strangely calm.”

Compare it to your own rewrite. Did breaking the sentence change the pacing, not just the length? A shorter sentence often lands harder when it is spoken aloud than the same idea would in one long sentence.
