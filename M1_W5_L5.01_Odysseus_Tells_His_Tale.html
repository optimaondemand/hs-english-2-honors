📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The poem hands its own microphone to its hero, and Odysseus takes it.
Through Book 8, an outside narrator has been telling this story, and you have watched Odysseus listen to Demodocus sing of Troy. In Book 9, that changes. Alcinous finally asks his guest who he is, and Odysseus answers by taking over the telling himself. For the next four books, you are hearing Odysseus’s wanderings the way he chooses to tell them, in his own voice, to a room full of strangers deciding whether to trust him. Notice what that shift does to the story.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what changes when the poem shifts from an outside narrator to Odysseus narrating his own adventures.
2
I will be able to describe the “Nobody” trick Odysseus uses against the Cyclops Polyphemus and explain why it works.
3
I will be able to explain how Odysseus’s decision to shout his real name back at Polyphemus reveals both his cunning and his pride, and what it costs him.

📚 PART 1 · A HERO BECOMES HIS OWN NARRATOR
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you told a friend, a coach, or an interviewer the story of something that happened to you. Did you shape the story to make yourself look a certain way? (No wrong answer.)

Odysseus finally answers Alcinous’s question of who he is, and the line he uses to introduce himself is one of the most famous in the poem:

“I am Odysseus son of Laertes, renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety, so that my fame ascends to heaven.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Notice what he leads with. Not his kingdom, not his family first, but his own cleverness, the very quality this whole telling is about to put on display. From here through the end of Book 12, an outside narrator is no longer describing Odysseus from a distance. Odysseus is describing himself, to an audience deciding in real time whether to believe him and help him home. Everything you learn about the Cyclops, Circe, and the rest comes filtered through his own account of his own adventures, told in his own words.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
In an oral storytelling culture, a guest who could hold a hall spellbound with his own adventures was proving something: that he was worth feeding, worth helping, worth trusting with a ship home. Odysseus is not only surviving his story. He is performing it, the same way Demodocus performed the fall of Troy last week. The line between hero and bard has nearly disappeared.
📚 PART 2 · TWO QUICK WARNINGS: THE CICONES AND THE LOTUS

Before the Cyclops, Odysseus tells two shorter stories against himself, or at least against his men. Leaving Troy, they raid the city of the Cicones and win easily, dividing the plunder fairly. But instead of leaving, the men linger on the shore, feasting and drinking, until the Cicones return with reinforcements and cut them down. It is not a monster that costs them here. It is their own delay.

Then the winds carry them to the land of the Lotus-eaters, where the local plant is not poison but something closer to total contentment: anyone who eats it stops caring about home entirely. Three of Odysseus’s scouts taste it and have to be dragged back to the ships, weeping, and tied down so they cannot go back for more. Notice the pattern starting here: the greatest dangers in this book are not always claws and teeth. Sometimes they are a man’s own appetite or carelessness.

📚 PART 3 · POLYPHEMUS, THE HOST WHO EATS HIS GUESTS

The Cyclops Polyphemus lives alone in a cave, tending flocks, answering to no king and, he says openly, caring nothing for the gods. When Odysseus and twelve of his men enter his cave hoping for the kind of welcome Nestor and Menelaus gave their own guests, they get the opposite: Polyphemus rolls a boulder across the entrance, traps them inside, and eats two men at a time for his meals. Every rule of hospitality you have watched built up for four weeks is broken here in the worst way imaginable. A proper host feeds his guest. Polyphemus feeds on his.

Odysseus’s escape plan depends on a trick of naming. In the actual Greek, when Polyphemus asks his name, Odysseus answers Outis, which means “Nobody.” Butler’s translation renders this as a single invented word:

“My name is Noman; this is what my father and mother and my friends have always called me.”
Odysseus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Polyphemus, pleased with what he takes as a kind of favor, answers:

“Then I will eat all Noman’s comrades before Noman himself, and will keep Noman for the last.”
Polyphemus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

That night, Odysseus and his men drive a fire-hardened stake into the Cyclops’s single eye while he sleeps off the wine they gave him: “We drove the sharp end of the beam into the monster’s eye, and bearing upon it with all my weight I kept turning it round and round as though I were boring a hole in a ship’s plank with an auger.” When Polyphemus screams for help, the other Cyclopes outside hear him shout that Nobody is attacking him, and they simply go back to sleep. A name built to disappear saves Odysseus’s life. In the morning, his men escape clinging to the undersides of the Cyclops’s own rams, tied together in threes, slipping past his blindly searching hands.

📝 THE BOAST FROM THE SHIP, AND WHAT IT COSTS
“Cyclops, if any one asks you who it was that put your eye out and spoiled your beauty, say it was the valiant warrior Odysseus, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca.”
Odysseus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
THE CUNNING THAT SAVED HIM
The Nobody trick was pure calculation: give up a name to protect a life, and let the other Cyclopes dismiss the cry for help.
THE PRIDE THAT DOOMS HIM
Once safely at sea, Odysseus cannot resist taking his name back and boasting of it. Polyphemus turns out to be Poseidon’s own son, and now knows exactly whom to curse.

Polyphemus, blind and furious, prays to his father:

“Hear me, great Poseidon; if I am indeed your own true begotten son, grant that Odysseus may never reach his home alive; or if he must get back to his friends at last, let him do so late and in sore plight after losing all his men.”
Polyphemus, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Everything that happens to Odysseus for the rest of the poem, every storm, every year lost, traces back to this one moment: the same cunning that got him out of the cave could not resist one boast that gave a god a reason to hate him. A story he is telling to make himself look impressive to Alcinous includes, without flinching, the exact mistake that is still costing him his voyage home.

⚡ TRY IT · CUNNING OR PRIDE?
For each moment from Book 9, choose what it most reveals about Odysseus. You will get feedback right away.
1. Odysseus tells Polyphemus his name is “Noman.”
2. Once safely at sea, Odysseus shouts his real name back at Polyphemus.
3. Polyphemus prays to Poseidon by name, now that he knows who blinded him.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Odysseus survives Polyphemus through cunning and nearly ruins himself through pride, and he tells both halves of that story without hiding either one. A teller’s voice reveals character just as much as a character’s actions do. Carry that idea with you into this week’s writing lessons.
