How to Read the Odyssey
A short, practical guide for pacing a long epic in your own copy
📖 WHY THIS PAGE EXISTS
The Odyssey is long, and it is easy to fall behind on a book you are reading on your own schedule. This page is not another assignment. It is a short set of tools: a pace to read at, a few things worth watching for as you go, and a reminder of where your notes belong. Come back to it any week you feel lost.
📚 READ BY THE BOOK

The Odyssey is divided into 24 “books,” which are really more like chapters, each covering a self-contained stretch of the story. Every reading assignment in this module tells you which book or books to read, never a line number or page count. Use whatever your own copy calls Book 1, Book 2, and so on; the numbering is the same across all Fagles editions. If you ever lose your place, the book number is the fastest way back in.

📅 THE MODULE 1 PACING PLAN

This is the whole nine-week reading plan for the Odyssey. You do not need to read ahead, but knowing the shape of the whole journey can help you see how each week fits.

Week Books Scene anchors
1 1–2 Gods’ council; Athena and Telemachus; the suitors in the house; the assembly on Ithaca
2 3–4 Telemachus at Pylos with Nestor; Sparta with Menelaus and Helen; guest-friendship on display
3 5–6 Odysseus freed from Calypso; the raft and the storm; Nausicaa and the Phaeacians
4 7–8 The Phaeacian court; the bard Demodocus sings of Troy; Odysseus weeps, still hidden
5 9–10 Odysseus tells his tale: the Lotus-eaters, the Cyclops Polyphemus, Aeolus’s bag of winds, Circe
6 11–12 The land of the dead and Tiresias; the Sirens; Scylla and Charybdis; the cattle of the Sun
7 13–16 Home to Ithaca in disguise; Eumaeus the swineherd; reunion with Telemachus
8 17–20 The beggar in his own hall; the suitors’ insults; Penelope; the bow is set up
9 21–24 The contest of the bow; the slaying of the suitors; Penelope’s test; peace restored
🔍 WHAT TO TRACK AS YOU READ

You do not need to write down everything. Three simple threads are worth following through the whole poem:

▶
Who Odysseus meets. Each stop on his voyage introduces a new host, monster, or temptation. Keep a running list.
▶
What each host or place demands of him. Every stop tests something different: patience, self-control, cunning, or trust. Note what the place asks of him and whether he passes the test.
▶
Moments that speak to the module’s question. What does a community owe its king, and what does a king owe his community? Flag any scene that seems to answer this, even partway.

📝 WHERE YOUR NOTES LIVE
Keep everything above in your Module 1 Organizer, the Word document you saved to your OneDrive at the start of this module. You do not turn it in every week; it is checked at points across the module, and it becomes the resource you draw on when it is time to write The Bard’s Hall.
🏛️ A QUICK REMINDER ON NAMES
This course uses the Greek names throughout: Odysseus, Penelope, Telemachus, Zeus, Athena, and Poseidon. If your own copy or an older resource ever uses Ulysses, Minerva, Jove, or Neptune, those are the same characters under their Roman names. Translators choose one tradition or the other; we stay with the Greek.
💪 A LAST WORD

This is one of the oldest stories in the world for a reason: it rewards patience. Read a little at a time, track the pattern of hosts and tests, and trust that the pieces will connect. You have nine weeks and a plan. That is more than enough.
