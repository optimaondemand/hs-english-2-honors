📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Giving up a little ground, on purpose, can make an argument harder to knock down, not easier.
Yesterday you learned to state the strongest case against your own verdict. Today you learn the exact language for answering it without sounding defensive: phrases that let you admit something true about the other side, then pivot straight back to your own claim. You will use this language, along with this week’s Word Study words, in Quick Write 12.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use concession language (“while it is true that,” “granted,” “admittedly,” “even if”) to acknowledge a real point on the other side before pivoting back to my claim.
2
I will be able to distinguish a genuine concession-and-pivot from empty hedging or from giving away the whole argument.
3
I will be able to define and correctly use this week’s Word Study words in my own writing.
📚 PART 1 · GIVING GROUND WITHOUT LOSING THE ARGUMENT

Concession language signals to the reader that you are about to admit something true or reasonable about the opposing side, before you pivot back to your own claim. Phrases like while it is true that, granted, admittedly, and even if all do this same job: they name the real, specific thing the other side gets right, and then a pivot word, often still, however, or but, turns the sentence back toward your claim. A concession does not weaken an argument. It shows a reader you have already accounted for the strongest objection, which makes everything else you say more trustworthy, not less.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CONCEDE, THEN PIVOT
While it is true that Oedipus could not have known the stranger at the crossroads was his father, his own choice to strike first still calls for an answer.
Concession names a real fact (he did not know); the pivot word “still” turns back to the claim (the act itself needs an answer).
Granted, the oracle set the terms of Oedipus’s fate long before his birth; his refusal to stop searching once Jocasta begged him to, however, was entirely his own.
“Granted” concedes a genuine point about fate; “however” pivots to a choice that belongs to Oedipus alone.
Even if Oedipus is guilty of striking the fatal blow, guilt of the act is not the same as guilt of the mind the city seems ready to assume.
“Even if” concedes the strongest possible version of the opposing fact, then redefines the actual standard the writer wants judged.

A useful test: after your concession phrase, ask whether you have actually named something specific and true, not just admitted vague uncertainty. Then check that a pivot word carries the sentence back to a specific claim of your own. If a sentence concedes everything and never pivots back, it has stopped being an argument.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in this week’s reading, the Corinthian messenger scene. Learn the sense the play gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

tidings (noun) — news, especially of an important event
Jocasta orders a servant to “bear these tidings” to Oedipus the moment she believes Polybus’s death has disproven the prophecy.
foundling (noun) — an abandoned child of unknown parentage, found and raised by others
Oedipus asks the messenger directly whether he was “a foundling or a purchased slave,” not yet realizing how close to the truth his own question lands.
herdsman (noun) — a person who tends a herd of animals, such as sheep or goats
The messenger reveals he received the infant Oedipus from another man, a herdsman “of Laius’ house,” the single witness who can now confirm the whole story.
guerdon (noun) — a reward or recompense given for a service
Oedipus promises the messenger “due guerdon for thy pains,” a reward the messenger has been hoping for since he first set out from Corinth.
bondwoman (noun) — a woman held in slavery or servitude
Oedipus tries to reassure Jocasta that even if he is “proved the son of a bondwoman,” her own honor remains untouched, entirely misreading what she actually fears.
Fortune (noun, here personified) — chance or luck, treated as a power that gives or withholds favor
After Jocasta’s exit, Oedipus declares himself “Fortune’s favorite child,” choosing to see his mysterious birth as a gift from chance rather than a warning.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A good concession says: this much is true, and I am not afraid to say so. The pivot word that follows says: and here is why my claim still stands. Carry both, along with two Word Study words, into Quick Write 12.
