🎯 THE QUESTION THIS WEEK SHARPENS
When the truth finally arrives, who judges the king, and who does he judge first?
Every clue you have gathered since Week 1 (the plague, Tiresias’s accusation, the Corinth story, the crossroads) converges this week. An unwilling witness is dragged forward and forced to speak. The truth lands all at once, and the play answers the question of judgment in a way no court in Thebes gets the chance to rule on: Oedipus sentences himself before anyone else can. That fact alone will matter enormously for the verdict you argue in your brief.
📖 THIS WEEK’S READING — PUBLIC DOMAIN, LINKED FREELY
Scene landmarks: the herdsman’s testimony, the revelation, the messenger’s report, and the closing scene through the final chorus
This is the whole rest of the play. Watch for: the herdsman brought in against his will (the last living witness to what happened to Laius’s infant son), Oedipus’s cry when every clue finally locks into place, the messenger’s report of what happens inside the palace (Jocasta and Oedipus, reported, not shown), Oedipus’s own account of why he did what he did, Creon’s measured response, and the final chorus that closes the play. Read or reread it all at the link below. We refer to these moments by name, never by line number.

📖 A NOTE ON THIS WEEK’S ENDING
Greek tragedy never stages its worst violence directly. Jocasta’s death and Oedipus’s self-blinding both happen offstage and reach the audience only through a messenger’s report, exactly as Sophocles wrote it. This week’s lessons follow that same restraint: what happened matters, and it is treated with the weight it deserves, but the point of these scenes is never shock. It is what the catastrophe means for a trial you are about to help decide.
⚖️ YOUR MODULE PROJECT — THE TRIAL OF OEDIPUS
Every scene you read this week is evidence a real advocate would have to reckon with, including evidence that might not go the way you expect. This is also the week you add a required piece to your brief: one researched scholarly source, found and evaluated on JStor, bearing on the central question your verdict has to answer: does an act pollute a city regardless of intent, or does guilt depend on what Oedipus meant to do? Lesson 5.03 walks you through the research itself.
