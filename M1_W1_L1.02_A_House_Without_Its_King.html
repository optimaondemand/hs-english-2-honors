📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Odysseus has been gone twenty years. In his absence, an entire community has forgotten how to behave.
Yesterday you learned the tools the poem uses to tell its story. Today you look at what the story actually shows you in Books 1 and 2: a household overrun by men who will not leave, a son too young and too alone to stop them, and a goddess who decides it is time to act. The poem never stops to tell you outright that the suitors are bad guests. It shows you what they do, and lets you draw the conclusion. That is the central skill of this lesson, and it is one you will use on every character you meet this year.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain xenia, the ancient rule of hospitality between guest and host, and why it matters so much to this poem.
2
I will be able to show how a character is revealed by what they do, not by what the poem says about them.
3
I will be able to summarize what happens in Book 2, when Telemachus finally tries to act.

📚 PART 1 · THE RULE EVERYONE KNEW
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time someone was a guest in your home, or you were a guest in someone else’s. What is one unwritten rule about how a guest should behave? (No wrong answer.)

Xenia: the world’s central rule

In the world of the Odyssey, hospitality between guest and host was close to sacred. A traveler could arrive at a stranger’s door with nothing, and custom demanded that the host feed him, shelter him, and send him off with gifts, before ever asking his name. In exchange, a guest owed his host respect: he did not overstay his welcome, damage the household, or take what was not offered. This exchange had a name, xenia, and it mattered so much because travel was dangerous and strangers had no other protection. A god might even be testing you, disguised as the stranger at your door. Xenia was not simply good manners. It was the thread that held a scattered, dangerous world together.

Keep that rule in mind as you picture the scene inside Odysseus’s hall. A crowd of local noblemen has moved in and refuses to leave, hoping to force his wife, Penelope, into remarrying one of them. They are, technically, guests. They are treating the rule of xenia as a joke.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
An audience listening to this poem performed aloud, likely in the 700s BCE, would have felt the suitors’ behavior as a genuine outrage, not a minor rudeness. Xenia protected a world without hotels, police, or reliable roads. A community that let this many strangers overrun one household without consequence was a community that had lost its center. The poem asks its listeners to hold that thought until the king returns.
📚 PART 2 · SHOWN, NOT TOLD

Notice something about how the poem introduces the suitors. It never simply announces, “these men are greedy and disrespectful.” It shows you a picture and trusts you to judge it:

“There she found the lordly suitors seated on hides of the oxen which they had killed and eaten, and playing draughts in front of the house.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
📝 READ THE ACTIONS, NOT A LABEL
“There she found the lordly suitors seated on hides of the oxen which they had killed and eaten, and playing draughts in front of the house.”
ACTION 1 — WHAT IT REVEALS
They have slaughtered and eaten a host’s cattle for their own comfort. A grateful guest does not consume the household that shelters him. This single detail tells you these men take without limit.
ACTION 2 — WHAT IT REVEALS
They pass the day playing a board game, in no hurry to leave. No urgency, no shame, no plan to go home. That is a household under occupation, not a house of guests.

This is the method to carry with you all year: when you want to know what the poem thinks of a character, do not look for a label. Look for what that character does when no one is forcing a choice on them. The suitors reveal themselves by helping themselves. Telemachus, by contrast, has done nothing at all, and that inaction is its own kind of revelation: a young man who does not yet believe he is allowed to act in his own house.

📚 PART 3 · TELEMACHUS TRIES TO ACT (BOOK 2)

Athena’s visit at the end of Book 1 has an immediate effect. In Book 2, Telemachus does something he has not done before: he calls a formal assembly of the men of Ithaca and stands up in public to accuse the suitors of ruining his household. It is a real act of courage for a young man who has spent his whole life in his absent father’s shadow.

It does not go the way he hopes. Two of the suitors’ ringleaders, Antinous and Eurymachus, answer him in public, and their answer tells you everything about how little they respect him:

“Telemachus, insolent braggart that you are, how dare you try to throw the blame upon us suitors? It is your mother’s fault, not ours, for she is a very artful woman.”
Antinous, trans. Samuel Butler, public domain

Notice the word Antinous reaches for: insolent. He calls Telemachus disrespectful for demanding that guests behave like guests. That is worth sitting with. The suitors, who are the ones actually behaving with no respect at all, accuse the household’s own son of insolence. Watch for this pattern all year: characters who lack a virtue are often the quickest to accuse someone else of lacking it.

Antinous also reveals why the suitors have been able to linger this long. Penelope, Telemachus’s mother, has been holding them off for years with a trick: she told them she would choose a husband once she finished weaving a burial shroud for Odysseus’s aging father, Laertes, then quietly unpicked her work every night so it was never done. The suitors have just discovered the trick and are furious about it. It is a small, patient act of resistance, and it is the clearest sign in these two books that someone in that house still believes the king is coming home.

Book 2 ends with Telemachus taking real action of his own. Athena, again disguised, this time as an old family friend named Mentor, helps him quietly gather a crew and a ship. Under cover of night, he sails off to seek news of his father, without telling his mother he is going. It is the first independent decision he makes in the whole poem.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND CHARACTER JUDGMENT
An AI tool can quickly summarize what happens in Book 2 and even define xenia accurately. What it cannot do is notice, on its own, that Antinous calling Telemachus “insolent” is itself a piece of characterization, or weigh whether Penelope’s trick counts as cleverness or as deception. That judgment comes only from reading the scene closely yourself, and it is yours to make.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH READING HOLDS UP?
Each question below offers four readings of a moment from Books 1–2. More than one will sound reasonable; choose the one the text best supports, and be ready to say why the others fall short.
1. The poem introduces the suitors seated on the hides of oxen “which they had killed and eaten,” playing a game in the doorway. What is the poet doing in this moment?
2. Confronted in the assembly, Antinous answers that the fault is not the suitors’ but Penelope’s, “for she is a very artful woman.” What is the strongest reading of this reply?
3. Penelope’s shroud trick is also a deception: for years she has lied to the suitors about her intentions. How does the poem weigh her deceit against theirs?
4. By the end of Book 2, Telemachus has spoken in the assembly and sailed secretly for news of his father. What is the best-supported reading of where he now stands?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Xenia held this world together, and the suitors have broken it in plain sight: eating another man’s cattle, refusing to leave, and mocking the one person who dares to object. Telemachus finally speaks up in Book 2 and is mocked for it, but he does not stop there: he acts, quietly and on his own, by the book’s end. A household in disorder, a son learning to act, and a wife holding the line by patience alone. That is the state of the king’s community while he is still an ocean away.
