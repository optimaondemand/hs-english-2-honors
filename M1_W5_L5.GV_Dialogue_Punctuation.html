📚 PART 1 · PUNCTUATING DIALOGUE

Dialogue has its own small set of rules, and they exist for one reason: to keep a reader from ever losing track of who is speaking. Three rules matter most. First, a comma (not a period) usually separates a dialogue tag like he said from the quotation itself, unless the quotation ends in a question mark or exclamation point. Second, when a tag interrupts a single sentence of dialogue, both halves get their own comma, and the second half starts with a lowercase letter, since it is still one sentence. Third, every time the speaker changes, the new speaker’s words start a new line or paragraph, even if the exchange is short.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PUNCTUATING A SCENE FROM BOOK 9
Odysseus said, “Cyclops, it was Odysseus who blinded you.”
Tag first: a comma follows said, and the quotation begins with a capital letter inside the quotation marks. The end punctuation stays inside the closing quotation mark.
“It was Odysseus,” the hero shouted, “who blinded you and escaped your cave.”
Tag in the middle: this is one sentence of dialogue split by the tag. A comma closes the first half inside the quotation mark, a comma follows shouted, and the second half starts lowercase because the sentence is still continuing.
“Nobody is killing me!” Polyphemus cried.
“If nobody is hurting you,” his neighbors answered, “then you must be sick.”
New speaker, new line: even in a fast back-and-forth, each new speaker gets a fresh line so the reader never has to guess who is talking.

One more detail worth knowing: quotation marks always come in pairs, and the closing mark goes after the final punctuation of the quoted sentence, not before it. A quotation that trails off without its closing mark is one of the fastest ways to lose a reader entirely.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words all appear in Books 9 and 10. Learn the sense the poem gives each one, not just a dictionary definition.

subtlety (noun) — skillful cunning, cleverness in achieving a purpose
The very quality Odysseus leads with when he finally names himself to Alcinous, before he mentions anything else about who he is.
valiant (adjective) — brave, showing courage especially in a difficult or dangerous situation
The word Odysseus uses to describe himself in his own boast to Polyphemus, a small clue that he knows exactly how he wants to be remembered.
fraud (noun) — deception; a trick meant to make someone believe something false
The word the other Cyclopes use when they hear Polyphemus shout that “Noman” is attacking him “by fraud,” and shrug the cry off as nonsense.
counsel (noun) — advice, or a plan reached by talking something over
Used to describe the crew’s decision to open Aeolus’s bag: “evil counsels prevailed,” a plan the men talked themselves into.
plight (noun) — a difficult, unfortunate, or dangerous situation
Part of Polyphemus’s curse, praying that Odysseus reach home only “in sore plight,” after losing everything along the way.
renowned (adjective) — widely known and admired, famous
Odysseus calls himself “renowned among mankind” for his cunning, in the very first sentence he uses to introduce himself.
