The Theory Built From This Play
Aristotle’s Poetics on tragedy · Weeks 5–6
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A century after Sophocles, an Athenian philosopher sat down to explain why tragedy works, and reached for this exact play as his proof.
You have spent five weeks inside Oedipus the King building the evidence for your own verdict. Today you step outside the play and read the theory it inspired: Aristotle’s Poetics, the earliest surviving work of Western literary criticism, written roughly a century after Sophocles staged this tragedy at the City Dionysia. Aristotle is not simply admiring the play. He is doing something closer to what you are already learning to do at the Designer’s Table: taking a finished structure apart to ask what produces what effect, and why. Your job today is not to accept his theory as settled fact, but to test it, the way any careful reader tests a claim against the evidence.

I will be able to state Aristotle’s definition of tragedy and explain what he means by pity, fear, and the purgation of those emotions.
2
I will be able to identify reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis) in Oedipus the King, using Aristotle’s own account of the play as his example.
3
I will be able to weigh whether Aristotle’s idea of the tragic error (hamartia) names a flaw in Oedipus’s character or a mistake in his past action, and connect that judgment to the verdict I am building for the Trial.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT ARISTOTLE SAYS TRAGEDY DOES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a story, film, or play that left you genuinely shaken, not just sad. What do you think it was doing to you as you watched or read it? (No wrong answer.)

A definition built to be tested

Aristotle wrote the Poetics in Athens in the fourth century B.C., roughly a hundred years after Sophocles staged Oedipus the King for the same city. By his time, Athenian tragedy already had a century of practice behind it, and Aristotle set out to explain, as a matter of craft, what a tragedy actually is and why it affects an audience the way it does. Here is his definition, in full:

“Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude; in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament, the several kinds being found in separate parts of the play; in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain

Unpack that sentence piece by piece. Tragedy imitates an action, meaning something that happens, performed rather than narrated. That action arouses pity, a feeling for a person suffering something they do not deserve, and fear, a feeling that the same thing could happen to anyone, including you. The effect Aristotle names for these two emotions is purgation (sometimes translated catharsis): the play does not simply make you feel pity and fear and leave them sitting there. It works those emotions through something, and releases them, so an audience leaves changed rather than merely upset.

🏛️ HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Athenians experienced tragedy communally, at a civic religious festival, in an outdoor theater holding thousands of citizens at once. Aristotle is not describing private reading. He is describing what happens when a whole city watches the same catastrophe unfold together, in daylight, as an official act of public life.
📚 PART 2 · THE TURN AND THE DISCOVERY

Aristotle names two structural moves he considers essential to the best tragedies, and for both of them, he reaches for the same example: this play. The first is reversal (peripeteia), a moment where the action turns and produces the opposite of what was intended:

“Reversal of the Situation is a change by which the action veers round to its opposite, subject always to our rule of probability or necessity.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain

Aristotle names the exact scene he means:

“Thus in the Oedipus, the messenger comes to cheer Oedipus and free him from his alarms about his mother, but by revealing who he is, he produces the opposite effect.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain

The second move is recognition (anagnorisis), a shift from not knowing to knowing:

“Recognition, as the name indicates, is a change from ignorance to knowledge, producing love or hate between the persons destined by the poet for good or bad fortune.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain

Aristotle considers this play the best possible model precisely because both moves happen at once, in the same scene:

“The best form of recognition is coincident with a Reversal of the Situation, as in the Oedipus.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain
🔍 ONE SCENE, TWO MOVES AT ONCE
REVERSAL (PERIPETEIA)
The Corinthian messenger arrives meaning to bring relief: Polybus, the man Oedipus believed was his father, is dead of natural causes, so the old prophecy cannot touch him through that line. The news is meant to cheer him. It does the opposite.
RECOGNITION (ANAGNORISIS)
In trying to reassure him, the messenger reveals that Oedipus was never Polybus’s son by blood at all, a foundling handed off years before. That single fact is what sets up the herdsman’s forced confession and the full recognition still to come.
WHY ARISTOTLE CALLS THIS THE STRONGEST FORM
A play could reveal the truth in one scene and let the situation turn in another. This play does both in a single conversation, so the audience learns the truth at the exact same instant the hope of escaping it collapses. That doubling is what Aristotle is pointing to when he calls this the model case.

Compare Aristotle’s account to your own reading of the herdsman scene from Week 5, where every earlier clue converges and Oedipus cries “all brought to pass, all true.” Notice that Aristotle is describing the same structural hinge you already identified on your own, weeks before you ever opened his book. That is worth sitting with for a moment: are you confirming his theory, or is his theory simply naming something Sophocles built and any careful reader would eventually notice regardless?

📚 PART 3 · THE TRAGIC ERROR, AND YOUR VERDICT

Aristotle also has a theory of what makes a protagonist tragic rather than merely unfortunate. He rejects two extremes: a purely good person brought low (which he calls simply shocking, not tragic) and a purely wicked person brought low (which he says arouses no pity at all). Between them he places the figure he thinks makes tragedy actually work:

“There remains, then, the character between these two extremes,—that of a man who is not eminently good and just,—yet whose misfortune is brought about not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain

The Greek word behind “error or frailty” is hamartia, and Aristotle names his model case directly:

“He must be one who is highly renowned and prosperous,—a personage like Oedipus, Thyestes, or other illustrious men of such families.”
Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, public domain

Notice that Aristotle never actually specifies, in the passage above, whether hamartia names a flaw in Oedipus’s character, something like a temper or an overconfidence in his own reasoning, or a mistake in a specific past action, the literal act of striking a stranger at a crossroads without knowing who he was. Scholars have argued over this distinction for centuries, and the play itself gives you evidence for both readings: Oedipus’s rage at Tiresias and at the herdsman looks like a trait; the killing at the crossroads looks like a single, isolated act, committed in ignorance of who his victim was.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND CONTESTED TERMS
An AI tool can define hamartia accurately and even summarize the scholarly debate over whether it means a character flaw or a mistaken act. What it cannot do for you is decide which reading your own verdict in the Trial of Oedipus actually depends on. That judgment has to come from your own argument, not from a definition, however accurate.

This is not an idle question for your brief. If hamartia names a flaw in Oedipus’s character, that supports a verdict built on intent: a man whose temper and pride made him dangerous, whatever the law says about the specific killing. If hamartia names a single mistaken act, committed in total ignorance, that supports a verdict built on innocence of intent, whatever the pollution his presence still brings to the city. Whichever standard of judgment you have been building toward, your reading of hamartia is already quietly inside it.

🎮 FOR THE DESIGNER’S TABLE
Notice what Aristotle is actually doing across this whole chapter. He is not just admiring the play; he is taking apart a finished structure to ask what specific choice produces what specific effect on an audience, and naming the moving parts so another maker could use them deliberately. That is the same move you are learning to make in your own design rationale: naming which of your mechanics produces which effect on a player, and arguing for it with evidence. Aristotle is doing design analysis on a play the way you are learning to do it on a game.
⚡ TRY IT · TESTING ARISTOTLE AGAINST THE PLAY
Each question below asks you to weigh Aristotle’s theory against the actual play, or against your own developing verdict. More than one option will sound reasonable; choose the one the evidence best supports.
1. Does the evidence suggest Sophocles built this play to fit Aristotle’s later theory, or that Aristotle built his theory by studying plays like this one?
2. Aristotle says the messenger’s news to Oedipus produces “the opposite effect” of what was intended. What does his own account identify as the actual cause of that reversal?
3. Which reading of hamartia does Aristotle’s own passage actually settle for you: a flaw in Oedipus’s character, or a mistake in a specific action?
4. If your Trial verdict rests on the standard of intent (did Oedipus mean to do it), which reading of hamartia does that verdict need to be doing the real work?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Aristotle read this exact play closely enough to build a theory of tragedy out of it, and he reached for it as his proof text again and again: for reversal, for recognition, for the tragic error. That does not make his theory automatically correct. It makes it a claim, built from evidence, exactly like the verdict you are building. Test it the way you would test any argument: does the play actually support the claim, or did the claim get remembered because it fit this one famous case especially well?
