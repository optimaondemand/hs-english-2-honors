📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A simple comparison says a moment is like something. An epic simile stops and shows you the whole something.
In Lesson 4.02, Odysseus wept as Demodocus sang. Homer describes that weeping using one of the epic’s signature tools: a comparison stretched out well beyond its starting point, until an entire second scene has unfolded inside a single sentence. Today you study how that tool works, and then you try building one of your own before you go any further. This is direct preparation for Quick Write 4.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the parts of an epic simile: the trigger, the extended comparison, and the return.
2
I will be able to draft an epic simile of my own that stretches a comparison well past its first line.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
A notebook or notes document to draft in.
▶
Ten quiet minutes, since you will draft two short passages of your own during this lesson.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT AN EPIC SIMILE DOES

An ordinary comparison is quick: he wept like a child. An epic simile takes that same starting idea and refuses to let it stay quick. It opens with a trigger (as or like), then lingers in the comparison itself, adding detail after detail as if the writer has wandered off into a small second story, before finally returning to the person or moment the comparison was about in the first place. Here is Homer’s description of Odysseus weeping at Demodocus’s song:

“He wept as a woman weeps when she throws herself on the body of her husband who has fallen before his own city and people, fighting bravely in defence of his home and children.”
trans. Samuel Butler, public domain
📝 TAKING THE COMPARISON APART
He wept as a woman weeps when she throws herself on the body of her husband who has fallen before his own city and people, fighting bravely in defence of his home and children.
THE TRIGGER
“As” announces the comparison is coming, and names its subject right away: weeping.
THE EXTENDED COMPARISON
Instead of stopping at “weeps,” Homer builds an entire small scene: a specific woman, a specific husband, a specific fight, a specific loss. None of this is literally true of Odysseus. It is true of the feeling.
Why it works: the comparison does not just say the sadness was big. It hands you a second, fully imagined moment of loss and lets the scale of that moment stand in for what words alone could not measure directly.

Notice what the poem does not do here. It never simply writes “Odysseus wept a great deal.” That version tells you the size of the feeling. The version above makes you feel the size, by handing you a whole scene to measure it against.

📚 PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Think of a strong feeling from your own Bard’s Hall tale: fear, relief, pride, grief, longing. Pick a moment where a character feels that emotion sharply. You are going to compare it to something else entirely, and then keep going past the first line.

✍️ In your notebook, write two to three sentences using “as” or “like” to compare your character’s feeling to a small, fully imagined scene, not just a quick label. Do this now, before you open this box to see one more worked example.
ONE MORE EXAMPLE, FOR COMPARISON

Relief moved through the room the way a held breath finally leaves a diver breaking the surface, all at once, with a sound nobody meant to make, and for a moment nothing else in the world seemed to matter but the air itself.

Compare it to your own draft. Did you stop at the first image, or did you let it unfold a little further? Is there a specific detail, a sound, a person, a place, that makes your comparison feel like its own small scene rather than a quick label?

⚡ TRY IT · EPIC SIMILE OR PLAIN DESCRIPTION?
For each line, decide whether it is an epic simile (a comparison that keeps building a genuine second scene) or a plain description. Watch for a trigger word like “as” or “like” that isn’t doing the real work of the test.
1. “He felt the weight lift, the way spring finally arrives after a winter that seemed determined never to end, one gray morning giving way, almost without announcement, to birdsong and a softer wind.”
2. “He felt calm settle over him, the way relief always feels after a long fear ends.”
3. “He collapsed, the way a tide finally goes out, pulling back low and slow across the sand until the whole shore lies exposed and still.”

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A long comparison earns its length: it does not just label a feeling, it builds a whole second scene to measure that feeling against. Carry whichever draft felt stronger into Quick Write 4, and keep the habit of pushing a comparison one sentence past where it would be easiest to stop.
